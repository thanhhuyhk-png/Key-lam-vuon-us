## TTS SCRIPT

Every winter, another pot on the porch goes brown and empty. Come spring, you're back at the store, buying the same plant — again. Based on typical garden-center pricing, a plant plus a decent pot often lands somewhere around thirty to forty dollars. What if this September, you only paid that once?

Here's the good news. These six plants go in a pot once, and they come back year after year. No new plant, no new trip to the store. Every one of them lives in a pot, on a porch or a table. That means no kneeling into a garden bed to plant or tend a single one. That's a relief if your knees complain every time you kneel in a garden bed. We pulled this list from university extension guides, not personal trial and error. Coreopsis, heuchera, hosta, lavender, dwarf ornamental grass, and sedum.

And the last one on this list? You could almost forget it's even sitting in a pot.

Let's start with the one that might surprise you the most — it's not some rare find. Coreopsis. It's hardy from zone three all the way to zone nine. So most of the country can grow it right through winter, even sitting in a pot on a porch. Like every plant on this list, give it about six weeks before your first hard frost. Extension offices agree on that window across the board. It applies no matter which of these six you end up planting. Coreopsis blooms most of the summer in cheerful yellow, and once it's settled into its pot, it barely asks for anything else. Good drainage, and that's most of the work — done and dusted.

Coreopsis solves the "easy" problem. Our next one solves a different problem entirely — color, without waiting on a single flower. Heuchera, also called coral bells. The leaves come in purple, lime, even near-black, and they hold that color most of the year. It's hardy zone three to eight. Here's a nice bonus: the ASPCA lists it as non-toxic to cats, dogs, and horses. One less thing to worry about. Many gardeners trace coral bells' popularity back to the early nineties, when a purple-leafed type first turned heads for its foliage instead of its flowers.

Heuchera handles color in the shade. This next one is the plant that's probably already living in somebody's memory. Hosta. If you've got a shady corner on that porch, this is the one your mother or grandmother probably grew. It's long been a favorite in American shade gardens. It's hardy zone three to nine, same tough range as coreopsis. One quick note: the ASPCA lists hosta as mildly toxic to cats, dogs, and horses. It can cause an upset stomach, nothing life-threatening. Just keep new sprouts out of reach if you've got a chewer at home.

Three plants down, three to go. But before we get to the rest of the list, I need to stop for a second. There's one job "plant once" doesn't erase.

Here's the truth nobody puts in the title: a pot doesn't insulate roots the way real ground does. In the dirt, the surrounding soil buffers the cold. In a pot, the roots sit exposed on every side — and that's the part that catches people off guard. So extension offices have a rule of thumb. Pick plants hardy to about two zones colder than where you live, if that pot is staying outside all winter. Here's something reassuring, though: three separate university extension offices — Iowa State, the University of New Hampshire, and Illinois — all landed on the same three ways to winterize a potted perennial. So this isn't guesswork. One: sink the whole pot into the ground until it sits level with the soil around it. That way, the earth does the insulating for you. Two: move it into an unheated garage or shed, somewhere that holds between twenty and forty-five degrees. Three, if neither of those work: group your pots together out of the wind. Bury them in a thick layer of straw or leaves. Pick whichever fits your space — all three work. For the soil itself, Illinois Extension recommends one part garden soil, one part peat moss, one part perlite. For sedum and lavender specifically, lean even more toward perlite. Soggy roots kill those two faster than any cold snap does. As for pot size, lavender actually has a real number behind it. Extension guides call for at least a four to six gallon pot, eight to twelve inches deep. Good news for the rest: coreopsis and heuchera aren't picky. Most growers get by with something in the one to three gallon range.

Now, back to the list — because two of the last three plants are exactly why that two-zone rule matters. Speaking of lavender, I want to be straight with you here, not sell you on it. English lavender is only reliably hardy from zone five and up. If you're zone five or warmer, plant it now, give it that deep pot we just talked about. It'll reward you for years. Colder than that? Don't panic — just treat this one as a bonus, not a must. You'll likely be fighting the cold most winters otherwise.

Number five is dwarf ornamental grass — the Little Bunny or Hameln varieties. It adds height and movement the flowers can't. It's the one that makes a pot feel alive, not just planted. Extension sources put it somewhere around zone five to nine. So like lavender, this one's better suited to warmer areas in a pot. The upside: you only need to divide it once every five to ten years — not every spring. No toxicity has been flagged for this one — the main risk to watch for is the seed heads, which can poke if a pet noses in too close. So just keep an eye on curious noses.

One bonus plant down. Now, the one I've been promising you since the start. And that brings us to the last one on this list. Sedum. It's hardy all the way from zone three to zone ten, tougher than every other plant on this list. Give it well-drained soil, and it stores its own water right in those thick leaves. Winter, summer, drought — sedum tends to keep showing up when other plants struggle. You could genuinely forget it's living in a pot at all.

So that's all six: coreopsis, heuchera, hosta, lavender for the warmer zones, dwarf grass for the warmer zones too. And sedum. Plant them once this September, tuck them in for winter the way we just talked about. By next spring, you're not buying a replacement. You're just standing on that same porch, looking at pots already full again. No single trip back to the garden center. No kneeling to plant them, no kneeling to tend them, and no bare pot staring back at you in April.
