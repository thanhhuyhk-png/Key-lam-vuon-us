## TTS SCRIPT

A bare stick. No leaves, no roots, nothing green. Cut it from a grape vine in February, push it into the ground, and it can become a whole new vine.

All it needs is three buds. No rooting hormone, no special equipment, nothing to buy.

That's how you get six fruit plants for free, as long as you already have the parent plant, or a neighbor who prunes. Grapes. Currants. Gooseberries. Elderberries. Figs. Pomegranates.

But there's one fruit tree gardeners love to try this on, and it rarely works. Which one?

Let's start with the one rule all six share. Cut while the plant is asleep. That means winter, after the leaves have dropped.

Picture a bare vine in a January yard. It looks dead. It's just asleep. And all six follow the same pattern. Dormant wood from last season, cut in winter, kept cool.

So take a piece of last year's growth, about as thick as a pencil. Make a straight cut just below a bud. Then slant the top cut, so you always know which end is up. To take the cutting, all you need is a pair of pruning shears.

Now keep it cold, but never frozen. A fridge works, somewhere between thirty-three and forty degrees. Use a bag that doesn't seal tight, and don't store it with fruit.

So what does free really mean here? It means you already own the parent plant. Or a neighbor does, and says yes. A nursery plant costs money. A cutting from your own bush costs nothing.

A few of these plants, or their fruit, aren't pet-safe, so we'll flag each one. And one small tag can make a cutting off-limits, even from your own yard.

Here's your first step. Walk your yard this week and tie a ribbon on every fruit plant you already own. That's your parent plant list. It's a quick walk. And while you're there, read each tag.

Let's start with the vine.

Plant one: grapes. Grapes root very easily from one-year-old dormant wood.

Timing matters. Take cuttings late in the dormant season, right before you prune in February.

Pick canes at least as thick as a pencil. Aim for three buds. A longer cutting can carry up to six. And you don't need rooting hormone.

Once the soil can be worked, set each one in the ground with just the top bud above the soil. Space them about six inches apart. It's generally best to give them a full year in a nursery bed first.

One note if you have a dog. The ASPCA says grapes and raisins can cause kidney damage in dogs. So keep the fruit out of reach. If your dog eats some, call your vet right away.

And that fruit tree from the start? It's still coming.

Next is another forgiving plant. And its cousin isn't quite as easy.

Plant two is just as easy. Currants. Red, black or white. Currants are among the easiest fruit crops to root from stem cuttings.

Use one-year-old wood, about six to eight inches long. Take it in late autumn or early winter. If you wait, store the cuttings cold, and don't let them freeze or dry out. Then plant in early spring, leaving two or three buds above the ground.

And currants are tough. They can handle winters down to about minus twenty-two degrees.

Plant three: gooseberries. Same idea, but slower. They do root from cuttings, but it can take longer. Sticking them straight into the ground outdoors works less often than with currants.

One option is to store the cuttings in damp sand or peat in the fridge until early spring. And an older reference warns that European gooseberries are the stubborn ones. Treat that as a warning, not a rule. If you only have room for one, start with currants. They're the more forgiving of the two.

Before you plant either one, check your state. Currants and gooseberries can carry a pine disease called white pine blister rust. Black currants are the most prone to it. Red and white currants are less so, and gooseberries least of all. A federal ban on growing them ended in nineteen sixty-six. At least one state bans them statewide, and another needs a permit. Rules vary. A quick call to your state ag office settles it.

Now notice something. The cutting dates aren't the same for every plant. Grapes are cut in February. Currants, anytime from late autumn to late winter. Elderberries, February or March.

The sources don't agree on one date, because two dates keep getting mixed up. When you cut. And when you plant. So here's the rule. Cut it asleep. Keep it cool until you're ready. Then pot it up, or plant it when the soil softens.

Hold onto that fruit tree question. It's coming.

But one plant on this list will leaf out before it has a single root. And that can fool you.

Plant four: elderberries. Take cuttings from last year's growth, two to four buds long. February and March work well. Pot them in clean potting mix, and keep them moist and warm. Another option is early spring, with pieces ten to twelve inches long.

Here's the part that fools people. The buds open first. Leaves come out. It looks like it's working. But the roots aren't there yet. Good rooting takes about four to six weeks.

So don't pull it up to check, and don't give up on it. Give it time.

Rooting hormone is optional here, and too much can hurt.

A safety note, because elderberries need care. Cook ripe berries before eating. Skip the unripe ones, the leaves and the stems.

The last two plants need a milder winter than a lot of gardeners get.

And this next part is for you if your winters are hard. Stay with me, because there's a workaround.

Plant five: figs. Figs are one of the easiest fruit crops to propagate. Take eight to ten inches of dormant wood in winter. 

Plant six: pomegranates. They root readily from winter cuttings. Cut pieces about as thick as a pencil. Unlike grapes, they usually get a dip in rooting powder.

Both are tender, so cold is the catch. Where winters are mild, pot them up right away. Where winters are hard, keep the cuttings in the crisper drawer, in a bag with damp paper, until early spring. Never let them freeze. Then pot them up.

Once the pomegranates start growing, give them four to six weeks in gallon pots. Where winters are mild, they can go in the ground after that. Picture a small pot on a bright windowsill in March, with a fig shoot just starting.

Hardiness is the limit. One fig variety, Celeste, is rated for zones six B to ten. Below about twenty degrees, a fig can die back to the ground. Pomegranates want zones eight to ten, and in zone seven B a hard winter can cut them back. If you live where winters are hard, treat both as pot plants, and keep the pots where they won't freeze.

One more thing for figs. The milky sap can irritate skin in sunlight, so wear gloves and sleeves. According to one university plant database, fig is also toxic to cats, dogs and horses. So keep pets away from the leaves and cuttings.

That's six. But now for the fruit tree a lot of gardeners try first.

It's the apple. Apples are the one people ask about. And apples don't root well. One extension expert said they'd seen a cutting root just once.

It's not only apples. Cuttings from most fruit trees are hard to root. That's why growers graft them instead. Pears are usually grafted, too.

Grafting means joining a branch of the tree you love onto a compatible root. It's a different skill, and it's for another day.

Blueberries are the in-between case. People do root them from winter cuttings. But some varieties are hard to root. And the cuttings need a mister or sprinkler until they do. That's a setup, not a stick in the ground.

So here's the pattern. The plants on our list root from a bare stick. The ones we just covered need extra help, or a different method altogether.

Even with all that, one thing could stop you from cutting a plant you love.

Some plants are protected by a plant patent. It lasts twenty years from the filing date, and it can't be renewed. While it lasts, copying the plant from cuttings needs the patent holder's permission. That includes giving or trading it.

Lawsuits against home gardeners are rare. But it's still not allowed.

Plants grown from seed usually aren't covered by a plant patent.

Not sure? Ask the nursery or your local extension office. This is information, not legal advice.
