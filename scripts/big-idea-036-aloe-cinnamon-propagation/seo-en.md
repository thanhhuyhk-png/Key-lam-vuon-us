Aloe vera propagation from leaf cuttings — why cinnamon isn't a rooting hormone, and what actually works instead.

In this video, you'll learn:
- Why cinnamon is an antifungal (not a growth hormone) — and why it still helps
- The real reason most aloe leaf cuttings fail (and it's not what you think)
- How drying and callus formation give your cutting a real advantage
- The soil mix that prevents root rot (and why regular potting soil backfires)
- How to know when roots are actually forming — without a microscope

This is a science-backed mythbust of the gardening trick everyone swears by: sprinkling cinnamon on aloe leaf tips. Turns out, cinnamon doesn't trigger root growth at all — research from Purdue University shows only IBA and NAA (the compounds in rooting hormone) actually work. But cinnamon does have real antifungal power. The actual secret? Patience and the right conditions. A dried aloe tip seals itself within minutes of drying, needs 4–7 days to air-dry before touching soil, and works best in a fast-draining mix (like 3 parts potting soil, 2 parts coarse sand, 1 part perlite). Most folks fail because they water too soon (when there are no roots yet to use it) or use regular potting soil, which holds moisture and causes rot from the inside out. Here's what success actually looks like: over 6–8 weeks, you'll see thin white roots at the base of the leaf, feel resistance when you gently tug it, and a tiny plantlet starting to form. Tying it all together — the soil drains, the callus seals the wound, and the leaf uses its own internal auxin to grow roots, not external hormones. No shovel, no kneeling, no rooting hormone needed — just time and the right conditions.
