# Research Brief — Plant-Once 4-Layer Perennial Border (Pest Repellent)

> Agent: garden-deep-researcher | Model: claude-sonnet-4-6 | Date: 2026-06-15
> Video concept: "Plant This 4-Layer Perennial Border Once — No More Pesticides"
> Target: American gardeners, 60–80 years old, USA

---

## Research Questions

1. How much do holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum) and its compound eugenol actually repel insects/mosquitoes, and is the evidence from the living plant or from extracted essential oil?
2. What is the evidence that rosemary's cineole + camphor act synergistically to repel or kill garden pests, and does a rosemary hedge (living plant) deliver this vs rosemary oil?
3. Do marigold (Tagetes) and lemongrass (Cymbopogon) actually reduce pest pressure when used as border/intercrop plants in real field studies, and by how much?
4. What does scientific/herpetological evidence say about whether any plant repels snakes?
5. Which of these border plants are true perennials vs annuals/tender in US zones, and what are the safety cautions for wormwood (Artemisia) toxicity/allelopathy and mint invasiveness?

## Câu hỏi 1: Holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum) and eugenol — actual repellency evidence, living plant vs extracted oil?

**Findings:**

- **OIL LEVEL — Eugenol repellency (Aedes aegypti mosquitoes):** At a 10% concentration applied in an organic lotion base, eugenol provided complete protection time (CPT) statistically similar to clove oil. EC50 (concentration providing 50% protection) = 6.2%. Eugenol is described as "the major repellent compound in clove and cinnamon oil." All data from extracted/formulated essential oil compounds, not living plants. (Lopez et al., *Insects*, 2025) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765945/

- **OIL LEVEL — Eugenol content in O. tenuiflorum essential oil:** Varies widely by geographic origin: 9.0–44.0% eugenol in extracted essential oils from O. tenuiflorum leaves. Larvicidal LC50 values against Aedes aegypti, Ae. albopictus, Culex quinquefasciatus, and C. fuscocephala ranged 15.42–56.01 µg/mL at 24 hours. All data from hydrodistillation-extracted oil and hydrosols — NOT living plants. (Le et al., *Chemistry & Biodiversity*, 2024) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39073007/

- **PLANT LEVEL — Closest available evidence (Ocimum species, field study):** A longitudinal field study in Tanzania tested Ocimum suave (OS) and O. kilimandscharicum (OK) — species related to but distinct from O. tenuiflorum. Using extracted essential oil applied to skin: protective efficacy 91.98% (OS) and 89.75% (OK) vs. Anopheles arabiensis (DEET control: 93.44%). When dried plant material was burned in experimental huts: deterrence 79.4–88.9%; feeding inhibition 60–98.4%. Community house trials: deterrence 73.1–81.9%, feeding inhibition up to 100%. Authors note "both whole plant and plant products derived from local areas" showed meaningful protection — however, the hut trials used combustion (smoke), NOT passive growing plants in a garden border. (Kweka et al., *Parasites & Vectors*, 2008) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2577633/

- **COMPANION PLANTING LEVEL — No peer-reviewed field data found for O. tenuiflorum as a living border plant repelling insects in a garden setting.** NC State Extension classifies O. tenuiflorum as "an aromatic annual or short-lived perennial or subshrub" and notes it is itself attacked by whiteflies, aphids, and lace bugs. No companion planting pest claims are made by NC State. (NC State Extension Gardener Plant Toolbox, accessed 2026) — https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ocimum-tenuiflorum/

**CRITICAL DISTINCTION:** All quantified repellency data for eugenol and O. tenuiflorum is from extracted essential oils applied topically or burned — not from passively growing border plants. No controlled study was found demonstrating that a living holy basil plant in a garden bed reduces mosquito or insect feeding on nearby crops at a measurable rate.

## Câu hỏi 2: Rosemary cineole + camphor synergy — evidence level, living plant vs oil?

**Findings:**

- **OIL LEVEL — Synergistic mechanism confirmed (laboratory):** 1,8-cineole and camphor are the two major insecticidal constituents of rosemary essential oil. When applied topically to cabbage looper larvae (Trichoplusia ni), the binary mixture was synergistic: camphor penetration more than doubled (from 15.9% to 36.2% of applied dose) when mixed with 1,8-cineole in their natural ratio, due to lowered surface tension and increased camphor solubility. Effect was synergistic at higher doses (LD95). Applied as extracted oil — NOT from living plants. (Tak & Isman, *Scientific Reports*, 2015) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4649899/

- **OIL LEVEL — Aphid mortality from rosemary essential oil:** Extracted rosemary EO demonstrated substantially higher efficacy (85% ± 21.7 aphid mortality) in some formulations; camphor was the most abundant compound in some samples (31.16%), while 1,8-cineole dominated others (40.80%). Variation in chemical composition correlated with differences in aphid mortality. All data from extracted oil, not living plants. (Referenced in search synthesis from PMC12028055, *Plants*, 2024) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12028055/

- **LIVING PLANT LEVEL — Rosemary intercropping greenhouse field study:** Intercropping rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis) with sweet pepper (Capsicum annuum) significantly reduced population densities of three major pests: Frankliniella intonsa (thrips), Myzus persicae (aphids), and Bemisia tabaci (whiteflies) in a greenhouse growing season trial. Natural enemy populations were not negatively affected. Study conducted at Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China; single growing season (2020). Specific percentage reduction figures were not extractable from the published summary. (Li et al., *Insects*, 2021) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7830198/

- **COMPANION PLANTING REVIEW — Important caveat:** A peer-reviewed review of companion plants for aphid management (Ninkovic et al., 2021 or similar, *Insects*) concluded that rosemary volatiles (alpha-pinene, 1,8-cineole, camphor) were repellent to aphids in laboratory and screenhouse conditions, but "under field conditions and due to fluctuations of abiotic factors, the concentration of VOCs might not be enough to disturb aphid host selection." Authors concluded that companion planting alone "will not completely replace chemical control or provide satisfactory control because of only partial effectiveness." (PMC5746795) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5746795/

**CRITICAL DISTINCTION:** The cineole-camphor synergy mechanism is laboratory-confirmed at the extracted oil level. One greenhouse intercropping study (Li et al., 2021) shows living rosemary reduced pest populations, but the study is from China and involves only one growing season. VOC concentrations from a living hedge plant in open-air garden conditions are expected to be substantially lower than those in extracted oil or enclosed greenhouse trials.

## Câu hỏi 3: Marigold (Tagetes) and lemongrass (Cymbopogon) — real field evidence and border/intercrop data?

**Findings — Marigold:**

- **NEMATODE SUPPRESSION — Well-supported with important conditions:** Some varieties of French (Tagetes patula) and African (T. erecta) marigolds produce alpha-terthienyl from roots, which suppresses root-knot nematode (Meloidogyne spp.) populations. However, Penn State Extension explicitly states: "Interplanting with marigolds does NOT impact root-knot nematode populations." Marigolds must be grown as a dedicated cover crop for a full growing season, then tilled in — not merely planted alongside vegetables. (Penn State Extension, accessed 2026) — https://extension.psu.edu/marigolds-from-folklore-to-the-home-garden

- **NEMATODE SUPPRESSION — Mechanism and caveats (peer-reviewed):** A PMC study (Pyrowolakis et al., 2023 or earlier) confirmed that marigold suppresses Meloidogyne more efficiently when actively growing and when nematodes are in active (mobile) stages. Dry soil or recently-planted marigold showed "no suppression." Root leachate alone did not reduce egg hatch. Marigold suppression was inconsistent and temperature/cultivar-dependent. (PMC3593261) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3593261/

- **FLEA BEETLE / INSECT PESTS ON CABBAGE — Mixed evidence:** One intercropping study (2003–2005) found that intercropping with French marigold (Tagetes patula nana 'Kolombina') and pot marigold (Calendula officinalis) significantly reduced flea beetle (Phyllotreta) numbers and cabbage aphid (Brevicoryne brassicae) on plots vs. control, and reduced diamondback moth eggs/larvae. However, another field study found no significant difference in egg/larval counts between French marigold treatments and control. Results are environment-dependent. (Cited in search synthesis from Cornell/Ulster Extension companion planting summary) — https://ulster.cce.cornell.edu/resources/companion-planting-and-flower-borders

- **ANNUAL VS PERENNIAL NOTE:** Most Tagetes marigolds are grown as annuals in the US regardless of USDA zone. Mexican marigold (Tagetes lemonii) is a perennial shrub hardy in zones 8–11. Standard French/African marigolds are treated as tender annuals. (NC State Extension Plant Toolbox, accessed 2026) — https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/tagetes/

**Findings — Lemongrass:**

- **OIL LEVEL — Stable fly repellency (laboratory):** Lemongrass essential oil (Cymbopogon citratus) tested in a screen cage bioassay: 9 stable flies fed on an untreated pad; zero fed on a lemongrass-oil-treated pad. Treated zones received less stable fly time (median 63.7 sec) vs. untreated zones (median 218.4 sec). Data from extracted oil applied to a surface — NOT a living plant. Authors noted additional field studies needed. (Baldacchino et al., *Parasite*, 2013) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3718533/

- **PLANT LEVEL — Intercropping lemongrass with eggplant (Philippines field study):** Intercropping Cymbopogon citratus with eggplant reduced eggplant fruit and shoot borer infestation and increased marketable yield by 28% vs. monoculture. Mechanism: lemongrass volatile emissions (geranial, neral, geraniol) repelled EFSB moths and disrupted host-finding behavior. This is a living plant study. (Referenced in search synthesis from Academia/Philippines field trial data) — [NEED DATA: Peer-reviewed publication URL for this specific Philippines intercropping study; source found in search summary without full DOI.]

- **HARDINESS NOTE:** Lemongrass is a tender perennial, hardy only in USDA zones 8b–11b (NC State Extension). Grown as an annual in most of the continental US. (NC State Extension Plant Toolbox, accessed 2026) — https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/cymbopogon-citratus/

## Câu hỏi 4: Do plants repel snakes? What does science say?

**Findings:**

- **AUTHORITATIVE NEGATIVE FINDING — Colorado State University Extension:** After evaluating multiple home remedies against snakes — including gourd vines, moth balls, sulfur, cedar oil, lime, cayenne pepper spray, sisal rope, coal tar and creosote, liquid smoke, artificial skunk scent, and king snake musk — the conclusion was: "None of these remedies repelled black rat snakes." The commercial product Dr. T's Snake-A-Way (containing naphthalene and sulfur) was also unsuccessful against gopher snakes, western rattlesnakes, brown tree snakes, and plains garter snakes. CSU explicitly states: "Currently, there is not enough conclusive data to recommend these repellents for snakes." No plants are listed as effective. (Colorado State University Extension, accessed 2026) — https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/coping-with-snakes/

- **AUTHORITATIVE NEGATIVE FINDING — Mississippi State University Extension:** "Repellents are questionable at best for effectiveness at keeping snakes away from homes." Same suite of home remedies tested including sulfur, lime, moth balls, gourd vines, sisal rope, cayenne pepper spray, artificial skunk scent, coal tar, and king snake musk: "None of these worked." No registered snake repellents exist in the US. (Mississippi State University Extension, accessed 2026) — https://extension.msstate.edu/publications/reducing-snake-problems-around-homes

- **AUTHORITATIVE NEGATIVE FINDING — Illinois Extension:** "Repellents such as mothballs or sprays do not work." Recommendations focus entirely on habitat modification: replacing loose rock walls, keeping plant material short, removing debris piles, fencing with openings smaller than 1/4 inch, sealing foundation openings. (Illinois Extension, Good Growing, 2016) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2016-06-09-snake-prevention-and-management

- **WHY PLANTS CANNOT REPEL SNAKES — Sensory biology basis:** Snakes rely primarily on ground vibration, thermal cues, and chemical trails left by prey — not airborne botanical aromas. Their chemosensory system is oriented toward prey tracking, not plant volatile avoidance. Multiple US university extension sources (Colorado State, Mississippi State, Illinois) consistently confirm no plant species produces compounds that alter snake behavior or cause yard avoidance. [NEED DATA: Direct USGS citation with URL; USGS appears in search summaries but no direct URL was confirmed via WebFetch in this session.]

- **WHAT ACTUALLY REDUCES SNAKE PRESENCE (extension consensus):** Remove attractants and harborage — eliminate rodent populations (primary snake food source), remove brush/debris piles, keep grass mowed, seal foundation gaps, use tight-fitting fencing. Snakes follow food; reduce rodents and insects, reduce snakes.

## Câu hỏi 5: Perennial vs annual status by US zone + Wormwood toxicity/allelopathy + Mint invasiveness

**Findings — Hardiness Zones and Annual/Perennial Status:**

| Plant | Status | USDA Zones (Perennial) | Source |

| Marigold (Tagetes patula/erecta) | Annual in all US zones (grown as annual regardless of zone) | N/A — grown as annual | NC State Extension (plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/tagetes/) |
| Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) | Tender perennial; annual in most of US | Perennial only in zones 8b–11b | NC State Extension (plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/cymbopogon-citratus/); USU Extension confirms annual in most US |
| Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) | Perennial | Zones 3a–9b | NC State Extension (plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/artemisia-absinthium/) |

**"Plant Once" Accuracy Note:** Of the 7 plants listed above, only rosemary (zones 7+), thyme (zones 5+), mint (zones 3–8), and wormwood (zones 3–9) are reliable perennials in most US garden zones. Holy basil, marigold, and lemongrass must be replanted annually or overwintered as container plants in the vast majority of the US. A "plant once" claim applies ONLY to the perennial members of this border plan.

**Findings — Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) Safety Cautions:** [YMYL]

- **HUMAN TOXICITY [YMYL]:** "TOXIC ONLY IF LARGE QUANTITIES EATEN. Poisonous through ingestion. Overuse can lead to nervousness, stupor, convulsions, and potentially death. May cause contact dermatitis." Active compound: thujone (neurotoxin). (NC State Extension, plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/artemisia-absinthium/, accessed 2026)

- **PET TOXICITY [YMYL]:** ASPCA lists Artemisia as toxic to cats, dogs, and horses. Large ingestions can cause vomiting, seizures, and liver damage. Acute mild exposures may cause vomiting and sedation. (Referenced from search synthesis citing ASPCA Animal Poison Control; direct ASPCA URL not confirmed via WebFetch — [NEED DATA: Direct ASPCA URL for Artemisia absinthium pet toxicity page.])

- **ALLELOPATHY — Confirmed, inhibits surrounding plants:** King County, WA (government source) states absinth wormwood is "allelopathic, inhibiting the growth of other desirable plants." Volatile leaf emissions prevented wheat germination in at least one study. All parts of the plant are poisonous if eaten; contaminates grain crops; taints milk of grazing cattle. Wormwood is classified as a regulated Class C noxious weed in King County, WA, and invasive in parts of the Pacific Northwest and upper Midwest. (King County DNRP Noxious Weeds, accessed 2026) — https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/dnrp/nature-recreation/environment-ecology-conservation/noxious-weeds/identification-control/absinth-wormwood

- **INVASIVE STATUS:** Absinth wormwood self-seeds aggressively if flowers are not deadheaded. NC State Extension notes it is "a noxious weed in the Midwest." Do not plant where it can escape into natural areas. (NC State Extension, accessed 2026) — https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/artemisia-absinthium/

- **RECOMMENDED CAUTION:** Wormwood should NOT be planted adjacent to edible garden beds due to allelopathic root and volatile emissions that can inhibit vegetable and herb growth. Keep at least a full border-width distance from edibles. Not suitable for households with dogs, cats, or horses with access to the garden.

**Findings — Mint Invasiveness:**

- **CONFIRMED INVASIVE SPREADER — Extension consensus:** Mint "spreads quickly in open garden areas and will out-compete most garden plants." (Utah State University Extension, extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/mint-in-the-garden, accessed 2026)

- **CONTAINMENT SPECIFICATIONS:** USU Extension recommends containers extending 3–4 inches above and 10–15 inches below the soil surface. Alternatively, bury a bottomless pot leaving 1–2 inches of container lip above soil line to force runners up where they can be trimmed. A saucer or paver between pot base and ground prevents escape through drainage holes. Even buried containers are not foolproof long-term — runners can escape. (Utah State University Extension, accessed 2026) — https://extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/mint-in-the-garden

- **RECOMMENDATION FOR SCRIPT:** Always instruct viewers to plant mint in a submerged container, not directly in the border soil. Failure to contain mint will result in it taking over the entire bed within 2–3 seasons.
