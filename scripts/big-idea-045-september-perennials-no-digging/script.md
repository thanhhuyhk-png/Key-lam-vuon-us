## TTS SCRIPT

Every single spring, you're back at the garden center. Buying the exact same tray of plants you bought last year — again. Ugh, sound familiar? That's real money gone every spring — a tray like that often runs fifteen to twenty dollars — for something that's going to die back by winter anyway. What if this September, you only had to do that once?

Here's the thing. These seven plants, you dig once — this month — and they come back on their own every single year after that. No new hole, no new trip to the store. We've gone through university extension guides, not personal trial and error, to land on this exact list: black-eyed Susan, hosta, coreopsis, daylily, Russian sage, lavender, and sedum.

Number three is rated a full zone hardier than what zone four needs. Extension offices agree: bury the pot, or mound mulch over it — that's your best shot. And Number seven? You can plant that one sitting in a chair, not bent over a shovel. Comfortable AND easy — we like that combo.

Let's start with the one almost everyone already knows — black-eyed Susan. It's one of the most familiar flowers in the country. Most people don't realize it's also one of the toughest. Plant it in September, and it comes back reliably from zone three to zone nine. That's most of the United States! Give it six weeks before your first hard frost, so the roots can settle in before the ground freezes. Extension offices agree on that six-week window, no matter which of these seven you end up planting. Black-eyed Susan also spreads a little wider every year on its own. One clump this September, a bigger patch by next fall — without you lifting a shovel again.

Speaking of that six-week window — here's why the "plant once" idea works so well. It actually goes back further than you'd think. A gardener named Ruth Stout figured this out decades ago. She spent almost fifteen years digging her Connecticut farm the hard way. Picture that — fifteen years of digging, every single year. Then she switched to letting a thick layer of mulch do the work, instead of turning the soil over and over. She wrote about it for magazines starting in the nineteen fifties, and gardeners are still using her approach today. That's the same principle behind every plant on this list: do the work once, let the ground handle the rest.

Next up, one you might already have a personal memory of — hosta. If you've got a shady spot, this is the one your mother or grandmother probably grew. It's been a staple in American shade gardens for generations. Zone three to nine, so it handles cold about as well as black-eyed Susan does. Plant it about an inch deep — same depth it's already sitting at in the nursery pot. One quick note if you've got a cat, a dog, or a horse around: the ASPCA lists hosta as mildly toxic — it can cause an upset stomach, but it's not life-threatening. Keep new sprouts out of reach, and you're fine.

Hosta handles shade. Our next one wants full sun — and it's about to answer the promise I made you at the start.

Number three: coreopsis. This one's rated hardy down to zone four, in the ground. In a container, University of Illinois Extension has a simple rule. Go about one zone colder than what your area needs, before you can trust it through winter. Coreopsis meets that bar. Their advice, if you're growing it in a pot in a cold zone: bury the pot in the ground for winter. Or mound mulch up over the sides. That's not a guarantee. Even a well-mulched pot can struggle in a brutal winter, and extension offices are honest about that. But it's your best real shot at zone four in a container. Coreopsis blooms most of the summer too — cheerful yellow — and it needs almost nothing from you once it's established.

But before we get to number four, I need to stop and say something important. This one isn't just a gardening tip — it's a safety one.

Daylilies are everywhere in American yards — tough, forgiving, zone three to nine, and about as easy to plant as anything on this list. You just cover the crown with about an inch of soil and walk away. But here's the part that matters: daylilies are one of the most dangerous plants there is for cats. Every part of the plant — the leaves, the petals, even the pollen — can cause kidney failure in a cat, sometimes within three days, according to the ASPCA. This isn't a "watch them a little closer" situation. Keeping this one away from a cat is non-negotiable. If you have a cat, plant this one somewhere fenced off, well out of paw's reach, or skip it and stick with the other six. That's worth pausing the whole video for.

Alright — deep breath. Let's get back to easier territory!

Number five, Russian sage. If deer keep eating your flower beds, this is one they tend to leave alone. The silvery leaves carry a strong scent deer don't care for. It's hardy zone four to nine, drought-tolerant once it's rooted in. It'll bloom a soft purple through most of the fall, right up until frost. Plant it at the same depth it's sitting at in the nursery pot. Water it in well, and that's genuinely it!

Number six is a little different. I want to be straight with you about this one, not sell you on it — lavender. English lavender is only reliably hardy from zone five and up. If you're zone five or warmer, plant it now. It'll come back for years, and it smells wonderful along a walkway. But if you're in a colder zone, growers generally agree fall isn't the best time for lavender anyway. The roots need more warm weeks than September gives them, before a hard freeze. So think of this one as a bonus, not a must. Zone four and colder? Save it for spring instead.

And that brings us to the last one — the one I promised you right at the start. Sedum! Most growers plant this one barely below the surface, under an inch of soil. Just enough to hold the roots in place. There's no deep hole, no real digging at all. You could do this one from a garden stool, or sitting right in a chair — without ever picking up a shovel. It's hardy zone three to nine, it stores water in its own leaves. And by next spring, it comes back thicker than the year before.

So that's all seven: black-eyed Susan, hosta, coreopsis, daylily, Russian sage, lavender for the warmer zones, and sedum. Plant once this September, and by next spring, you're not back at the garden center buying the same tray again. You're just watching it come back.
