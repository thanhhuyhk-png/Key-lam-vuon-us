## TTS SCRIPT

That healthy hydrangea by your porch is already deciding next year's flowers. Extension research points to one hard date: August first. After that, hidden inside this year's stems, next year's flower buds are already forming.

For a lot of the country, that date has either just arrived or already slipped by. Cross it with a pair of shears, and you're not trimming a bush. You're cutting off flowers you won't even notice missing until next June.

Today: the one deadline extension offices actually agree on, straight from their own guidance, plus what to do if you've already crossed it. Then three things worth doing for the rest of this month, plus one safety note that's easy to miss. By the end, you'll know exactly which of those you're already getting wrong. So let's start with the one most folks get wrong first: water.

This is where most people either overdo it or forget it completely. Ask Extension and university programs in several different states don't agree on much. But they agree on this: one inch of water a week is the floor, not the ceiling. Push it to two inches once summer turns hot and dry. That's exactly when next year's buds are forming, so this isn't the week to let watering slide. It's easy to feel behind on this one — most gardeners are.

Here's why depth matters more than frequency. A quick daily splash only wets the top inch or two. Hydrangea roots sit shallow to begin with. A light sprinkle just trains them to stay even shallower, chasing water instead of digging down for it.

Extension research recommends soaking the soil about six inches deep instead, once or twice a week. A soaker hose or drip line at the base does this for you, come drought or downpour, usually a fraction of what a fancy bottle of root stimulator runs. Set it up, then step back. That's the kind of gardening that's easy on the back.

Watering keeps the plant alive. Mulch is the quiet second piece that makes it work even better.

Just a two to three inch layer of dirt-cheap mulch around the base holds moisture in. It also keeps roots cooler during a heat wave. That means less watering, and less stress. That's during the exact weeks the plant is setting next year's buds.

Just keep it pulled back an inch or two from the stems themselves. Piled against the base, mulch can trap moisture and invite rot.

One gardener in coastal Georgia says her hydrangeas barely need watering some weeks now that she mulches heavy every spring. Just the occasional deep soak in a real dry spell.

One more thing worth knowing, especially if you've got a dog, a cat, or grandkids who like to explore the yard. It doesn't come up in most hydrangea advice, but it deserves a place here.

Hydrangeas contain a compound called cyanogenic glycoside. The ASPCA lists them as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses. That's a real fact, not scare talk.

Here's the good news. Serious poisoning is rare. Most pets that nibble a leaf just end up with an upset stomach. Maybe some vomiting, maybe a little lethargy.

But if a pet eats a large amount, call your vet right away. The same goes if you notice repeated vomiting, diarrhea, or unusual tiredness. ASPCA Poison Control can help too. If a grandkid puts a leaf in their mouth, a quick call to Poison Control at one, eight hundred, two two two, one two two two, or your pediatrician, is the safe move.

Freshly cut stems and leaves are the easiest for a curious pet to reach. That's one more reason to keep those trimmings out of the yard once you've cleared them.

Water and mulch, handled. One safety note along the way. Now the one that decides everything else: the shears.

Here's the part that catches even careful gardeners off guard. If your hydrangea is a bigleaf or an oakleaf type, the pruning shears need to go back in the shed. That's it for the rest of the year.

Extension offices in different states word it differently, but they land on the exact same date: August first.

Prune after that, and you're not shaping the plant. You're removing next year's flower buds, buds so small right now, they're invisible.

Picture the stem itself for a second. Where a leaf meets the stem, there's a tiny swelling, barely a bump, easy to miss even up close. That bump is next June's flower, months before it ever shows color. Cut the stem off there, and that flower is simply gone.

That's the trap. It looks like tidy garden care. Extension offices point to it as one of the most common ways this plant loses its bloom for an entire year.

And this one's for anyone who already trimmed back a hydrangea in the last few weeks. Folks do it with good intentions, tidying up before fall. You may not have known the rule, and that's alright. Look closely at what's left. If any growth from this year is still there, leave it alone from here on. The buds may already be set, and every stem you keep is a chance at a flower next June.

Extension experts also warn that a hot, dry summer isn't good for bud development. So don't panic if you missed a watering or two. Occasional stress isn't the end of the world. But repeated neglect right now, on top of a late cut, adds up fast. That's how a healthy green bush ends up with zero flowers next spring.

If you have a panicle or a smooth hydrangea instead, you're in safer territory. Those bloom on new growth that hasn't even formed yet, so a late-summer trim typically won't cost you next year. Not sure which type you've got? A cone-shaped or football-shaped flower cluster usually points to panicle. A big round mophead points to bigleaf, the type this whole August rule is really about.

So that's the whole list — nothing complicated, just three things to remember, plus that one safety note. A deep watering once or twice a week does the job. A mulch layer protects those shallow roots. And the shears stay put after August first for bigleaf and oakleaf types. Fresh trimmings are worth watching if curious pets are around, that's the safety note.

Get the timing right, skip one bad habit, and enjoy a hydrangea that's the envy of the block.

People have loved this plant enough to build history around it. Back in the eighteen-twenties, a young German physician worked at Japan's only outpost for Westerners, a small trading island run by the Dutch. It was the only spot where Westerners were even allowed to set foot. He named a hydrangea species after the woman he loved there, folding her name right into its scientific name. Two centuries later, that same plant is still growing by front porches all over this country. Usually, it was planted by someone's grandmother. That was long before anyone in the family asked why it sometimes skipped a year.

The real cost of getting this wrong isn't money. It's an entire spring staring at a healthy green bush. Meanwhile your neighbor's hydrangea is covered in blooms.

When the timing is right, and the shears stay away at the wrong moment, the proof shows up next June. That's the bush people slow down to look at.
