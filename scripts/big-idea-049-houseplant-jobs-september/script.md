## TTS SCRIPT

Tonight, you flip the switch on the heat for the first time this year. Somewhere in the house, a plant you've cared for years is about to feel that shift. That's the one you'd hate to lose. Based on typical nursery pricing, replacing an eight-inch plant you've kept alive that long can run thirty to forty dollars. So before that heat kicks on, here's what's worth doing first.

Here's the thing. These seven jobs aren't really about one plant. They're about getting your whole indoor collection through heating season. Not just hoping for the best, then cleaning up the damage after. We pulled this from university extension guides, not guesswork. A couple of these might genuinely surprise you.

One of these is the job most people skip completely. And one of them? You'll want to handle it before that heat comes on — trust us on this one.

Let's start with the one everyone half-remembers but rarely acts on in time. This is a good week to stop, or scale back, on fertilizing. University of Maryland Extension puts the feeding window at March through September. Right now is close to your last real chance this year. Once the days shorten and growth slows, fertilizer doesn't disappear. It just sits in the soil as salt your plant isn't using anymore. A last feed sometime this month works well. Then the bottle can sit on the shelf until spring rolls back around.

Here's the one most people skip: wiping the dust off your leaves. It sounds almost too simple to matter. But Ohio State University Extension is direct about it. A layer of dust blocks sunlight and cuts into how much a plant can photosynthesize. That matters more right now, not less, because the days are already getting shorter. There's a reason extension offices call this one out specifically. It's the easiest job to let slide, because it never feels urgent. A plant almost never looks obviously worse from one day to the next.

Ever heard of the Aspidistra — the old "cast-iron plant"? By most accounts, it became the plant of nearly every Victorian parlor, bank lobby, and boarding house in England starting in the eighteen-twenties. It was tough enough to survive gaslight, neglect, and yes, layers of dust. And here's the honest part: it survived. It didn't thrive. Even the toughest houseplant on record just barely gets by under a coat of dust. A wipe with a damp cloth works well. So does a gentle rinse in the sink — worth doing before the light gets any scarcer.

Next up, watering. You'll likely water less often than you have been. Illinois Extension puts it simply: as the light drops, you'll be watering less. The fixed schedule you got used to in summer? It's worth dropping now. A quick finger-check of the soil beats reaching straight for the watering can. Less light means slower growth. And slower growth means your plant isn't drinking as much water as it did back in July. That's true even in the exact same pot.

Number four is a bug problem you didn't bring in from outside. This one grows right there in your living room. Spider mites are worth watching for this time of year. Iowa State University Extension notes that winter is actually when a lot of people first spot them. The dry indoor air favors them more than the cold ever does. It helps to check the underside of the leaves, not just the top. Tiny yellow-green speckles or fine webbing between the stems are the signs to catch. Catch it early, and that's one less thing to worry about — a simple insecticidal soap usually gets it under control within a week or two. Once the spray's dried on the leaves, it's fine for pets and grandkids to be back around the plant, just keep them clear until then, and store the bottle up out of reach like you would any spray. Miss it for too long, and the leaves can turn bronze and start dropping off the plant.

And that dry air causing the mites? It's frustrating — but it's also the root of almost every other problem this season.

Boosting humidity around your plants makes a real difference this time of year. Once the heat's running, indoor humidity can fall below thirty percent. Most houseplants want somewhere between forty and sixty. Penn State Extension and the University of New Hampshire agree on one thing: a small humidifier running near your plants a few hours a day makes the biggest real difference. Grouping your plants together helps a little too. But here's the honest part: pebble trays and misting, the two things most people reach for first, barely move the needle. Penn State found misting fades within minutes, and one extension office found pebble trays make almost no difference to the room overall. They're not useless — they just work best alongside the real fix. If the tips and edges of your leaves start turning brown and brittle, that's a signal. Your plant is telling you the air around it is simply too dry for what it needs right now.

And that brings us to the one worth handling before you flip that switch. It's a simple walk-through: move every pot a few feet clear of heating vents, radiators, and doorways. Three separate extension offices — New Hampshire, Iowa State, and Illinois — all point to the same thing. It's not the cold that does the damage here. It's a blast of hot, dry air hitting the leaves directly, right when the room's already short on moisture. One walk-through, before the heat's even on for the season, does the trick. You'll skip the scorched leaf tips most people don't notice until a few weeks in. By then, the damage is already done.

And here's the last one, and it's the opposite of what you'd expect. Don't repot anything right now. September feels like a natural deadline for everything. But three separate extension offices — Clemson, the University of Arkansas, and Penn State — all say the same thing: repotting works best in spring, when a plant is actively growing and ready to root into new soil. Do it now, right before your plant slows down for winter, and that fresh soil just stays wet. There's nowhere for the roots to use it — which sets you up for root rot, not new growth. So if a pot's been bugging you all summer, let it bug you a little longer. A note on the calendar for March means you won't forget. And the potting soil can wait.

So that's all seven: cutting back the fertilizer, wiping the dust off those leaves, and watering less than you did in summer. Also watching for spider mites, and boosting the humidity where you can. Then moving plants clear of the vents, and leaving the repotting for spring. Getting through this list before the heat's really running means you're not troubleshooting sick plants all winter long. You're just watching them sit there, fine, feeling a little smug while everyone else wonders what went wrong with theirs.
