## TTS SCRIPT

This tomato looks perfect. Until you turn it over.

That sunken, leathery black spot on the bottom? That's blossom-end rot. And here's the thing — it's almost never a calcium shortage in your soil. Penn State and Minnesota Extension have been saying so for years.

So before you crush another eggshell, before you buy another bottle of calcium spray, stop. Save your money. The real reason your tomatoes rot on the bottom is how calcium travels inside the plant — and the fix is one simple change to how you water.

Stick with me, because there's a watering mistake folks make in a heat wave that can ruin a whole crop in a single weekend. It's the third thing on our list, and I'll show you exactly how to dodge it.

I dug into what the university extension labs actually found — Purdue, Rutgers, Clemson, the works. And what they say flips the old calcium story right on its head. Here's what we'll cover: first, why calcium isn't the villain; then the three things that actually trigger the rot; and finally, the simple watering routine that fixes it for good, for free. By the end, you'll know why the eggshells never worked.

Let's start with the myth. For years the advice was simple: tomato bottoms rotting? Add calcium. Eggshells in the hole, lime in the soil, a spray on the leaves. And the rot kept coming back. Here's why it didn't work — most garden soil already has plenty of calcium. Minnesota Extension puts it plainly: this rot is not a sign your soil is short on calcium. It's a sign there's a water transport issue in the plant.

Here's the whole secret. Calcium can only ride one bus inside a tomato plant, and that bus is water. The plant pulls water up from its roots, through tiny pipes, out to the leaves and fruit — calcium hitches a ride on that water. No water moving, no calcium delivered, even when the soil is full of it.

The leaves are thirsty. They sweat out water all day, so they pull most of that calcium toward themselves. The fruit barely sweats at all — researchers measured it, and as a tomato grows, the fruit's thirst drops to about one tenth of what it was. So the leaves win, and the far end of the fruit, the blossom end, gets shorted first. That's why it's the bottom that rots, not the top, not the side — it's simply the last stop the water reaches. And it shows up early: the first signs often appear about two weeks after the flower sets fruit, so that gorgeous first tomato of the season is often the one that breaks your heart.

An eggshell takes years to break down in the soil — it can't feed this season's tomato. Mississippi State Extension tested it and said the same: eggshells don't fix this. So it's not about how much calcium is in the dirt — it's about whether the plant can move it.

There are three big triggers, and I'd bet you're guilty of at least one — I know my grandmother was.

Trigger one: too much nitrogen, the wrong kind. Feed a tomato heavy high-nitrogen fertilizer, especially ammonium, and two things go wrong. Ammonium elbows calcium out of the way at the roots — Purdue and Illinois both found it competes for the same door in. And all that nitrogen pushes fast, leafy growth that simply can't move calcium out to the fruit fast enough. So go easy. A tomato doesn't need to be huge. It needs to be steady.

Trigger two: damaged roots. This one catches careful gardeners — hoeing too close to the plant slices roots that drink water and carry calcium. Rutgers Extension says it straight: cultivating too deep and too close cuts off the roots the plant needs. Pull weeds by hand near the base, or better yet, smother them with mulch.

And trigger three, the big one — the heat-wave mistake I promised you. It's not too much water. It's not too little water. It's the swing between the two. Picture a hot July week: you let the bed go bone-dry for three days, then feel guilty and drown it, then it dries out again. That seesaw — dry to soaked to dry — is the single most common cause of blossom-end rot there is. Every time the soil dries out, the water stops moving, and the calcium delivery stalls. Don't panic if this is you. It's the most natural mistake in the world, and also the easiest to fix.

And that fix costs nothing — no products, just a watering can and a handful of mulch. Stick with me, because near the end I'll give you the honest truth about that calcium spray on the store shelf, and whether it's worth a single dollar.

Step one: water deep, and water even. Tomatoes want about one to two inches of water a week — that's the number extension services keep coming back to. But it's not the amount that matters most, it's the steadiness. One to two inches spread across the week, not one big flood on Sunday and nothing till Friday. The goal is soil that stays evenly damp, like a wrung-out sponge — never dust, never mud. When the moisture holds steady, the plant moves its own calcium, all by itself. Here's a thirty-second trick: push your finger two inches down into the soil at the base of the plant. Dry, it's time to water. Still damp, wait a day. No fancy meter, just your finger.

Step two, the lazy gardener's secret weapon: mulch. Lay down two to three inches — straw, shredded leaves, wood chips, whatever you've got. That blanket holds moisture in so the soil doesn't swing from wet to dry, and it keeps the weeds down too, so you're not slicing roots with a hoe. One blanket solves two of our three triggers at once. My grandmother mulched with straw and never once said the word calcium — she just knew covered soil grows better tomatoes. And it's perfectly safe around pets and grandkids, no chemicals in the bed.

Step three: let the water reach the roots, not the leaves. A soaker hose or drip line at the base of the plant puts water in the soil slow and steady, right where the roots can drink, and it runs you a few dollars and lasts for years. If you're hand-watering, do it in the cool of the morning, so the roots are loaded before the heat hits. Water at night and the soil sits soggy for hours. Container tomatoes dry out much faster than the ground — in a real heat wave, check daily, since that fast drying is exactly the swing that causes rot.

Now, the honest truth about that calcium spray on the store shelf. Spraying calcium right onto the fruit does not work — Clemson Extension explains that the skin of a tomato simply can't soak up calcium. So that eight-dollar bottle sprayed on your tomatoes is eight dollars gone. A light calcium spray on young leaves can help a little, according to UConn, but even they say the water matters more. And adding calcium to the soil is only worth it in one case: if a soil test actually shows you're short. Most folks who test are surprised to find their soil had plenty all along. So before you spend a dime, do a simple soil test first — your local extension office can help, often for just a few dollars.

So what about the tomatoes already rotting on the vine? A tomato with the black bottom won't heal — pick it off and toss it, so the plant sends its water and calcium to the healthy fruit instead. Most extension services say the unspoiled half is fine to eat — though when in doubt, throw it out.

Once you steady that watering, the next round of tomatoes usually comes in clean — the plant was never sick, just thirsty at the wrong moments.

So let's bring it home. Blossom-end rot was never really about calcium. Your soil most likely had plenty all along. The black bottom is just the plant telling you it couldn't move that calcium to the fruit, because the water kept stopping and starting. Go easy on the nitrogen, keep that hoe away from the roots, and above all, never let the soil swing from bone-dry to soaked. Steady watering quiets all three triggers at once.

Remember the three little words that fix it: it's not the calcium, it's the water, and the steadiness. Do two things this week: lay down a few inches of mulch, and soak deeply two or three times a week instead of waiting for the plant to wilt. No sprays, no eggshells, no money spent.

Grow good food, save your money, and have a little fun out there. Happy gardening.
