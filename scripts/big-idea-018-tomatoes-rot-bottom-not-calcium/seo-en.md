## YOUTUBE DESCRIPTION

**Blossom end rot: stop buying calcium — the real fix is how you water.**

Learn why tomatoes rot on the bottom isn't a calcium shortage in your soil—it's a watering consistency problem. Senior gardening tips backed by university research from Penn State, Purdue, and Minnesota Extension. Watch this before you buy another bottle of calcium spray or crush another eggshell.

In this video, you'll learn:
- Why most garden soil already has plenty of calcium (and why that doesn't stop the rot)
- The exact reason the blossom end (not the top) gets hit first
- The 3 triggers that cause BER in your tomatoes—including the heat-wave watering mistake
- The free, 1-page watering routine that fixes it for good
- Why calcium sprays and eggshells fail (and what actually works)
- How to handle tomatoes that are already rotting on the vine

**Here's the science:** Calcium travels through the plant only in water, through transpiration. When soil moisture swings from dry to soaked—especially in summer heat—the water flow stops, and calcium delivery stalls. The fruit's blossom end, being at the far end of the line, gets shorted first. The fix isn't more calcium. It's steady watering.

Three simple steps:
1. Water deeply and evenly—1 to 2 inches per week, spread across the week (not one big flood)
2. Mulch 2–3 inches around the base with straw or leaves (the lazy gardener's secret weapon—buffers the moisture swings and stops root damage)
3. Use a soaker hose or drip line, water in the cool morning—let the roots drink before the heat of the day

For container tomatoes, check daily in heat—pots dry out fast and create the same moisture swings that ruin ground-planted fruit.

**Before you spend a dime:** Most folks who test their soil find they already had enough calcium all along. Do a simple soil test first (your local extension office can help, often for a few dollars). That's the only way to know for sure.

A tomato that's already rotting on the bottom won't heal. Pick those off so the plant stops wasting water and calcium on a lost cause. The good part of the fruit (where there's no dark spot) is still safe to eat—just cut away the blackened bit.

The honest truth about foliar calcium sprays: spraying calcium directly onto the fruit doesn't work. The skin of a tomato can't absorb it. Even spraying young leaves is unreliable. Fix the water first. Always the water first.

**Bottom line:** It's not the calcium. It's the water, and the steadiness. One gardener told us she'd thrown away three bottles of calcium spray over the years, fixed nothing. Then she laid down straw, set a simple watering rhythm, and her August tomatoes came in flawless.

Do this today: lay down a few inches of mulch, set yourself a simple rhythm—a good deep soak two or three times a week instead of waiting for the plant to wilt. That's it. No sprays. No eggshells. No money spent.

Your tomatoes will thank you. Your neighbors will ask what you changed. And you'll have the answer they've all been getting wrong for years.

Happy gardening.
