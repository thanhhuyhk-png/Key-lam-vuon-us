## Câu hỏi 1: Xác nhận độc tính của 10 cây phổ biến bán ở nursery US và bộ phận độc, độc tố, triệu chứng, mức độ phổ biến

### 3-Tier Source Plan
- **Tier 1 target:** aspca.org (ASPCA Poison Control Plant Database), extension.edu (Colorado State, Penn State, Wisconsin Extension)
- **Tier 2 target:** poison.org (National Capital Poison Center), peer-reviewed journals via PubMed
- **Tier 3 fallback:** poisonousplants.cvmbs.colostate.edu (CSU Vet Med)

### Findings

**1. Foxglove — Digitalis purpurea**
- Toxic compound: Cardiac glycosides (digitoxin, digoxin, and related genins) found in **all parts** of the plant. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/foxglove
- Clinical signs (pets): Cardiac arrhythmias, vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, cardiac failure, death — toxic to dogs, cats, horses. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/foxglove
- Clinical signs (humans): Cardiac arrhythmia, gastrointestinal symptoms, CNS symptoms; life-threatening bradyarrhythmias and ventricular arrhythmias possible. (ScienceDirect — Digitalis purpurea overview, peer-reviewed) — https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/digitalis-purpurea
- Nursery availability: Widely sold at Home Depot, specialty nurseries; popular cottage garden perennial in USDA zones 4–9. (LSU AgCenter Extension, 2013) — https://www.lsuagcenter.com/portals/communications/news/news_archive/2013/november/headline_news/plant-foxgloves-in-fall-for-gorgeous-spring-blooms
- **[YMYL]** All plant parts toxic; cardiac glycosides have "very narrow therapeutic range." Even small accidental ingestion warrants immediate contact with Poison Control.

**2. Oleander — Nerium oleander**
- Toxic compound: Cardiac glycosides (all parts implied; page does not specify individual parts — entire plant should be considered dangerous). (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/oleander
- Clinical signs (pets — dogs, cats, horses): Drooling, abdominal pain, diarrhea, colic, depression, death. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/oleander
- Nursery availability: Very common landscape shrub, USDA zones 8–11; widely sold in Southern/Southwestern US nurseries.
- **[YMYL]** "Life threatening" per ASPCA; cardiac arrhythmias and low blood pressure documented.

**3. Lily of the Valley — Convallaria majalis**
- Toxic compound: Cardenolides (convallarin and others) — **all parts** implied (ASPCA does not specify sub-parts). (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/lily-valley
- Clinical signs (pets): Vomiting, irregular heartbeat, low blood pressure, disorientation, coma, seizures. Toxic to dogs and cats. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/lily-valley
- Nursery availability: Popular shade groundcover, sold spring at most garden centers, USDA zones 2–9.
- **[YMYL]** Cardiac effects similar to digitalis; all above-ground parts and roots considered toxic.

**4. Yew — Taxus spp. (English, Japanese, Pacific, American)**
- Toxic compound: Taxine A and Taxine B alkaloids + volatile oil. Present in **all parts except the fleshy red aril** (the red berry coating). The seed inside the aril IS toxic. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/japanese-yew ; (Penn State Extension, date on file) — https://extension.psu.edu/toxicity-of-yew-wood-and-roots
- Clinical signs (pets): Tremors, difficulty breathing, vomiting, seizures, sudden death from acute heart failure — dogs, cats, horses. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/japanese-yew
- Taxine persistence: "Almost completely insoluble in water"; roots retain toxic levels even 7 years after excavation. (Penn State Extension) — https://extension.psu.edu/toxicity-of-yew-wood-and-roots
- Nursery availability: One of the most popular evergreen foundation/hedge shrubs in US; sold in nearly every garden center nationwide.
- **[YMYL]** Sudden death documented in animals; the red berry aril is the only non-toxic part — however the seed inside remains toxic.

**5. Daffodil — Narcissus spp.**
- Toxic compound: Lycorine (highest in bulb) + calcium oxalate crystals (sap and bulb). (National Capital Poison Center / poison.org, accessed 2026) — https://www.poison.org/articles/daffodils
- Toxic parts: **All parts toxic; bulb is most dangerous.** "All parts of the daffodil are toxic." Bulb most concentrated. (poison.org, accessed 2026) — https://www.poison.org/articles/daffodils
- Clinical signs (humans): Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea (typically lasts ~3 hours); severe cases: difficulty swallowing, drooling. Severe problems (low blood pressure) documented in animals consuming very large amounts. (poison.org, accessed 2026) — https://www.poison.org/articles/daffodils
- Clinical signs (pets): Vomiting, salivation, diarrhea; large ingestions cause convulsions, low blood pressure, tremors, cardiac arrhythmias. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/daffodil
- Bulb confusion risk: Daffodil bulbs can be mistaken for onion bulbs when foraging — documented poisoning case in peer-reviewed literature (case of family intoxication). (Ageta et al., Acute Medicine & Surgery, 2020) — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ams2.595
- Nursery availability: Among the top-selling spring bulbs in the US; sold at virtually all garden centers.
- **[YMYL]** Bulbs are especially dangerous; warn viewers not to store loose bulbs near kitchen produce.

**6. Hydrangea — Hydrangea arborescens (and related spp.)**
- Toxic compound: Cyanogenic glycoside (hydrangin). (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/hydrangea ; (LSU School of Veterinary Medicine) — https://www.lsu.edu/vetmed/toxic_plants/hydrangea.php
- Toxic parts: ASPCA page does not specify sub-parts. General toxicology sources note cyanogenic glycosides throughout plant tissue.
- Clinical signs (pets): Vomiting, depression, diarrhea. "Actual cyanide poisoning from hydrangea is uncommon." (LSU Vet Med / ASPCA, accessed 2026)
- Human toxicity note: Requires ingestion of a **very large quantity** for severe symptoms; gastrointestinal upset is the typical presentation. Cyanide intoxication is rare. (multiple toxicology sources reviewed)
- Nursery availability: One of the best-selling flowering shrubs in the US; universally available at garden centers, USDA zones 3–9.
- **[YMYL]** Severity is dose-dependent and typically mild for casual exposure; however the cyanogenic mechanism warrants mention with appropriate hedging (see Q5 for framing guidance).

**7. Sago Palm — Cycas revoluta**
- Toxic compound: Cycasin (a carcinogenic, hepatotoxic glycoside). (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/sago-palm
- Toxic parts: **Entire plant is toxic** (seeds, fronds, roots); seeds contain the highest concentration. (ASPCA News — The Dangers of the Sago Palm, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/news/dangers-sago-palm
- Clinical signs (pets): Vomiting, melena (bloody stool), icterus (jaundice), increased thirst, hemorrhagic gastroenteritis, bruising, coagulopathy, liver damage, liver failure, death. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/sago-palm
- Fatality rate: "Up to 50% of ingestion cases are fatal" — ASPCA Animal Poison Control data. (ASPCA News, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/news/dangers-sago-palm ; (Tufts Cummings School of Veterinary Medicine, accessed 2026) — https://vet.tufts.edu/news-events/news/ingestion-popular-house-plant-can-be-fatal-pets
- Nursery availability: Common indoor/outdoor ornamental especially in USDA zones 8–11 (FL, TX, CA, Gulf Coast); sold at Home Depot, Lowes, specialty nurseries.
- **[YMYL]** Highest-severity plant on this list for pets. Tufts veterinary faculty state prevention is the only safe strategy: "the best way to prevent sago toxicosis is not to have the plant in the home."

**8. Azalea / Rhododendron — Rhododendron spp.**
- Toxic compound: Grayantoxin (also called andromedotoxin). (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/azalea
- Toxic parts: ASPCA does not specify sub-parts; general guidance treats all parts as potentially hazardous.
- Clinical signs (pets): Vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, cardiac failure — dogs and cats; vomiting does not occur in horses. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/azalea
- Human risk: "Even a few leaves can cause serious problems" per general extension guidance reviewed.
- Nursery availability: One of the most popular flowering landscape shrubs in the US; sold nationwide, USDA zones 4–9 (varies by species).
- **[YMYL]** Cardiac failure documented; "ingestion of a few leaves can cause serious problems."

**9. English Ivy — Hedera helix**
- Toxic compound: Triterpenoid saponins (hederagenin). (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/english-ivy
- Toxic parts: **Foliage is more toxic than berries** — both are toxic. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/english-ivy
- Clinical signs (pets): Vomiting, abdominal pain, hypersalivation, diarrhea. Toxic to dogs, cats, horses. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026)
- Human risk (additional): English ivy can cause allergic contact dermatitis in sensitized individuals; the allergen is falcarinol (a polyacetylene). Symptoms include linear vesicular rashes on hands/forearms. (Jones et al., Contact Dermatitis, 2009 — peer-reviewed) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19260922/
- Nursery availability: Extremely common groundcover/houseplant; sold nationwide.
- **[YMYL]** Contact dermatitis risk for gardeners handling bare-handed; ingestion risk primarily for pets.

**10. Lantana — Lantana camara (selected as plant #10)**
- Rationale for selection: Widely sold as annual bedding plant/container plant nationally; unripe berries attract children and pets due to bright color.
- Toxic compound: Pentacyclic triterpenoids. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/lantana
- Toxic parts: Unripe berries are toxic; leaves cause contact dermatitis or minor skin irritation. (University of Wisconsin Extension — Horticulture, accessed 2026) — https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/lantana/
- Clinical signs (pets): Vomiting, diarrhea, labored breathing, weakness; liver failure more common in livestock than companion animals. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026)
- Human/children data: A study of 641 pediatric cases (mean age 2.5 years) found ingestion "not associated with significant toxicity" — most children had no or minimal symptoms; vomiting in 30/641 cases. (PubMed — Ingestion of Lantana camara is not associated with significant effects in children, 2010) — https://pubmed-ncbi-nlm-nih-gov.translate.goog/21041281/
- Nursery availability: One of the most popular summer annuals/perennials in the US; sold at virtually all garden centers as bedding plant, container plant, and hanging basket, USDA zones 8–11 as perennial, annual in zones 1–8. (UW Extension, accessed 2026)
- **[YMYL]** Unripe berries are a child/pet concern due to attractive appearance; human toxicity appears milder than pets based on pediatric case data, but still warrants mention.

## Câu hỏi 2: Luật liên bang và quy định về dán nhãn cảnh báo cây độc tại nursery Hoa Kỳ

### 3-Tier Source Plan
- **Tier 1:** epa.gov (FIFRA), ftc.gov (FTC Nursery Guides), cpsc.gov (FHSA), congress.gov
- **Tier 2:** State extension offices, state legislation
- **Tier 3:** Legal analysis sites

### Findings

**FIFRA (EPA) — scope is pesticides ONLY:**
- FIFRA (Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act) governs registration, labeling, sale, and use of **pesticides** — it does NOT apply to the inherent natural toxicity of ornamental plants sold to consumers. (US EPA — FIFRA and Federal Facilities, accessed 2026) — https://www.epa.gov/enforcement/federal-insecticide-fungicide-and-rodenticide-act-fifra-and-federal-facilities

**FTC Nursery Guides — rescinded 2019:**
- FTC had "Guides for the Nursery Industry" (16 CFR Part 18, issued 1979) addressing deceptive sales practices for plants (quantity, size, species, hardiness, etc.) — but these guides did NOT require toxic plant warning labels.
- The FTC **formally rescinded** these guides on June 12, 2019, because "they no longer appear necessary." Nurseries remain subject to general FTC prohibitions on deceptive trade practices, but no sector-specific toxic labeling requirement exists. (FTC Press Release, 2019) — https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/06/ftc-approves-proposal-rescinding-nursery-guides

**FHSA (CPSC) — applies to packaged hazardous substances, NOT live plants:**
- The Federal Hazardous Substances Act (CPSC) requires cautionary labeling for hazardous consumer products (toxic, corrosive, flammable, etc. substances). However, the FHSA's scope applies to products/substances in packages — live potted plants sold in nurseries are not classified as "packaged hazardous substances" under FHSA. (CPSC — FHSA Requirements, accessed 2026) — https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/FHSA-Requirements

**FDA — no applicable jurisdiction:**
- FDA regulates food, drugs, cosmetics, and medical devices. Live ornamental plants sold in nurseries do not fall under FDA jurisdiction unless marketed with health/drug claims.

**USDA — no consumer toxic labeling requirement:**
- USDA regulates plant pests, disease, invasive species, and agricultural trade. No USDA regulation requires consumer toxic warning labels on ornamental nursery plants.

**Conclusion — confirmed: NO federal law requires toxic plant warning labels at retail:**
- The Lady Bird Johnson Wildflower Center (affiliated with University of Texas) states plainly: "There aren't any laws pertaining to the sale of poisonous plants." The center notes: "If there were laws prohibiting the sale of poisonous plants, there wouldn't be much left to sell in the nurseries." (wildflower.org — expert response, accessed 2026; note: site returned 403 during fetch — citation sourced from search snippet only) — https://www.wildflower.org/expert/show.php?id=3796

**State-level exception (Connecticut):**
- Connecticut introduced HB 06470 (2003) requiring retailers to affix warning labels on plants containing poisonous substances. The bill text exists in public record. (Connecticut General Assembly Bill Text, 2003) — https://www.cga.ct.gov/2003/tob/h/2003HB-06470-R00-HB.htm
- [NEED DATA: Confirm whether Connecticut HB 06470 (2003) was enacted into law or died in committee. Bill text only confirms it was introduced, not passed.]

**California Prop 65 — plants:**
- Some plant retailers in California affix Prop 65 warnings on plants. This is a California-specific consumer protection law, not federal. (Monrovia — "Why the Prop 65 Warning on Plants," referenced in search results) — https://go.monrovia.com/help/why-the-prop-65-warning-on-plants-in-california

## Câu hỏi 3: Cặp "cây độc → cây thay thế an toàn cho pet & trẻ em"

### 3-Tier Source Plan
- **Tier 1:** ASPCA non-toxic plant list (aspca.org)
- **Tier 2:** NC State Extension Plant Toolbox (plants.ces.ncsu.edu)
- **Tier 3:** UW Extension horticulture articles

### Findings

**Cặp 1: Foxglove → Snapdragon (Antirrhinum majus)**
- Foxglove: Toxic (cardiac glycosides, all parts, pets + humans) — see Q1
- Snapdragon: Non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses per ASPCA. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/common-snapdragon
- Landscape equivalence: Both are tall-spike cottage garden flowers producing columnar flower wands 2–4 ft tall; comparable ornamental use in borders and cutting gardens. Tall snapdragon cultivars USDA zones 7–10; grown as cool-season annual in zones 1–6. (NC State Extension Plant Toolbox, accessed 2026) — https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/antirrhinum-majus/
- Snapdragon described as: "An old-fashioned cottage garden flower that bears a resemblance to its cousin the foxglove." (Gardenista, non-extension source — for context only; not cited as primary)

**Cặp 2: Oleander → Crape Myrtle (Lagerstroemia indica)**
- Oleander: Toxic (cardiac glycosides, all parts, dogs/cats/horses) — see Q1
- Crape Myrtle: Non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses per ASPCA. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/crape-myrtle ; (ASPCA — Lagerstroemia indica page) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/lagerstroemia-indica
- Landscape equivalence: Both are large flowering shrubs/small trees used as landscape anchors in warm climates; Crape Myrtle provides summer blooms (pink/red/white), tolerates heat, USDA zones 6–10. Both are commonly sold at big-box garden centers in the South.

**Cặp 3: Lily of the Valley → Sweet Alyssum (Alyssum spp. / Lobularia maritima)**
- Lily of the Valley: Toxic (cardenolides, cardiac effects) — see Q1
- Sweet Alyssum: Non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses per ASPCA. Scientific name: Alyssum spp. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/alyssum
- Landscape equivalence: Both are low-growing, fragrant, spring-blooming groundcover plants. Sweet alyssum produces clusters of small fragrant flowers, grows 3–6 inches tall, excellent for borders, edging, and suppressing weeds. Tolerates partial shade. Annual in most US zones. (NC State Extension Plant Toolbox, accessed 2026) — https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/lobularia-maritima/
- Note: Sweet alyssum does NOT have the deep shade tolerance of Lily of the Valley; for shaded areas under trees, consider creeping Jenny (Lysimachia nummularia) — however ASPCA classification for creeping Jenny should be independently verified before recommending.

**Bonus Cặp 4: Azalea/Rhododendron → Camellia (Camellia japonica)**
- Azalea: Toxic (grayantoxin, cardiac effects) — see Q1
- Camellia: Non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses per ASPCA. (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/camellia
- Landscape equivalence: Both are evergreen flowering shrubs with large showy blooms; Camellia blooms fall–spring (pink, red, white), USDA zones 7–9, provides similar landscape structure and seasonal interest without cardiac toxicity risk.

## Câu hỏi 5: Cách diễn đạt "all parts toxic" vs "berries/bulb/seeds only" cho người xem 60–80 tuổi (Grade 6–8)

### 3-Tier Source Plan
- **Tier 1:** aspca.org toxicity data, poison.org (National Capital Poison Center)
- **Tier 2:** UConn Extension disclaimer language
- **Tier 3:** Toxicology summaries reviewed above

### Findings

**Khung ngôn ngữ Grade 6–8, không gây hoảng sợ:**

**Ví dụ 1 — Yew (All parts except aril):**
- Fact: Taxine alkaloids are present in needles, bark, roots, and the dark seed inside the red berry. The fleshy red coating (aril) is the one part that is NOT toxic — but the seed it wraps around IS. (Penn State Extension, accessed 2026) — https://extension.psu.edu/toxicity-of-yew-wood-and-roots ; (ASPCA Poison Control, accessed 2026) — https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/japanese-yew
- Framing language (Grade 6–8): "With yew, the concern is the whole plant — the green needles, the bark, and especially those pretty red berries, which have a poisonous seed hiding inside. The only part that isn't toxic is the red skin around that seed. That's a lot to keep track of, which is why many gardeners with pets choose a different hedge altogether."

**Ví dụ 2 — Daffodil (Bulb is most toxic, but all parts are toxic):**
- Fact: All parts contain lycorine; bulb has the highest concentration and is the primary concern. Ingesting any part can cause GI symptoms; the bulb poses the greatest risk because it is sometimes mistaken for an onion. (poison.org — National Capital Poison Center, accessed 2026) — https://www.poison.org/articles/daffodils
- Framing language (Grade 6–8): "Daffodils are interesting because the whole plant has some toxicity — the stems, leaves, even the flowers. But the big worry is the bulb. It sits underground all winter and looks a lot like an onion. There have been documented cases of people accidentally cooking with them. So if you have grandchildren or grandpets who dig in the garden, keep that in mind."

**Ví dụ 3 — Hydrangea (Large dose required, typically mild):**
- Fact: Cyanogenic glycoside present; however, "actual cyanide poisoning from hydrangea is uncommon" — gastrointestinal upset is the typical outcome. A very large quantity would be needed for severe symptoms. (LSU Vet Med / ASPCA, accessed 2026)
- Framing language (Grade 6–8): "Hydrangea is on the list because it does contain a compound that can release cyanide when chewed. That sounds alarming, but here's the honest picture: vets say true cyanide poisoning from hydrangea is quite rare. Your pet would have to eat a lot of it. You're more likely to see some stomach upset. It's still worth knowing — but hydrangea is not in the same category as sago palm or oleander."

If you or someone in your home has ingested any plant material and you're concerned, call the National Poison Control Center at 1-800-222-1222 (for people) or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 1-888-426-4435 (for pets). When in doubt, call first. This information comes from publicly available ASPCA, university extension, and government sources — but your local poison control specialists have the full picture."

## YMYL Claims — Cần Hedge Trong Script

1. **Foxglove ingestion (all parts)** → "If anyone or any pet has eaten any part of a foxglove, call poison control immediately — do not wait for symptoms." Do NOT say "it will cause death" — say "it can be life-threatening." Never advise home treatment.
2. **Oleander cardiac effects** → ASPCA uses word "life threatening" — mirror that language; add "this is an emergency call to poison control, not a wait-and-see situation."
3. **Sago Palm 50% fatality** → Sourced from ASPCA official data; use exactly "up to 50% of ingestion cases are fatal even with treatment" — cite ASPCA and Tufts. Do NOT reframe as "certain death." Always add "early veterinary care improves outcomes."
4. **Daffodil bulb / onion confusion** → Frame as practical safety tip, not alarm: "store bulbs away from kitchen produce." Cite peer-reviewed case study.
5. **Yew seed toxicity** → Clarify the red aril vs seed nuance carefully; a misstatement ("the red berry is safe") could mislead viewers. Always add "the seed inside is still toxic."
6. **English Ivy contact dermatitis** → YMYL only for gardeners handling plants: "wear gloves when pruning — some people develop a skin rash from repeated contact." Not a severe systemic toxin for casual contact.
7. **Lantana unripe berries** → Pediatric data from PubMed shows mild outcomes in children; hedge appropriately: "most children who eat a few berries experience only mild stomach upset, but call Poison Control to be safe."
8. **Hydrangea cyanogenic glycoside** → Do NOT say "contains cyanide" without the qualifying statement that severe cases are rare and require large quantities. Frame: "technically contains a compound that can become cyanide, but real-world severity is usually mild stomach upset."
