Tonight might be the first real cold snap of the year. And if you've got houseplants still sitting outside, that's the night the countdown quietly starts. So here's the real question: what if you brought it in the right way, instead of just the fast way?

Here's the thing. Bringing a plant inside isn't just about beating the frost — how you do it matters just as much as when. Rush it, and you can lose a plant you actually love — one that would've been totally fine outside for another month. That's a frustrating way to lose a plant. A few of these six also come with a pet-safety note worth knowing if you've got a curious cat, dog, or grandkid who likes to touch everything. We pulled this list from university extension guides, not personal trial and error: pothos, spider plant, snake plant, jade plant, ponytail palm, and Christmas cactus.

Number two is the one most folks kill on the way inside. And number six needs a step nobody talks about.

Let's start with the plant that's probably already sitting on a porch or windowsill somewhere near you. Whether people realize it or not, it's pothos. It does best between sixty and seventy degrees at night. Cold drafts sneaking in from a window or heater vent can dry out and damage the leaves fast. That can happen even before real frost hits — easy to miss until the damage is done. One safety note before we move on: the ASPCA lists pothos as toxic to both dogs and cats. It's the calcium oxalates in the leaves, and they cause mouth irritation, drooling, and trouble swallowing if chewed. Keep this one up on a shelf, or out of reach, if you've got a curious pet at home.

That toughness pothos has? NASA actually put it to the test, decades before houseplant trends were a thing. Back in nineteen eighty, a NASA scientist named Bill Wolverton built a small sealed test chamber. He wanted to see whether ordinary houseplants could clean formaldehyde out of the air. The goal: future space stations and energy-efficient homes. The very first plant he tested was pothos. There's something reassuring about knowing this plant's been tested that hard. Nine years later, NASA released the full report at a press conference in Washington, D.C. And that same report put a number on the next plant on our list.

Spider plant. And here's the truth about number two on this list — the one I promised would surprise you. Spider plants tolerate cool rooms just fine. Extension offices even list them as fine down into the mid-forties, colder than most people would guess. What actually kills them isn't the cold, though. It's the shock that gets them — a blast of freezing wind through an open door. Or a sudden jump from bright shade straight into a dim hallway. Clemson's extension office tells people to keep spider plants a few feet back from drafty windows and doors. That distance is worth keeping all winter long. And unlike pothos, this one's completely non-toxic to dogs and cats — one less thing to watch for. That's a nice bit of relief if you've got a curious pet.

Number three, snake plant. It handles cool rooms down to around fifty degrees, tougher than pothos. But here's what makes it sneaky: it won't show you it's struggling. No droopy leaves, no obvious wilting, nothing that jumps out at you. The damage happens quietly, on the inside — kind of unsettling, honestly — long before you'd notice from across the room. One more note before we move on: the ASPCA lists snake plant as toxic to dogs and cats too, from compounds called saponins — worth remembering if you share your home with a chewer.

Three plants in, three to go. But before we keep moving, there's one I need you to hear clearly. It's the plant most people get completely wrong.

Jade plant. It looks like a tough little succulent, and it is — but here's what almost nobody expects. The ASPCA lists jade as toxic not just to dogs and cats, but to horses too. Most people assume succulents are the safe choice around pets, because nothing about a jade plant looks dangerous. Jade is the exception to that assumption. If you have a cat or dog that likes to chew on leaves, keeping this one out of reach is non-negotiable. And if you ever suspect they've gotten into it, call your vet — better a wasted phone call than a guess.

And while we're talking safety, here's the part of "bringing plants inside" most people skip entirely. The actual process. Extension offices agree it's not one step, it's three.

First up, it helps to check for pests. A magnifying lens helps here, if you've got one handy. Either way, it's worth checking both the top and underside of every leaf. That's where things like mealybugs like to hide. An insecticidal soap spray on both sides of the leaves handles most of what you'll find. Let the leaves dry before pets or grandkids get close again, and keep the bottle up out of reach when you're done.

Second, the plant does better with a gradual adjustment. Moving it straight from bright outdoor light into a dim hallway tends to stress it out. Extension guides recommend easing it in over about a week instead.

Third, it's smart to keep any new plant isolated from your other houseplants for three to four weeks. If it's carrying something, that's how you catch it before it spreads to the whole collection. Honestly, this three-step routine takes the guesswork out of bringing plants in.

One more thing worth knowing: a plant might drop or yellow a few leaves in its first couple weeks inside. That's not a crisis — promise. It's just adjusting.

Number five has the strangest shape on this whole list — ponytail palm. It's got that big swollen base at the bottom, storing water like a camel's hump. There's something oddly comforting about a plant built to forgive your mistakes. That's what lets it shrug off the occasional missed watering better than almost anything else here. It handles the cold about the same as jade and snake plant, down around fifty degrees. And the good news: the ASPCA lists it as non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses. After jade, that's a nice one to hear.

Unlike every other plant on this list, this one actually needs a cold spell before it comes inside for good. Extension offices agree it needs several weeks with night temperatures around fifty to fifty-five degrees to set its flower buds. Skip that, and you'll end up disappointed — a healthy green plant with no flowers at all come December. And despite the name, this isn't a desert plant.
