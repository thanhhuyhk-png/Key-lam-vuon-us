The end of November is nearly here, and gardeners all across the United States are getting their beds ready for winter.
But here is something a lot of people overlook. Falling temperatures do not automatically mean your planting season is
over. Depending on where you live, there are still several vegetables you can get into the ground before December
arrives. Some will put down roots before the soil freezes. Others will grow slowly through the cool months, particularly
with the help of mulch, row covers, or a simple cold frame. And in the warmer parts of the country, November can
actually be one of the best times of the year to start a brand new vegetable garden. There is one detail you need to
keep in mind though. A vegetable that thrives in USDA zone 9 may struggle in zone 5, and a planting method that works
through the mild winters of the South may come too late for gardeners in the northern states. That is why today we're
looking at nine vegetables you can still consider planting before December. For each one, we'll cover the suitable USDA
hardiness zones, the planting method, the growing requirements, and practical tips for winter protection. We'll also
explain which of these vegetables are realistic choices for colder regions, which ones suit warmer climates better, and
which ones need a little extra help to make it through the winter. So before you put your tools away for the season,
take a look at these nine vegetables. Your next harvest may be closer than you think. And be sure to stay until the end
because our final vegetable is especially interesting for anyone who wants a head start on next year's harvest. Number
9, garlic. If one vegetable truly belongs in a late fall garden, it is garlic. Most vegetables need warm weather and a
long growing season, but garlic is naturally suited to an autumn schedule. It spends the winter building roots beneath
the soil, and then it sends up strong green shoots as soon as temperatures rise in spring. That makes November an
important planting opportunity for many gardeners across the country. USDA hardiness zones roughly zones 3 through 9
depending on the garlic variety and your local growing conditions. Garlic is an especially good fit for gardeners in
zones 3 through 7 who soil is still workable and has not yet frozen deeply. In zones 8 and 9 planting can often continue
later into November because the winters are generally milder. In very warm regions, choose garlic varieties that are
adapted to your climate, such as suitable soft neck types or varieties recommended by local growers. How to plant
garlic? Garlic is planted from individual cloves, not from ordinary seeds. Begin with healthy bulbs that are sold for
planting. Try not to rely on supermarket garlic because some commercial bulbs are treated to prevent sprouting and they
may not be suited to your climate. Gently break the bulb apart into individual cloves and keep the papery covering on
each one intact. Pick a spot that gets at least 6 hours of direct sunlight and has loose, well-drained soil. Plant each
clove about 2 in deep with the pointed end facing up and the flat root end facing down. Space the cloves 4 to 6 in apart
with about 12 in between rows. If your soil needs improving, mix in some finished compost before planting. Garlic does
not do well in compacted soil or in areas where water stands after rain. Once the cloves are in, water the bed well if
the soil is dry, then cover it with 2 to 4 in of loose organic mulch, such as clean straw or shredded leaves. What
happens during winter? Throughout the colder months, garlic concentrates on growing its underground root system. The
growth you can see may slow down dramatically, but that does not mean the plants are asleep. When spring arrives, the
leaves send up green shoots. Those leaves capture sunlight and fuel the growth of the developing bulbs. One common
mistake is planting garlic too early while the weather is still warm or giving it too much nitrogen fertilizer. Too much
leafy growth before winter can leave the plants more exposed to cold damage. For northern gardeners, the goal is to give
garlic enough time to establish roots without pushing it to produce too much top growth. Important tip. If your ground
is already frozen solid, do not try to force clothes into it. Plant them in suitable containers in a protected spot
instead or wait for the right spring planting window for your variety. Garlic will not give you a winter harvest, but
planting it before December can set you up for a rewarding harvest next summer. Number eight, spinach. Our next
vegetable is spinach, one of the most valuable leafy greens for cool season gardening. Spinach grows best in cool
weather. Unlike many warm season crops, it can handle cold conditions once it is established and some varieties can
survive a good deal of winter cold when given the right protection. Its compact size also makes it a great match for
raised beds, containers, and small backyard gardens. USDA hardiness zones. Roughly zones three through 11 for suitable
varieties and growing systems, but planting outdoors in late November is most practical in zones seven through 11.
Colder regions may need established plants, protected beds, or indoor growing. How to plant spinach? Spinach is usually
grown directly from seed. Choose a variety that is described as cold tolerant or suitable for winter growing.
Bloomsdale, Tyee, and Space are all popular options. Prepare a bed with loose, fertile soil that is rich in organic
matter. Sow the seeds about half an inch deep. Keep the rows 10 to 12 inches apart and thin the seedlings to 3 to 6
inches apart, depending on whether you want baby leaves or larger plants. Spinach like steady moisture, especially while
the seeds are germinating. Soil temperature matters a great deal here. Spinach seeds may germinate poorly if the soil is
too warm. Cooler soil is generally better, but extremely cold soil can slow germination as well. In November, gardeners
in warmer climates can often sow spinach straight into the garden. Gardeners in colder climates should focus on
established plants or protected growing spaces if the outdoor sowing window has already closed. Growing spinach through
winter. Once spinach has developed several leaves, it becomes far more tolerant of cold weather. Still, cold tolerance
is not the same as unlimited frost protection. In regions where temperatures regularly drop below freezing, a floating
row cover or a low tunnel can shield the plants from harsh winds and sudden temperature swings. Water when the soil
starts to dry out, but do not let the bed become waterlogged. Harvest the outer leaves and let the center of the plant
keep growing. This cut and come again method can give you several harvests from a single plant. Spinach also does well
in containers. If garden space is limited, pick a container with drainage holes and enough depth for healthy roots.
Spinach is especially useful for gardeners who want fresh greens without waiting for spring. Number seven, radishes. If
you want a vegetable that gives you results quickly, radishes deserve a spot in your garden. Radishes are among the
fastest growing vegetables you can raise at home. Depending on the variety and the conditions, some are ready to harvest
in about 25 to 35 days. That makes them especially appealing to gardeners in warmer regions who still have time to
finish a crop before winter turns USDA hardiness zones, roughly zones two through 10 as a crop, but planting outdoors in
November is most practical in zones seven through 11 depending on your local frost dates and the variety. In colder
regions, radishes planted this late may not have enough time to form mature roots before the soil freezes. How to plant
radishes. Radishes should be sown directly from seed. Choose a sunny location with loose, well-drained soil. Remove
stones and large clumps because obstacles can interfere with root development. Sow the seeds about half an inch deep.
Space the rows six to 12 inches apart and thin the seedlings to about two inches apart for many small-rooted varieties.
Crowding is one of the biggest reasons radishes end up small and misshapen. Keep the soil evenly moist for the whole
growing period. If it repeatedly dries out and then gets soaked, the roots can turn tough, split, or take on an
unpleasant texture. For late season planting, look for quick-maturing varieties such as Cherry Belle or French
Breakfast. Can radishes survive frost? Many radish varieties can handle a light frost, but severe and prolonged freezing
can damage both the leaves and the roots. In zones eight through 10, November offers a useful opportunity for another
round of radish planting. In zone seven, success depends on your local climate, the date of the first hard freeze, and
how much protection you can provide. Gardeners in zones three through six should generally avoid counting on newly
seeded outdoor radishes this late in the season unless they have a suitable protected growing space. A simple row cover
can help extend the season and a layer of mulch can shield the soil from rapid temperature changes. One last tip.
Harvest radishes when they reach their recommended size. If you leave them in the ground too long, the roots can turn
woody and overly spicy. For gardeners in mild winter regions, radishes are one of the easiest vegetables to add to a
November garden. Number six, leaf lettuce. Next on our list is leaf lettuce. Lettuce is a cool season crop that performs
best in relatively mild temperatures. It can supply fresh salad greens at a time when many traditional summer vegetables
have already stopped producing. One of its biggest advantages is the wide range of shapes, colors, and textures
available. You can grow green loose leaf lettuce, red leaf varieties, oak leaf lettuce, and compact types that work
especially well in containers. USDA hardiness zones, roughly zones four through 11, depending on the variety and the
protection you provide. Planting in late November is most practical in zones seven through 11. In colder areas, you may
be able to plant established seedlings under protection, but lettuce sown outdoors this late may grow too slowly to give
you a meaningful winter harvest. How to plant leaf lettuce? Lettuce can be started from seeds or from transplants. For a
late November garden, transplants are often the better choice because they already have a developed root system and
several leaves. Choose a spot that receives about four to six hours of sunlight during the cooler season. In regions
with mild winters, full sun can be a real benefit. Prepare the soil with compost and make sure excess water can drain
away. If you are planting seeds, sow them very shallowly. Lettuce seeds need light to germinate reliably, so do not bury
them deeply. Thin the seedlings according to the spacing recommended for your variety. For loose leaf lettuce, about six
to eight inches between plants is a good starting point. How to protect lettuce during winter? Lettuce tolerates cool
conditions, but prolonged freezing temperatures can damage exposed leaves. A floating row cover can add protection,
particularly in zones seven and eight. Raised beds are helpful because they warm up quickly on sunny winter days,
although they can also cool down faster at night. Whenever possible, water in the morning so the leaves and the
surrounding surfaces can dry before evening. Avoid heavy nitrogen fertilizer. Too much can encourage soft growth and
leave the plants more vulnerable to stress. Harvest the outer leaves instead of pulling up the whole plant. That way the
center keeps producing new leaves. If you live in zones 9 through 11, November is often a far better time to establish
lettuce than the heat of summer. Just remember that lettuce dislikes extreme heat as much as it dislikes severe cold.
Your goal is to provide steady moisture, suitable temperatures, and enough sunlight. Number five. Kale. Kale is one of
the most dependable vegetables for cold season gardening. Its leaves are packed with nutrients and many varieties keep
growing in conditions that would slow down or damage less hardy vegetables. Another interesting feature is that kale can
taste sweeter after it has been exposed to cool temperatures. That happens because some plants convert part of their
stored carbohydrates into sugars as the temperature drops, and this can change the flavor of the leaves. USDA hardiness
zones. Roughly zones 3 through 9 for many garden varieties with exact performance depending on the cultivar and the
winter conditions. For November planting, gardeners in zones 7 through 9 may still be able to establish kale
transplants. In zones 3 through 6, it is more realistic to protect plants that are already established than to expect
new seedlings to grow large before winter. How to plant kale. Kale can be grown from seeds or from transplants. For late
season planting, choose healthy transplants rather than sowing seeds outdoors in a region where a hard freeze is
approaching. War Borne, Red Russian, and Lacinato are all popular varieties. Select a sunny location with fertile, well-
drained soil. Dig a hole slightly larger than the transplant's root ball and set the plant at the same soil level it had
in its original container. Space the plants 12 to 18 inches apart depending on the variety. Water thoroughly after
transplanting so the roots can settle into the soil. If the soil needs more organic matter add compost before planting.
Winter care for kale. Even though kale is cold tolerant young transplants still need time to establish their roots. In
areas with frequent freezing temperatures protect young plants with a row cover or a low tunnel. Do not let the soil dry
out completely. At the same time avoid over watering during cold spells when evaporation is slow. Harvest the lower
outer leaves first. Never strip off too many leaves at once because the plant needs its central growing point to keep
producing new foliage. Kale is especially valuable in gardens where winter temperatures stay cool but the soil does not
remain frozen for long stretches. For gardeners in zones eight and nine planting in November can provide a productive
winter crop. And even in colder areas established kale plants can be a valuable part of a protected winter garden.
Number four. Carrots. The fourth vegetable on our list is carrots. Carrots are a favorite among home gardeners because
they fit into small spaces and deliver a satisfying harvest. They are also interesting because cool weather can improve
their flavor. As temperatures drop some carrot varieties build up more sugars which gives them a sweeter taste. However
carrots demand careful timing. They take much longer to mature than radishes and freshly sown seeds need the right soil
conditions to germinate. USDA hardiness zones. Roughly zones three through 10 as a crop but sowing outdoors in November
is generally most suitable in zones eight through ten and in some milder zone seven locations. In colder zones, November
is usually too late for a new outdoor carrot crop unless you have a heated or well-protected growing space. How to plant
carrots? Carrots must be grown from seed. Unlike garlic or onions, they are not normally transplanted because disturbing
the developing roots can lead to misshapen carrots. Choose loose soil with deep and no stones or compacted layers. For
standard long-rooted varieties, aim for 12 inches or more of suitable soil depth. Short-rooted varieties can be grown in
shallower beds. Sow the seeds about 1/4 inch deep and follow the spacing guidance on the seed packet. Carrot seeds are
tiny, so be careful not to plant them too deeply. Keep the soil consistently moist during germination. This is
especially important because carrot seeds can take one to three weeks to emerge, depending on the soil temperature and
conditions. Once the seedlings appear, thin them to about two to three inches apart for many varieties. Growing carrots
in late fall. For November planting, choose varieties with shorter maturity periods and good cool weather performance.
Baby carrots and short-rooted types may be more practical than varieties that need a long growing season. In zones eight
through ten, gardeners can often keep sowing carrots throughout November. In zone seven, planting may work in warmer
locations as long as there is enough time before the ground turns too cold. Mulch can help regulate soil temperature and
hold in moisture. In areas where the ground freezes, a thick protective layer may help preserve mature roots, but it
cannot guarantee that newly sown seeds will grow into a successful crop. Do not expect November sown carrots to mature
quickly. Shorter days and colder soil will slow their development. The main objective is to give them the best possible
growing conditions before winter becomes severe. Number three, turnips. Turnips are another excellent choice for cool
season gardens. They give you two useful harvests from a single plant, the edible roots and the leafy greens. Depending
on the variety, turnips can mature fairly quickly, which makes them a good option for gardeners who want to grow
something before the year ends. They also adapt well to different garden spaces and they can be grown in traditional
beds or in containers that are deep enough. USDA hardiness zones, roughly zones two through 10 as a crop with November
planting outdoors most practical in zones seven through 10. In colder areas, established turnips may survive some frost,
but sowing fresh seeds late in November can be risky. How to plant turnips? Turnips are grown directly from seed. Choose
a location that gets about six hours of sunlight or more. Prepare loose soil and remove any large stones that could
interfere with root growth. Sow the seeds about one quarter to one half inch deep. Space the rows 12 to 18 inches apart.
Once the seedlings emerge, thin them to about three to four inches apart for many small rooted varieties. If you want
larger roots, follow the wider spacing recommended for your chosen variety. Water regularly to keep the soil evenly
moist. Turnips grow best when they are not exposed to repeated cycles of severe drying followed by heavy watering. Why
turnips are useful in winter gardens? Compared with many other root crops, turnips grow quickly. Some varieties can
mature in about five to 18 weeks under favorable conditions. However, cool temperatures and shorter days can stretch
that timeline. In zones 8 through 10, November can still offer a reasonable planting window. In zone 7, choose early
varieties and consider row covers if temperatures are expected to fall. You can harvest turnip greens while the roots
keep developing. Take only a portion of the leaves so the plant has enough foliage left to support root growth. For
gardeners who want both nutritious greens and edible roots, turnips offer excellent value. Number two, garden peas. Now
let's look at a vegetable that many gardeners think of as a spring crop, but which can also be planted in late fall in
the right climates. Garden peas are cool season legumes that prefer relatively mild temperatures. They can grow well
through the cooler months in the warmer parts of the United States, especially when they are planted early enough to
establish themselves before harsh winter weather arrives. USDA hardiness zones roughly zones 3 through 11 as a seasonal
crop, but planting outdoors in November is mainly suited to zones 8 through 11 and some mild zone 7 locations. In colder
regions, spring is generally the more reliable planting season. How to plant garden peas? Peas are grown directly from
seed. There are several types to choose from. Shelling peas are grown for the peas inside the pod. Snow peas are
harvested for their flat, tender pods, and snap peas are picked when the pods are plump and crisp. Choose a sunny
location with well-drained soil. Plant the seeds about 1 to 2 in deep and follow the spacing recommendations for your
variety. Many garden pea varieties can be spaced about 2 in apart within the row. Give climbing varieties support with a
trellis or garden netting. Bush varieties need less support, but even they can benefit from a simple structure. Water
the soil after planting and keep the moisture consistent. Avoid heavy nitrogen fertilizer. Peas work with beneficial
soil bacteria to obtain their own nitrogen, so heavy feeding is usually unnecessary. Growing peas during winter. Peas do
not perform well in extreme heat, which is exactly why they are useful in cooler climates. However, young seedlings are
not immune to freezing temperatures. In zones 8 through 10, November can be an excellent time to establish peas. In zone
7, your local frost patterns and winter temperatures will decide whether late fall planting is practical. In colder
regions, a protected greenhouse or cold frame may offer another option. Do not plant peas in waterlogged soil. Their
roots need oxygen, and standing water increases the risk of root problems. One of the best reasons to plant peas in late
fall is that they can establish during mild weather and keep growing as long as conditions stay favorable. For gardeners
in mild winter regions, that can mean an earlier harvest than waiting for spring. Number one, fava beans. And finally,
our number one vegetable is the fava bean, also known as the broad bean. Fava beans are especially interesting because
they are among the legumes that tolerate cool growing conditions. Unlike many traditional garden beans, which need warm
soil and warm weather, fava beans are well adapted to the cooler seasons. In suitable regions, you can plant them in
autumn, let them establish roots through the cool months, and keep them growing toward a spring harvest. USDA hardiness
zones roughly zones 7 through 11 for practical autumn and winter growing, although the variety, local temperatures, and
protection all matter. Some varieties may survive colder conditions with suitable protection, but that should never be
treated as a guarantee. How to plant fava beans. Fava beans are grown directly from seed. Choose a spot with full sun or
partial sun in regions where winter conditions are mild. The soil should be fertile, loose, and well-drained. Plant the
seeds about 1 to 2 inches deep. Space the plants about 6 inches apart with 18 to 24 inches between rows depending on the
variety and its mature size. If your garden is small, compact varieties can be a practical choice. Water the soil after
planting and keep the moisture consistent while the plants establish. Fava beans develop substantial roots and can grow
into fairly large plants, so leave enough room for good air circulation. Why plant fava beans before December? Fava
beans can establish during the cooler months without needing the high temperatures that warm season beans prefer. Their
roots also partner with beneficial nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which helps them draw nitrogen from the air. That makes
them useful for crop rotation and for planning your vegetable garden. In zones 8 through 10, November is often a
suitable time for autumn selling. In zone 7, success depends on your local winter temperatures and the protection you
can provide. Gardeners in colder regions should generally wait for spring or use a protected growing environment. As the
plants develop, keep an eye out for aphids and other common garden pests. Provide support if the plants grow tall or
become heavy with developing pods. Harvest the pods when the beans reach the size you want. Younger beans are generally
more tender, while mature beans may need extra preparation before eating. Fava beans are an excellent choice for
gardeners who want to make productive use of the cool season and prepare for a spring harvest. What should you plant in
your USDA zone? Now that we have covered all nine vegetables, let's make your planting decisions easier. Keep in mind
that USDA hardiness zones are based on the average annual extreme minimum winter temperature. They do not tell you
exactly when your first frost will arrive or how quickly your soil will freeze. That is why your local climate and your
remaining growing season matter just as much as your zone. Zones three and four. For gardeners and zones three and four,
outdoor planting opportunities in November are very limited. You may still be able to plant garlic if the soil is
workable and has not frozen, although in many locations the ideal planting window has probably passed. Established
spinach, kale, and other cold-tolerant vegetables may survive with suitable protection. For any new vegetable crops,
consider indoor growing systems or simply wait until spring. Zones five and six. Gardeners in zones five and six should
focus on garlic as long as local conditions still allow planting. Established kale and spinach may keep growing with
protection, but newly planted radishes, carrots, lettuce, and other short-season vegetables may not have enough time to
mature outdoors. A cold frame or low tunnel can extend your growing season, but it cannot fully replace the time that
plants need to develop. Zone seven. Zone seven offers more opportunities, especially in warmer areas and in spots with
favorable microclimates. Garlic is a strong option. Depending on your local frost dates, you may also be able to plant
spinach, radishes, lettuce, kale transplants, and turnips. Use row covers to protect vulnerable crops and choose early-
maturing varieties. Zones eight and nine. These zones offer some of the best opportunities for planting vegetables in
late November. You can consider garlic, spinach, radishes, lettuce, kale, carrots, turnips, peas, and fava beans.
However, the exact planting dates vary between coastal, inland, and southern locations. Check your local extension
planting calendar and choose varieties that suit your region. Zones 10 and 11. In the warmest regions, November can be
an excellent time to grow a wide selection of cool-season vegetables. Spinach, lettuce, radishes, carrots, turnips,
peas, and fava beans can all be useful choices depending on your local growing conditions. You can grow garlic as well,
but it is especially important to select a variety adapted to mild winters. Remember that even warm zones can see
unexpected cold snaps. Watch the forecast and protect sensitive seedlings when necessary. Winter gardening tips. Before
you start planting, there are five important things to remember. First, check your soil condition. Cold, wet, compacted
soil can keep seeds from germinating properly and make root development difficult. Work the soil only when it is
suitable for planting. Second, choose the right varieties. Not every vegetable variety performs equally well in winter.
Look for descriptions such as cold tolerant, winter hardy, early maturing, or suitable for autumn planting. Third,
protect your plants. Floating row covers, low tunnels, cold frames, and organic mulch can all help extend the growing
season. However, keep in mind that levels of protection vary and lightweight covers are no substitute for proper
insulation during a severe freeze. Fourth, be careful with watering. Plants still need moisture in winter, but they use
less of it when temperatures are low and daylight is limited. Check the soil before you water and avoid keeping the
roots constantly wet. Fifth, don't ignore sunlight. Winter days are shorter, which means vegetables grow more slowly.
Choose the sunniest spot available, especially for crops such as carrots, peas, and other root vegetables. Most
importantly, do not assume that every vegetable on this list can be planted successfully in every USDA zone before
December. Use your local first frost date, your soil conditions, the instructions on the seed packet, and your
cooperative extension recommendations to make the final decision. So, even though December is just around the corner,
your vegetable garden does not have to come to an end. From garlic and spinach to radishes, kale, carrots, turnips,
peas, and fava beans, there are still plenty of chances to grow food during the colder months, especially if you live in
a region with mild winters. The key is knowing what grows well in your USDA zone and choosing the right planting method
at the right time. Now, tell us in the comments, which USDA hardiness zone do you garden in? And which of these nine
