If you have ever lost a pot of vegetables over winter, the cold probably wasn't what killed it. The pot was. A plant in
the ground has the whole earth wrapped around its roots. On a brutal winter night, the soil a few inches down can be 20°
warmer than the air above it. A plant in a container has about an inch of plastic or clay between its roots and that
same freezing air. That's why gardeners talk about the twozone rule. A pot behaves like it's two hardiness zones colder
than the ground it's sitting on. That doesn't mean you can't grow food through winter on a balcony or a patio step. It
just means you have to pick the right vegetables and give each one the right little trick. That's what this video is. 15
vegetables that genuinely survive winter in containers and the one thing each of them needs from wrapping the pot to
knowing when not to pick a leaf. And number 15 grows in shade. So even the darkest, coldest balcony in the building has
something that will grow. Let's start with the one everybody asks about first. Number one, kale. Kale goes in the first
pot, and the reason is simple. It's tough. Hardy types like winterbore and vates handle temperatures down around 5° F,
and Siberian kale goes colder still to roughly zero. Red Russian and linenado are hardy, too, and plenty of people think
they taste best. One thing before we go further, every temperature I give you in this video is for a plant growing in
the ground. In a pot, give yourself a margin. So, protect the roots. Use a pot of at least 5 gallons because more soil
around the roots means more insulation. Wrap the outside with bubble wrap or burlap. Spread a few inches of straw or
shredded leaves over the soil and push the pot up against the wall of your house with other pots clustered around it.
Take the saucer off for winter, too. Because water sitting in a saucer freezes solid around the pot. Here's why kale is
worth the effort. As the temperature drops, the leaves pile up sugars. Those sugars work like antifreeze inside the
plant and they also make the leaves sweeter. That's why kale after a frost tastes better than kale in September. If the
leaves turn a little purple in the cold, don't worry. That's just the plant reacting to the weather. One rule, never
pick frozen leaves. Frozen leaves bruise and turn to mush when you handle them. wait until midday when they've thought,
then pick from the bottom up and leave the growing center alone. And if you're watching from Canada or the UK, the pot
rule works exactly the same way for you. Think of your pots as living two zones colder than the ground and plan around
that. Number two, garlic. Garlic does something no other vegetable on this list does. It wants the winter. A clove
planted in fall grows roots before the soil freezes and then the cold itself tells the plant to form a bulb. Skip the
cold and you can end up with one big clove instead of a bulb with separate cloves. So, there's nothing to protect garlic
from here. The job is to leave it alone. If you're reading this in early November and the soil in your pot isn't frozen,
you still have time in most places. Use a pot at least 10 in deep. Push the cloves in about 2 in pointed end up and
space them four to 6 in apart. In a 12-in pot, that's four or five cloves. Small space, real harvest. Two details matter
in a pot. First, the material. Terracotta and glazed ceramic crack when the water in the clay freezes and thaws. So,
choose plastic, wood, fabric, or thick resin. Second, drainage. Raise the pot on pot feet or a couple of bricks so water
never sits at the bottom and freezes around the cloves. Then, add three or 4 in of straw or shredded leaves on top. That
mulch matters more than anything else you do. Now, the moment that worries people. Green shoots poking up in December.
That's normal. Garlic tops handle freezing weather. Leave them and add more mulch if they're exposed. If you garden
somewhere warm, like zones 9 and 10, garlic may not get enough cold on its own. The fix is to chill your clothes in the
refrigerator for 4 to 6 weeks before planting. In cold zones, choose hard neck types like rombo, purple stripe, or
porcelain. In warm zones, soft neck does better. Harvest in early to midsummer when the lower leaves turn brown. Number
three, spinach. Spinach in a winter container will scare you. After a hard freeze, you'll walk outside and the leaves
will be limp and flat against the soil, looking completely dead. Don't touch it. As the day warms up and the leaves
thaw, they stand back up. Spinach has the same built-in antifreeze as kale, and the tough varieties handle temperatures
near zero in the ground. Look for winter blooms, Dale, Thai, or giant winter. Here's something most container guides
never tell you. In winter, growth slows to a crawl. Depending on how far north you live, from late fall until late
winter, the days are shorter than about 10 hours, and plants barely grow. They hold what they have. So, the plan is to
get the plant to a decent size before the deepest cold. Then harvest slowly, a few outer leaves at a time. Think of it
as a savings account, not a paycheck. And if you haven't sewn yet, sew now anyway. Tiny seedlings sit through winter and
take off the moment the days lengthen. Light isn't a big problem. Leafy greens manage on 3 or 4 hours of winter sun,
which is far less than a tomato would ever need. Water is the real danger. In winter pots, the killer is often drying
out, not freezing. Cold, dry wind pulls moisture out of containers. Check the soil every week or so. Water in the
morning on a day above freezing, just enough to moisten the soil so any extra has time to drain before night. Use a pot
8 to 10 in deep and on nights that drop into the low teens, drape frost cloth over the top. Number four, carrots.
Carrots are the original winter pantry. Instead of digging them all at once, you leave them in the soil and pull what
you need when you need it. The tops handle temperatures down to roughly 18 or 20°, and the roots underneath are more
protected than the tops. The cold helps, too. Carrots turn starch into sugar in cold soil. So, a carrot pulled in
January tastes sweeter than one pulled in August. Container rules for carrots are strict. The pot has to be at least 12
in deep because a carrot that hits the bottom bends or forks, and you need short varieties. Nance, danvers, and chant
types finish at 5 to 7 in. The long imperator types simply won't fit. The winter trick is mulch. Once frost knocks the
tops back, cover the soil with four to 6 in of straw or shredded leaves. That keeps the soil from freezing into a solid
brick, which makes harvesting impossible, and it protects the top of each root. Now, an honest note about timing.
Carrots need about 70 to 80 days, and they grow slowly in the cold. If you sewed in early fall, you'll have full-size
carrots to store in the pot. If you're sewing in November in a cold zone, you'll mostly get baby carrots, which are
still sweet and still worth growing. In zones 8 and warmer, November sewing works fine. If you're in zone five or
colder, and the pot might freeze solid, move it into an unheated garage or shed. It stays cold, but out of the worst
wind and temperature swings. Water it lightly now and then. Pull one, rinse it, eat it. Fresh carrots in January. Number
five, lettuce. Lettuce sounds out of place on a winter list, and ordinary lettuce is. Standard varieties collapse in a
hard freeze, but a group of winter lettucees like winter density, rouge de, north pole, and merllo handle temperatures

plastic storage tote upside down over the pot and you've built a mini cold frame. Two rules. Prop one edge open on sunny

faster than frost kills them. Number six, Swiss chard. Swiss chard is the prettiest thing you can put in a winter

And it's tougher than it looks. Chard handles temperatures into the teens, and in a mild winter, it just keeps going.

Green onions, which the UK calls spring onions, are the best winter container vegetable for tiny spaces. They need just

Number eight, arugula. Arugula, which the UK calls rocket, doesn't win in winter by being the toughest. It wins by being
