## TTS SCRIPT

Honey. Cinnamon. Aloe. Willow water. Every gardener's heard at least one of these tricks for rooting cuttings faster — but real research says only some of them actually work. And the one almost everybody reaches for first? It might be the weakest of all four.

Turns out, researchers actually put these four to the test — head to head.

Here's the thing — you don't have to guess anymore. Four home remedies: aloe, honey, willow water, cinnamon. All lined up against a real commercial rooting hormone. Not opinions — actual university studies. By the end, you'll know exactly which one to trust, and which one to skip.

This isn't a coin flip — the data settles it. And the one everyone swears by? It might not make the final cut.

Let's set the baseline first, so the numbers ahead actually mean something. Nurseries use a synthetic rooting hormone called IBA — short for indole-three-butyric acid. It's the gold standard nearly every trial measures against. Everything else on this list gets compared to it.

Here's a fun bit of history most folks don't know. Back in nineteen thirty-five, two scientists at the Boyce Thompson Institute in New York went hunting. They wanted something to root cuttings faster. They tried everything from aspirin to nicotine before landing on IBA. Merck bought the rights and sold it as Hormodin. That's the same chemistry inside most rooting powders on garden center shelves today. That's the baseline. Now let's see who actually comes close.

One quick safety note before we get into it. Keep curious cats or dogs away from the tray while you're testing any of this. Keep the powder container out of reach of grandkids, too. Don't let little ones handle a treated cutting until it's fully dry. Food-grade honey and cinnamon are fine around the house. But store-bought rooting hormone powder should stay in its original container. Keep it out of pet and child reach, same as any garden chemical.

Here's the first home remedy that genuinely holds its own.

Aloe vera is the one most gardeners already trust — it's sitting right there on the windowsill. And here's the good news: the research actually backs it up. A university study in Sri Lanka tested aloe vera gel against commercial rooting hormone on citrus cuttings. Dip the cut end in fresh gel for five minutes. The root growth was statistically no different from the hormone. Both beat plain water by a wide margin — that's reassuring if you've relied on this trick for years. A separate study out of Bangladesh found something similar on grapevine cuttings. Aloe came close enough to the hormone that researchers often cite it as a legitimate alternative. Here's why this works: aloe gel contains natural plant hormones, auxins and gibberellins. They mimic what the commercial product does chemically. But — and this matters — not every plant needs the help. That same Sri Lankan study tested aloe on coleus, a soft, easy-rooting plant. It found zero difference between aloe, hormone, or nothing at all. Coleus just roots on its own, no fancy chemistry needed. So here's the real lesson, species by species: for woodier, harder cuttings, aloe genuinely competes with store-bought hormone. For soft stems that root themselves anyway, you're wasting a good aloe leaf.

So aloe earns its spot. But the kitchen has another contender — one that actually beat the hormone outright in one trial.

My grandmother swore by a spoonful of honey for rooting cuttings. That was long before anyone ran a university trial on it. Turns out, plain honey diluted in warm water has real research behind it too. The University of Hawaii's Master Gardener Program tested honey against commercial hormone on five different plants. On peanut cuttings, honey rooted ninety-two percent. Compare that to seventy-eight percent for the hormone, and just forty percent with no treatment. Honey actually beat the commercial product outright — that's a fun surprise for something from the pantry. On an easy grower like hemigraphis, honey nearly matched untreated cuttings. That plant barely needs help rooting in the first place. Here's the catch, and it matters. On Hibiscus clayii, a woody native Hawaiian shrub that's genuinely hard to root, honey only managed eighteen percent survival. The hormone hit forty-four percent. The researchers were blunt about it: honey worked, but it wasn't as reliable once the cutting turned woody and stubborn. Nearly every trial agrees on this point. Natural remedies can rival lab-made hormone on easy, soft cuttings. But none of them reliably beat it once the wood gets hard. So, soft, fast-rooting cuttings? Honey competes, sometimes wins. Tough, woody cuttings? Reach for the real hormone instead.

Which brings us to the one you've probably tried yourself. Snipping a willow branch and soaking it in a jar of water.

Here's where it gets interesting. Three separate university studies — in Jordan, Australia, and Iraq — actually tested willow extract on cuttings. And the results? A mixed bag. In Jordan, researchers found willow bark extract, soaked for two full days, boosted rooting on olive cuttings. That's close to what the commercial hormone achieved. But — and this is the important part — extract from willow leaves, shoots, or wood did nothing at all. Only the bark worked. In Australia, willow bark extract helped chrysanthemum and lavender cuttings. But only at one very specific, measured concentration. Too weak or too strong, and the benefit disappeared. In Iraq, researchers testing willow on a shrub called bottlebrush found something surprising. It didn't even increase root count compared to plain water. Put all three studies together, and one principle emerges: willow water is real, but weak. It only works when it comes from bark, at a controlled strength — not a folk-remedy overnight soak. So willow water isn't a total myth. It's close but no cigar most of the time, fussy about exactly how you use it. It only works from the bark, at a precise strength, soaked for days. That's not the quick jar-on-the-windowsill method most of us have tried.

And now, the one almost everybody reaches for first.

Cinnamon shows up in more rooting videos and blog posts than honey, aloe, and willow water combined. And here's the twist: it has the weakest science behind it of all four. We looked hard for a real university trial on the popular method. That's dusting dry cinnamon powder straight onto a fresh cut. We couldn't find one. Not a single peer-reviewed study has measured how dry cinnamon actually works on a rooting cutting. What we did find was a study that tested cinnamon differently. Cuttings soaked directly in a liquid extract. And honestly, the results weren't good. On bottlebrush cuttings, the cinnamon soak produced the lowest rooting rate of twelve treatments tested. Worse than plain water, worse than doing nothing at all. None of those cuttings survived being transplanted afterward. Now, that's a soak, not a dusting — not a perfect match for how most people use cinnamon. But it's the only hard data we could find, and it points the wrong way entirely. So here's the honest verdict: cinnamon might have some antifungal properties on a fresh cut. That idea isn't crazy on its face. But the specific numbers floating around online for cinnamon's rooting success? We traced them through three separate sources. We couldn't confirm a single one against the original research. The most popular trick on this list is also the one with the least evidence behind it.

So where does that leave you, standing at the kitchen counter with four options and one cutting to root?

Here's the order that actually holds up. Reach for aloe vera first — five minutes in fresh gel, and it competes with real hormone on almost anything. Honey comes second. Two tablespoons in two cups of cooled water works great on soft, easy cuttings. It falls behind on woody ones though. Willow water earns a cautious third place. Only if you're soaking actual bark, not just twigs. And only at a controlled strength. And cinnamon? Save it for banana bread. There's simply no solid research backing the popular dusting trick. And the one real study we found on cinnamon extract? It came back worse than doing nothing.
