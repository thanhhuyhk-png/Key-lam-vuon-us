## Question 1: How long do crushed eggshells take to break down in soil, and is their calcium actually plant-available in time to help a tomato crop this season?

**Findings:**

- Eggshells are composed primarily of calcium carbonate (CaCO3), which is insoluble and NOT directly available for plant uptake unless ground to a very fine powder or dissolved in acidic solution. Hand-crushed coarse fragments performed "not much better than nothing at all" in terms of calcium delivery. (Illinois Extension / Christopher Enroth, 2018) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2018-03-28-using-eggshells-garden-and-compost

- Whole or coarsely crushed eggshells in garden soil take at least 1 year to break down; whole halved shells can take 2 years or more — meaning calcium from shells added this spring will NOT be plant-available this tomato season. (Illinois Extension, 2018) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2018-03-28-using-eggshells-garden-and-compost

- Finely ground eggshell powder can match the performance of pure calcium hydroxide and exceed agricultural lime in a controlled test. Particle size is the critical variable: the finer the grind, the faster calcium dissolves into soil solution. (Illinois Extension / Christopher Enroth, citing Mitchell 2005 / Alabama Cooperative Extension, 2018) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2018-03-28-using-eggshells-garden-and-compost

- NC State Cooperative Extension (Chowan County) confirms: "Adding eggshells will not make calcium available to plants in a timely enough fashion" to address deficiency within a growing season. The calcium in eggshell carbonate remains locked until the shell physically breaks down. (NC State Extension / Chowan County Center, n.d.) — https://chowan.ces.ncsu.edu/news/gardening-mythbusters-eggshells-for-calcium/

- UC Marin Master Gardeners (University of California Cooperative Extension) state: "Unless the eggshells are ground into a fine powder and are put into an acidic solution, the calcium stays intact" — archaeological digs show eggshells can survive in soil for hundreds of years without fully dissolving. (UC Cooperative Extension / Marin Master Gardeners, n.d.) — https://ucanr.edu/sites/MarinMG/BASICS/GARDEN_MYTHS_BUSTED

- Iowa State University soil fertility lab document (Brent Pringnitz, 2006) analyzed eggshell composition and its potential as a liming source; metadata confirms authorship and year. Full text was in PDF binary format and specific neutralizing-value numbers could not be extracted — see NEED DATA below. (Iowa State University Extension, 2006) — https://www.agronext.iastate.edu/soilfertility/info/eggshell-lime.pdf

**Bottom line for script:** Eggshells crushed by hand are essentially useless for this season. To get any calcium from them within weeks rather than years, grind to talcum-powder fineness OR use a proven lime/gypsum amendment instead.

## Question 2: What actually causes blossom end rot in tomatoes — a calcium shortage in the soil, or a calcium-uptake/watering problem?

**Findings:**

- Blossom end rot (BER) is a physiological disorder caused by insufficient calcium in developing fruit tissue, not necessarily by low calcium in the soil. "Calcium is only moved into the plant with an ample moisture supply" — drought or irregular watering cuts off the delivery pipeline even when soil calcium is adequate. (Michigan State University Extension, 2016) — https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/blossom_end_rot_tip_sheet

- Calcium is immobile in the plant: it travels only upward through the xylem via water movement (transpiration) and CANNOT redistribute from leaves to developing fruit. Rapid early-season vegetative growth diverts calcium to leaves, leaving fruit starved — again a water-management and growth-rate issue, not a soil-level shortage. (UConn Home Garden Education Center / Nora Doonan, UConn Soil Science, June 2025) — https://homegarden.cahnr.uconn.edu/2025/06/15/blossom-end-rot/

- MSU Extension recommends: at least 1 inch of water per week during fruit development, delivered by soaker hose (not overhead); organic mulch (straw, grass clippings, wood chips) to conserve soil moisture and prevent the wet-dry cycles that starve fruit of calcium. Maintain soil pH 6.5–7.5 and use low-nitrogen fertilizer formulas (e.g., 4-12-4). (Michigan State University Extension, 2016) — https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/blossom_end_rot_tip_sheet

- UConn Extension confirms plants need 1–2 inches of water per week; soil should never dry completely between waterings, but overwatering (waterlogged) reduces root oxygen and also impairs calcium uptake. Apply organic mulch (wood chips or straw) after soil warms. (UConn Home Garden Education / Nora Doonan, June 2025) — https://homegarden.cahnr.uconn.edu/2025/06/15/blossom-end-rot/

- Competing ions worsen calcium uptake: "excessive fertilization with ammonium, potassium, and magnesium creates competition for calcium absorption at the roots." High-nitrogen ammonium fertilizers accelerate vegetative growth that diverts calcium from fruit. (UConn Extension, 2025) — https://homegarden.cahnr.uconn.edu/2025/06/15/blossom-end-rot/

- Mississippi State University Extension confirms BER is a calcium deficiency caused by uneven water availability: "Blossom end rot is a calcium deficiency, but that deficiency is normally caused by uneven water availability to the plant." Eggshells are not the answer; consistent watering and soil testing are. (Mississippi State Extension / Susan Collins-Smith, n.d.) — https://extension.msstate.edu/blog/can-eggshells-prevent-blossom-end-rot

- University of Nebraska–Lincoln Extension: "Apply mulch around plants to help retain soil moisture and reduce fluctuations." "Regular, deep watering helps ensure that plants can take up calcium effectively." (UNL Extension / Backyard Farmer, n.d.) — https://byf.unl.edu/blossom-end-rot/

**Feynman-level explanation for viewer 60–80:** BER is a plumbing problem, not a supply problem. The soil usually has plenty of calcium — the plant just can't pump it to the fruit when the water supply goes up and down. Fix the water, fix the rot.

## Question 3: Is there any scientific mechanism by which baking soda near tomato roots makes fruit sweeter? And what harm can the sodium do to soil?

**Findings:**

- There is NO verified scientific mechanism by which sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) causes tomatoes to taste sweeter. The popular claim is that alkaline baking soda raises soil pH, and "less acidic soil means less acid fruit" — this logic is scientifically incorrect. Fruit flavor chemistry (sugar-to-acid ratio / Brix) is governed by variety genetics, sunlight hours, potassium availability, and consistent water management — not by soil sodium or minor shifts in pH. (No .edu source located that specifically debunks this mechanism with a named author — see NEED DATA below. Closest .edu statement: Illinois Extension notes that "the sweetness of a tomato is pre-determined by the variety of tomato you plant." Christopher Enroth, Illinois Extension, May 2025) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2015-05-20-busting-garden-myths

- [YMYL] Sodium accumulates in soil with repeated baking soda applications. UC Cooperative Extension / Marin Master Gardeners states: "you're adding sodium bicarbonate, a salt, to your plants and soil. Since repeat applications are necessary, this can cause burning of the foliage AND it can add salt to the soil, which is not something you want." Sodium disperses clay particles, damages soil structure, impairs drainage, and can cause plant stunting and dark-colored foliage over time. (UC Cooperative Extension / Marin Master Gardeners, n.d.) — https://ucanr.edu/sites/MarinMG/BASICS/GARDEN_MYTHS_BUSTED

- WSU (Washington State University) researcher Dr. Linda Chalker-Scott compiled a bibliography on baking soda's documented horticultural effects. The literature shows baking soda functions through antimicrobial/antifungal mechanisms only — zero studies demonstrate any pathway from sodium bicarbonate to increased fruit sugar accumulation. (WSU / Dr. Linda Chalker-Scott, n.d.) — https://puyallup.wsu.edu/lcs/reference-baking-soda/

**Script note:** This is the strongest myth to bust — the "sweeter tomatoes" claim circulates widely on social media. No .edu source names a specific mechanism; they only note absence of evidence or point to variety/sun as the real sugar drivers.

## Question 4: Does baking soda work as a fungicide on powdery mildew — fungistatic or fungicidal, at what concentration, and what is the extension-recommended recipe (tbsp per gallon) plus the leaf-burn threshold?

**Findings:**

- Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) is **fungistatic** — it inhibits fungal spore germination and growth but does NOT kill existing fungal colonies. It is not fungicidal. Multiple extension sources confirm it prevents spread rather than curing established infection. (UVM Horticultural Myths article, referenced in WSU literature review — source page returned 403; confirmed from WSU baking-soda PDF search result summary) — https://puyallup.wsu.edu/lcs/reference-baking-soda/

- Lab and greenhouse studies show 0.5%–2% sodium bicarbonate concentrations "reduce germination and growth of several mildew species." Weak solutions of 0.5%–1.0% used in greenhouses "without causing significant phytotoxicity." Above 2%, leaf damage (chlorosis, burn) risk rises. (Colorado State University Extension, 1999, reviewed August 2025) — https://extension.colostate.edu/topic-areas/yard-garden/powdery-mildews-2-902/

- Baking soda ALONE is largely ineffective outdoors: "the application of sodium bicarbonate without oil failed to control powdery mildew" — the oil acts as a sticker/spreader and also has independent antifungal action. The combination outperforms either alone. (Colorado State University Extension, 1999) — https://extension.colostate.edu/topic-areas/yard-garden/powdery-mildews-2-902/

- **Wisconsin Horticulture Extension recipe** (Tier 1, revised March 2024):
  1.5 tablespoons baking soda + 3 tablespoons lightweight paraffin-based horticultural oil per 1 gallon of water.
  Apply every 7–14 days from plant emergence until humid conditions end. Pretest on a few leaves before treating entire plants, especially in warm weather. (Wisconsin Horticulture Extension, revised March 1, 2024) — https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/powdery-mildew-vegetables/

- **Cornell/Virginia Tech-documented recipe** (original Cornell University research, documented in JARS / Virginia Tech Libraries, 2009):
  1 heaping tablespoon baking soda + 2 tablespoons horticultural oil + 1 tablespoon mild liquid dish soap per 1 gallon of water.
  Apply late evening when sun is off foliage; spray to runoff on both leaf surfaces. (Frances Burns, documented in Journal of the American Rhododendron Society / Virginia Tech Scholarly Communications, 2009) — https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/JARS/v63n4/v63n4-burns1.htm

- **Phytotoxicity threshold:** The Horticultural Myths UVM page (returned 403 on fetch, but confirmed via WSU search summary) states baking soda "very easily could cause leaf damage if used at a higher concentration." UConn notes "potential for phytotoxicity when using vegetable oils or dishwashing detergent." Wisconsin advises pretesting on small number of leaves before full application, especially in warm weather. Specific % burn threshold number not confirmed in fetched text — see NEED DATA.

- Maryland researchers found horticultural oil alone was not significantly different from the oil-plus-baking-soda combination on lilac powdery mildew — suggesting oil may be doing most of the work. (UConn / Home and Garden Education Center, revised 2016) — https://homegarden.cahnr.uconn.edu/factsheets/powdery-mildew-on-ornamentals/

## Question 5: Does Epsom salt produce bigger/healthier tomatoes, or only help when soil is magnesium-deficient — and can excess magnesium worsen blossom end rot by competing with calcium uptake?

**Findings:**

- Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate, MgSO4) contains magnesium and sulfur — NO calcium. It does NOT prevent or treat blossom end rot. (NDSU Extension / North Dakota State University, n.d.) — https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/extension-topics/gardening-and-horticulture/vegetables/epsom-salt-myth

- Epsom salt is beneficial ONLY if soil is genuinely magnesium-deficient — a condition confirmed by soil test. Most garden soils, especially those regularly amended with organic matter, already contain sufficient magnesium. Applying Epsom salt to adequate-magnesium soils provides no benefit. (Clemson University Home & Garden Information Center, March 2025) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/epsom-salt-in-the-garden-is-it-truly-needed/

- [YMYL] Excess magnesium from Epsom salt WORSENS blossom end rot: "Adding Epsom salt to the soil may create more rot since magnesium and calcium ions compete for uptake into the plant, and the more magnesium in the soil, the less chance that calcium will be absorbed." (NDSU Extension, n.d.) — https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/extension-topics/gardening-and-horticulture/vegetables/epsom-salt-myth

- Clemson University Extension confirms the antagonism: "excess magnesium actively inhibits calcium absorption," directly worsening BER in tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant. (Clemson HGIC, March 2025) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/epsom-salt-in-the-garden-is-it-truly-needed/

- Excessive Epsom salt also creates saline conditions in soil: magnesium sulfate salt accumulation blocks water uptake, producing "dark-colored vegetation and stunted overall growth." (Clemson HGIC, March 2025) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/epsom-salt-in-the-garden-is-it-truly-needed/

- UMN Extension also debunks Epsom salt as a home remedy: it appears in their list of soil health remedies that lack scientific support for most garden soils. (UMN Extension — page returned 403 on fetch; confirmed present in search results) — https://extension.umn.edu/manage-soil-nutrients/coffee-grounds-eggshells-epsom-salts

- NDSU notes that most North Dakota (and broadly, US) soils already contain adequate calcium naturally; adding Epsom salt is doubly counterproductive — it supplies no calcium while blocking the calcium that is there. (NDSU Extension, n.d.) — https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/extension-topics/gardening-and-horticulture/vegetables/epsom-salt-myth

- Calcium nitrate spray (4 tablespoons per gallon), applied two to three times weekly to dime-sized or smaller tomato fruit, is an NDSU-recommended intervention for active BER — far more effective than Epsom salt. (NDSU Extension, n.d.) — https://www.ag.ndsu.edu/news/columns/dakota-gardener/dakota-gardener-preventing-rotten-tomatoes-in-a-drought

## YMYL Flags

- [YMYL] Baking soda sodium accumulation in soil: repeated applications harm soil structure and root function. Script should include: "If you're already using baking soda spray on your vegetables, consider a soil test to check sodium and pH before continuing all season." — consult local extension office for soil test recommendations.

- [YMYL] Epsom salt worsening blossom end rot: this is a counter-intuitive harm — a product sold in pharmacies for therapeutic use can actively damage tomato crops and worsen the exact condition gardeners are trying to fix. Script must be explicit: "Adding Epsom salt to fix blossom end rot can actually make it worse." No medical/human health risk here, but strong CAUTION warranted for garden health.

- [YMYL] Calcium nitrate spray (NDSU recommendation, 4 tbsp per gallon): this is a fertilizer-grade product — script should note "follow label directions" and "apply to fruit, not soil." Not a human-health concern but require accurate dilution guidance.
