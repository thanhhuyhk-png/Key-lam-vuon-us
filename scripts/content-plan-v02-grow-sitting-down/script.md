## TTS SCRIPT

If getting down on your knees isn't something your body lets you do anymore — your back, your hips, or maybe you're in a chair now — I want you to hear this. You can still grow your own food. Seven plants, every single one from a seat. No kneeling. No bending. We'll start with a head of lettuce you'll be cutting in about a month. And the easiest one to harvest? That's number six. Stay with me, because it's worth the wait.

Here's the thing — these seven aren't fancy. Lettuce. Radishes. Swiss chard. A dwarf tomato. Bush beans. Strawberries. And a little pot of herbs by your chair. Every one grows shallow, stays low, and asks nothing of your knees. There's also one measurement, right in the middle, that makes or breaks the whole thing — and most folks get it wrong. We'll get to it. But let's start picking.

Number one is leaf lettuce, and it's the perfect place to begin. Lettuce has shallow roots. It only needs about four to six inches of soil. That means it grows happily in a shallow box or pot, right at your seated height. Here's why this works so well for us. Lettuce is what gardeners call cut-and-come-again. You don't pull up the whole plant. You snip the outer leaves, leave the little center alone, and the plant keeps making more. Cut it when the leaves are six to ten inches tall, and in about two to three weeks there's a fresh batch waiting. One small pot can feed you salads for weeks, and you never once have to bend over. That's it. Leaf lettuce can be ready to eat in as little as forty days from seed.

Number two, radishes — and these are for anyone who wants a quick win. Radishes are the fastest food on this whole list. From the day you drop the seed, you're pulling crisp radishes in twenty-five to thirty-five days. They have the same shallow roots as lettuce, so four to six inches of soil is plenty. When the red shoulder pokes up about an inch wide, it's ready. And harvesting couldn't be gentler. You pinch the leaves and pull. No digging. No tools. Sow a few seeds every couple of weeks, and you'll have radishes coming all season long.

Number three is Swiss chard, and this one is a quiet workhorse. Chard gives you greens from spring all the way to the first frost. It's another cut-and-come-again plant, so it earns its spot at your seat. Here's the trick. Cut the outer leaves about two inches above the crown — that's the little base where new leaves are born. Leave that crown alone, and the plant just keeps sending up fresh, tender leaves. Don't cut too deep into the center. That's the one mistake that stops it cold. A two-gallon pot is all chard needs, and it'll feed you for months.

Now — remember that measurement I promised you? Here it is. This is the part most folks build backwards, so listen close. The height of your garden is the single thing that decides whether you can do this sitting down. Here's the rule of thumb: you want the soil about waist-high while you're seated — roughly two to two and a half feet up. That's it. If you use a wheelchair, raise it a touch and leave an open gap underneath, so your chair rolls right in. Getting that height right is non-negotiable. A raised table garden — what University of Maryland Extension calls a salad table — already sits at about the right height, with legs you can adjust to fit you. One more thing: keep the bed narrow enough to reach the middle without stretching. I'll put the exact inch-by-inch numbers down in the description, so you don't have to remember a thing. Get the height right, and all seven of these plants come to you.

One quick word on watering before we keep going — because raised beds and pots dry out fast up off the ground. The trick is to let the water take care of itself, and I'll show you the easiest way when we reach number six, the thirstiest plant on the list. For now, just know this: you won't be hauling a heavy can around every day.

Alright — back to the plants. Number four is a dwarf cherry tomato. And yes, you really can grow tomatoes from a chair. The secret is choosing a dwarf variety. A regular tomato climbs five or six feet and needs heavy staking. A dwarf stays between two and four and a half feet tall — small enough to sit beside you in a five-gallon pot. Varieties like Tiny Tim give you ripe cherry tomatoes in about forty-five days. You'll want a small stake when the fruit gets heavy, but nothing you can't reach — the tomatoes hang right at hand level, ready to pick.

Number five, bush beans — not pole beans. And that difference matters a lot for us. Pole beans climb tall and need a trellis, which means reaching up high. Bush beans don't climb at all. They stand on their own, about two feet tall, no support needed. You'll be picking beans in fifty-five to sixty-five days, and you can tuck three or four plants into one pot. Everything happens right at sitting level. No ladders, no stretching, no stakes.

And now, number six — the one I told you was the easiest harvest of them all. Strawberries. Here's the magic. Grow them in a hanging basket or a tall pot, and the berries spill over the edge. They dangle right out in the open, so you pick them without bending, without searching, without digging. The berries come to your fingertips. Choose a day-neutral variety and it'll fruit again and again, right up to frost. One thing to know. Strawberries in a basket dry out fast — sometimes needing water twice a day in summer. And that's the perfect moment to solve watering for good, like I promised. The easy way is a self-watering container. It holds a reservoir of water down in the base, and the soil drinks it up slowly on its own — through the dirt, like a paper towel pulling up a spill. You just top off the reservoir every few days. No dragging a hose. If you'd rather, a soaker hose on a timer does the same job — set it once, and it waters for you. Pair either one with your strawberries, and you're set. That's why number six wins. Pure reward, almost no effort.

Number seven is a little pot of herbs, kept right next to your chair. Basil, parsley, chives, mint — snip what you need while you sit. Cut no more than the top third each time, and they keep on giving. Basil likes a bigger pot, about ten to twelve inches. And mint? Always give mint its own pot. It spreads like it owns the place.

Now, here's an important one, and I'm not going to bury it. If you have a cat or a dog, please listen. Chives are toxic to pets. So is mint, and so is parsley in large amounts. The leaves and stems of tomato plants are toxic too — though the ripe fruit is perfectly safe. Basil and strawberries, on the other hand, are safe for pets. So if your furry friend wanders the garden, keep the chives, the mint, and the tomato leaves up where they can't reach. And if your pet ever does nibble something it shouldn't, call your vet, or the A-S-P-C-A poison control hotline, at eight-eight-eight, four-two-six, four-four-three-five. When in doubt, make the call.

Before we finish, one more bit of care — for you this time. The Centers for Disease Control tells us more than one in four older adults takes a fall each year, and one fall makes the next more likely. So when you've been sitting and tending your garden, rise slowly. Stand up too fast, and the dizziness can catch you. Use a chair with armrests, so you've got something solid to push up from. Wear shoes with good grip, not loose slippers. And keep a phone within reach. None of this is meant to scare you. It's meant to keep you out there, season after season.

So there they are — seven plants you can grow without ever leaving your seat. Get the height right, let the water take care of itself, and the garden comes to you. You grow real food, save a few trips to the store, and have a little fun doing it — then end with a bowl of something you grew yourself, picked without a single ache. That's the whole point. Happy gardening.
