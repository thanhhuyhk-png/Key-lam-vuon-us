## Câu hỏi 1: Tại sao 1 tép tỏi mùa thu → 1 củ nhiều tép vào hè — cần bao nhiêu tuần lạnh, ở ngưỡng °F nào, và điều gì xảy ra khi thiếu lạnh (củ round)?

**Findings:**

- Garlic requires exposure to **32–50°F temperatures for approximately two months** prior to bulb enlargement. (University of Maryland Extension, 2023) — https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-garlic-home-garden/
  → Action: Plant cloves in the ground or container outdoors in fall, not spring → Benefit: Cold naturally supplies the 2-month chill window that triggers clove division.

- Peer-reviewed research quantifies the requirement more precisely: **30–40 days at 0–4°C (32–39°F)** for full vernalization fulfillment, or **50–60 days at 10°C (50°F)** for the same result in bolting-type garlic. After vernalization is met, bulbing further requires **long photoperiod (≥13 h) and temperatures near 20°C (68°F)**. (Miao et al., *BMC Plant Biology*, 2016 — Tier 2 peer-reviewed) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4890667/
  → Action: Understand that spring warming + longer days in March–May is what triggers the bulbing after winter cold is already banked → Benefit: Explains why containers moved indoors too early in March can abort bulbing even if winter cold was adequate.

- Garlic naturally begins as **a large round undivided ball**; it only divides into individual cloves after receiving sufficient cold. When heat arrives prematurely — or cold exposure is incomplete — the bulb stays as an **undivided single ball known as a "round."** Rounds are edible and flavorful, but they lack the multi-toed structure. If a round is replanted in the fall, it will typically develop into a fully divided bulb the following season. (Ask Extension / University of Minnesota Extension expert response, 2022) — https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=413512
  → Action: If you harvest a round, do not discard it — plant it again that fall → Benefit: One extra growing season recovers a full bulb.

- Oregon State University Extension FS138 (Tier 1) confirms: **"A period of cold temperatures is required for bulb formation"** in hardneck varieties. (OSU Extension FS138) — https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/xmlui/bitstream/handle/1957/16950/fs138.pdf
  → Action: Never skip fall planting window by planting in late winter/spring → Benefit: Without adequate cold, you will harvest a round instead of a cloved bulb.

**Key science note (Feynman moment):** The cold period triggers hormonal changes at the clove meristem that initiate lateral bud differentiation — literally "telling" the single round to split into separate toes. Without this signal, the bulb grows but never divides. Bringing the container indoors too early in winter short-circuits this process.

## Câu hỏi 2: Chọn tép nào, tỏi siêu thị có trồng được không, hardneck vs softneck theo vùng?

**Findings:**

- **Larger cloves produce bigger bulbs than smaller ones.** (University of Maryland Extension, 2023) — https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-garlic-home-garden/
  → Action: When breaking a bulb for planting, always select the outermost, largest cloves and save them for the pot → Benefit: The clove you plant is directly predictive of the bulb you harvest — investing the big toe pays off at harvest.

- **"Never plant garlic from a grocery store. It may be a symptomless disease carrier."** (University of Maryland Extension, 2023) — https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-garlic-home-garden/
  → Action: Buy seed garlic from a reputable nursery or mail-order seed supplier, not the produce aisle → Benefit: Certified seed garlic is inspected for virus/disease; grocery store bulbs may look healthy but carry garlic mosaic virus or white rot that can persist in soil for years.

- WSU Extension states: **"Garlic bulbs purchased at the grocery store should not be planted due to the likelihood of diseases."** Additionally, commercial garlic may be treated or selected for shelf life rather than suitability to your region's climate. (WSU Extension, 2021) — https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/?p=11222
  → Action: Use same advice — seed catalog or local farm stand for planting stock → Benefit: Regional seed stock is selected for your zone's cold pattern and disease pressure.

- OSU Extension FS138 adds that grocery store garlic "often won't succeed in home gardens due to disease concerns and **lack of appropriate cold vernalization for your region.**" (OSU Extension FS138) — https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/xmlui/bitstream/handle/1957/16950/fs138.pdf

- **Hardneck varieties** ("stiffneck") are **most cold-hardy** and are the best choice for zones 3–6 (northern states, cold winters). **Softneck varieties** are **most adaptable** and perform well in a variety of climates, including zones 7–10 (mid-Atlantic south through California). (WSU Extension, 2021; Penn State Extension, 2022) — WSU: https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/?p=11222 · PSU: https://extension.psu.edu/growing-and-using-garlic
  → Action: If you are in the North (MN, WI, NY, PA, northern Plains), choose hardneck (Rocambole, Purple Stripe, Porcelain). In the South or Pacific Coast, softneck (Artichoke, Silverskin) performs more reliably → Benefit: Matching variety to zone prevents vernalization failure and disease susceptibility.

- PSU Extension confirms: **"Set aside the largest uniform bulbs for replanting"** from your harvest. This perpetuates the clove-size advantage each year. (Penn State Extension, 2022) — https://extension.psu.edu/growing-and-using-garlic
  → Action: At harvest, mark your best 3–4 bulbs before curing and reserve them as next year's seed stock → Benefit: Free seed garlic every year, improving in size and local adaptation over time.

## Câu hỏi 3: Thông số chậu: độ sâu, đường kính, khoảng cách, xô 5-gallon chứa bao nhiêu tép, giá thể, độ sâu trồng, thời điểm trồng theo vùng?

**Findings:**

- **Minimum container depth: 8 inches**; containers must have drainage holes near the bottom. Cloves should be spaced **4 inches apart**. (Oregon State University Extension, 2022) — https://extension.oregonstate.edu/es/node/236266/printable/print
  → Action: Use a pot at least 8–10 inches deep with drain holes → Benefit: Garlic roots grow vertically; shallow pots compress the developing bulb.

- **Planting depth: tops of cloves 2 inches below the soil surface**, pointed end up. Space **4–6 inches apart** in rows 12–24 inches apart (in-ground; scale proportionally for containers). (WSU Extension, 2021) — https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/?p=11222
  → Action: Push each clove pointed-end up until the tip sits 2 inches below the surface → Benefit: Depth provides winter insulation and proper root anchor; too shallow = frost-heave.

- PSU Extension specifies **1–2 inches of soil coverage** above the clove (flat bottom down, pointed tip up), spacing 6–7 inches apart in rows. (Penn State Extension, 2022) — https://extension.psu.edu/growing-and-using-garlic
  → Action: In colder zones (PA and north), 2 inches of soil coverage protects the clove better than 1 inch → Benefit: Prevents frost damage to the developing shoot.

- **5-gallon bucket estimate (Tier 4/5 — calculation from extension spacing guidance):** A standard 5-gallon bucket is approximately 12 inches in diameter at the top, with ~10 inches of usable planting space. At OSU's 4-inch spacing, approximately **4–6 cloves** fit comfortably. Professional growers often cite 4–5 cloves as the practical number for a standard 5-gallon container. *Note: this specific figure is inferred from OSU's spacing + standard bucket dimensions, not directly stated in an .edu publication.*

- **Planting timing by region** (Tier 1 sources combined):
  - UMD Extension: plant **mid-to-late October** in central Maryland (Zone 6–7). (UMD Extension, 2023) — https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-garlic-home-garden/
  - WSU Extension: **September–October** in eastern Washington (colder, Zone 5–6); **November** in western Washington (milder, Zone 7–8). (WSU Extension, 2021) — https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/?p=11222
  - General rule (from search summaries, consistent across multiple .edu guides): **4–6 weeks before the ground typically freezes** — late September in Zones 3–5; October in Zones 6–7; November–December in Zones 8–10.

- **Soil/media:** Garlic needs well-drained, moisture-retentive soil, pH 6.0–7.0. (WSU Extension) For containers, a high-quality potting mix with added perlite or coarse sand for drainage is appropriate. [NEED DATA: specific potting mix ratio for containers from a confirmed .edu source.]

## Câu hỏi 4: Chậu qua đông — chậu đóng băng nhanh hơn đất thế nào, phủ rơm bao nhiêu, và vùng ấm (8–10) pre-chill bao nhiêu tuần?

**Findings:**

- Hardneck garlic cloves **can survive Minnesota winters (Zone 4) in fabric pots left above ground**, provided the containers are covered with **3–4 inches of straw or leaf mulch**. (Ask Extension / University of Minnesota Extension expert, 2022) — https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=813062
  → Action: After planting in fall, heap 3–4 inches of clean straw or shredded leaves over each container → Benefit: Mulch acts as a thermal buffer; without it, a fabric pot in a Zone 4 winter can freeze through in hours, killing the cloves.

- General container-overwintering principle: above-ground containers are exposed to freezing temperatures **from all sides simultaneously**, unlike in-ground beds which only freeze from the top. Containers in Zones 4–5 can reach the same air temperature as the ambient surroundings, making them effectively 1–2 hardiness zones colder than the same plant in-ground. (Penn State Extension — principle referenced in Extension search results, consistent with PSU's overwintering container guidance at extension.psu.edu/overwintering-plants-in-containers; page content not fully accessible.) [NEED DATA: specific Penn State URL with this exact freeze-from-all-sides language verified — page could not be fully fetched.]
  → Action: Treat the container as if it were 1 zone colder than your actual zone when planning winter protection → Benefit: Zone 6 gardener should protect pots as if Zone 5.

- **Pre-chilling for warm climates (Zones 8–10):** [NEED DATA: No Tier 1-2 source (Texas A&M, UC ANR, UF IFAS) confirmed via WebFetch with specific temperature/duration for refrigerator pre-chilling of garlic cloves before planting in warm climates. Multiple non-.edu sources suggest 6–8 weeks at 35–40°F but this is NOT confirmed from a .edu extension source in this research session.] Claim should be dropped or hedged heavily in script. *(Professional growers and warm-climate seed garlic suppliers widely recommend 4–8 weeks refrigerator pre-chill at 35–40°F before planting in Zones 8–10 — Tier 4/5 consensus only; script should note "check with your local extension" for this step.)*

## Câu hỏi 5: Mùa xuân → bón đạm → scapes → thu hoạch → cure → trồng lại + tỏi lá trong nhà?

**Findings:**

- **Stop watering 2 weeks before harvest** to promote maturation and discourage bulb diseases. (University of Maine Extension — Highmoor Farm newsletter, 2024) — https://extension.umaine.edu/highmoor/ *(accessed via API endpoint: https://extension.umaine.edu/highmoor/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/13430)*
  → Action: When you see lower leaves yellowing, start counting — cut off irrigation 14 days before you plan to pull → Benefit: Dry bulbs are easier to cure and less prone to fungal rot in storage.

- **Harvest timing: when 4 leaves are still partly green** (lower leaves brown, but at least 4 green ones remaining) — typically **early to mid-July** in Pennsylvania (Zone 6). (Penn State Extension, 2022) — https://extension.psu.edu/growing-and-using-garlic
  → Action: Count leaves from the bottom — harvest when the lower half has turned but 4 green ones remain → Benefit: Each green leaf = one papery wrapper around the bulb; harvest too late → wrappers disintegrate, cloves separate, bulb won't store.

- UMD Extension confirms: harvest when **approximately ½ of foliage turns brown** (in central Maryland, around **July 1**); full maturation takes **250–270 days from fall planting**. Curing: **7–14 days** in a well-ventilated location out of direct sunlight. Long-term storage: **32–38°F, 65% relative humidity, up to 6–9 months**. (University of Maryland Extension, 2023) — https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-garlic-home-garden/
  → Action: Move cured bulbs to a cool, dark spot (basement, unheated garage) after curing → Benefit: Proper temperature + humidity prevents sprouting and extends usability to 6–9 months.

- OSU FS138 specifies cure duration of **2–3 weeks in warm, dry, well-ventilated location** until outer skin becomes papery and tops dry completely. WSU Extension says "several weeks" in cool/dry/ventilated. (OSU FS138) — https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/xmlui/bitstream/handle/1957/16950/fs138.pdf · WSU: https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/?p=11222

- **Scapes (hardneck only):** All three confirmed .edu sources agree — remove scapes before they straighten and become stiff. PSU: "remove while young." WSU: "remove when they begin to curl." OSU FS138: "removing flower scapes directs energy toward bulb enlargement, resulting in larger, higher-quality bulbs." (PSU, WSU, OSU — all confirmed WebFetch above)
  → Action: Snap or cut the scape at its base when it has made its first curl but before it straightens → Benefit: Redirects the plant's energy from seed production to bulb growth; scapes are edible (sauté, pesto).

- **Scape removal % improvement:** [NEED DATA: No Tier 1-2 source confirmed via WebFetch with a specific % bulb size or yield increase from scape removal for home gardens. Ontario Ministry of Agriculture page was inaccessible (403). Search results referenced a University of Guelph study (John Zandstra) citing 20–30%+ yield increase with hand removal, but this was not directly confirmed from the publication. Do not use a specific % in script without confirmed source.]

- **Nitrogen timing:** [NEED DATA: No Tier 1-2 .edu source confirmed via WebFetch with specific nitrogen application timing/amounts for container garlic. Multiple non-.edu sources suggest applying nitrogen-rich fertilizer in early spring when shoots emerge and stopping nitrogen ~4–6 weeks before harvest. This needs verification from extension source before citing in script.]

- **Replanting from harvest:** "Set aside the largest uniform bulbs for replanting." (PSU Extension, 2022) — https://extension.psu.edu/growing-and-using-garlic
  → Action: Each fall, break your best bulb and plant the biggest outer cloves → Benefit: Free seed garlic that is locally adapted and improving in size each season.

- **Virus/disease risk when replanting:** OSU FS138 notes disease concerns with non-certified stock; UMD and WSU emphasize this risk for grocery garlic. After several years of saving seed from the same source, viral load can accumulate. [NEED DATA: specific extension guidance on how many years you can save seed before disease resets → recommend sourcing fresh certified stock periodically.]

- **Indoor green garlic on windowsill:** Growing garlic indoors without a cold period will produce only green shoots ("garlic greens"), not a divided bulb — because vernalization cannot happen at room temperature. University of Kentucky Extension (county newsletter, 2023) mentions garlic greens as a winter indoor option (https://lyon.ca.uky.edu/sites/lyon.ca.uky.edu/files/23FallHortNews.pdf — not directly WebFetched in this session). ## Consensus Principles (3 — nguyên liệu twist/authority cho script)

### 1. Garlic must receive a sustained cold period to divide into separate cloves; skip the cold and you harvest an undivided "round."

- **Hội tụ:** UMD Extension ("32–50°F for 2 months"), PMC peer-reviewed research ("30–40 days at 32–39°F"), and OSU FS138 ("a period of cold temperatures is required for bulb formation") all agree that cold exposure is the critical trigger for clove differentiation — not just for growth, but specifically for the splitting process.
- **Cơ chế (from PMC4890667):** Vernalization fulfillment unlocks meristem transition; the subsequent long-day signal (spring) then drives actual bulbing. These are two separate gates.
- **Khác nhau:** PMC gives precise temperature × duration numbers; extension sources state this functionally ("plant in fall," "needs cold before bulbing"). Both converge on the same gardener action.
- **Nguyên tắc cho script:** *Cold isn't just about survival — it's the biological switch that turns one round ball into ten separate cloves. Bring the container indoors too early, and that switch never flips.*
- URLs: https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-garlic-home-garden/ · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4890667/ · https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/xmlui/bitstream/handle/1957/16950/fs138.pdf

---

### 2. The larger the clove you plant, the larger the bulb you harvest — and grocery store garlic is unsuitable for planting due to disease risk.

- **Hội tụ:** Three independent Tier 1 extension sources (UMD, WSU, OSU FS138) independently warn against grocery store garlic using different language but the same conclusion: it carries disease ("symptomless disease carrier" — UMD; "likelihood of diseases" — WSU; "disease concerns" — OSU). On clove size: UMD states larger cloves → bigger bulbs; PSU says save the largest uniform bulbs for replanting. The Jordan Journal study (peer-reviewed international) quantifies this at +25.88% total yield from 3–3.5g cloves vs 1–1.49g cloves — but this is from an international research context, not US extension.
- **Khác nhau:** US extension sources describe the principle qualitatively; the quantitative data comes from international research. No US .edu source found with specific % for home garden conditions.
- **Nguyên tắc cho script:** *Three different state extension offices tell you the same thing: grocery store garlic is a disease gamble. Buy from a seed supplier, plant the biggest cloves, save the biggest bulbs — that's the compounding return.*
- URLs: https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-garlic-home-garden/ · https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/?p=11222 · https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/xmlui/bitstream/handle/1957/16950/fs138.pdf

---

### 3. Containers freeze harder and faster than ground soil — they need insulation but must stay outdoors cold for vernalization to work.

- **Hội tụ:** Ask Extension (UMN expert, 2022) confirms that fabric pots can survive Minnesota Zone 4 winters with 3–4 inches of straw mulch. This aligns with the general container-overwintering principle (PSU — referenced but page not fully fetched) that containers lose heat from all sides simultaneously, making them effectively colder than in-ground equivalents. The key tension: the container needs cold (for vernalization) but not *killing* cold (which mulch prevents). Both sources point to mulch as the solution that threads this needle.
- **Khác nhau:** UMN expert specifically validates fabric pots (not just ceramic/plastic). PSU's general container principle was not confirmed from a directly fetched page — treat that element as supporting context only.
- **Nguyên tắc cho script:** *The container needs to stay cold — that's the whole point. But 'cold enough to vernalize' and 'cold enough to kill' are different thresholds. Three inches of straw is the difference.*
- URLs: https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=813062 · https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/?p=11222

  - **Hedge phrasing for script:** *"If you have dogs or cats, keep garlic plants and any harvested bulbs out of their reach. The ASPCA lists garlic as toxic to both dogs and cats — it can damage red blood cells even in small amounts."*

- **[YMYL] "Never plant garlic from a grocery store — it may be a symptomless disease carrier"** (UMD Extension). While this is a plant health claim, not a human health claim, it has YMYL implications if the video recommends grocery store garlic and a viewer unknowingly spreads white rot (Sclerotium cepivorum) or other soil-persistent pathogens into their garden.
