### Hook (hook.md A — đã duyệt, sửa «five ways to do it»)
Zero degrees. Snow on the ground. And the kale is still green, with no greenhouse anywhere in sight.

Kale, spinach and mache that have had a cold autumn can take temperatures near zero with no cover at all. [C01, C02, C05] Carrots can wait in the ground down to about five degrees. [C04] So the glass house was never what kept them alive.

For everything less tough, and for the very worst nights, the answer is a cheap cover that fits the crop. An old window. A few hoops. A sheet of thin fabric. There are five ways to do it, and none needs a greenhouse.

But that thin fabric might add only two degrees. [C12] So why is two degrees enough?

### M1 · Way one: pick the right plant
Way one costs nothing. All you need is the right plant.

Picture kale under four inches of snow. You walk out, brush the leaves clean, and cut dinner. No cover. No glass. That's what a hardy plant can do.

Here's the rule. Leafy greens like kale and spinach are the toughest. [C01, C02] Mache, also called corn salad, joins them, and in zone six or higher it easily survives winter with no protection at all. [C05, C08] Carrots and leeks come next. [C04, C06] And leaf lettuce is the soft one. It gives up around fifteen degrees. [C03]

Cabbage has a trick. The deeply crinkled savoy types are much hardier than the flat-leaf kinds. [C09] Leeks have one, too. The darker, blue-green varieties push lower than the rest. [C07] So the variety on the seed packet counts.

One note for pet owners. Leeks are in the onion family, and the ASPCA lists them as toxic to dogs and cats, so keep pets out of that bed. [C39]

Now, one catch. Those limits belong to plants that grew through a cold autumn. [C01] That's what toughens them up, much like a gardener's hands toughen up with work. Catch kale before it has had that chill, and it handles only moderate freezes, twenty-four to twenty-eight degrees. [C10] Same plant. Very different winter.

One more limit. The hardy numbers, zero to fifteen, come from mild, wet winters west of the Cascades, and the chart calls them guidance only. [C01] Where winters are harsher and windier, give yourself extra margin.

So a chilly October isn't your enemy. It's part of the plan.

Here's what to do tonight. Check this week's lowest forecast night, and write down which of your crops can't handle it. That short list is all you need to cover.

Lettuce is probably on it. Say the forecast says twelve, and lettuce quits at fifteen. [C03] That's a three-degree gap. So what closes a gap like that?

### M2 · Ways two and three: fabric and hoops
Way two is a sheet of fabric. It's called row cover, and it drapes right over the plants. No tools needed.

Think of it as a coat for your lettuce. A coat doesn't make heat. It just keeps in a little of what's already there.

Here's the surprise. Not every fabric is the same. Fabric comes in weights. A light one might add about two degrees. [C12] A medium one adds up to four. [C14] A heavy one gives you roughly four to ten, depending on the brand and the weight. [C13, C15, C16] There's a trade-off, though. A medium cover lets through about eighty-five percent of the light. A heavy one lets through as little as thirty. [C14, C15] The thicker the fabric, the less sunshine reaches your plants.

Picture a clear, cold December night. A pale sheet lies over the lettuce bed. By morning, the frost has settled on the fabric instead of the leaves.

Is your forecast dipping into the teens? Then stay with me, because the next part is for you.

Way three is hoops. Bend a few over the row and drape plastic across them. That's a low tunnel. A plastic tunnel like this typically adds three to a little over six degrees. [C17] It takes a little more work than fabric.

Every cover so far buys you a handful of degrees, and that's all. The next one is different. It can turn one sunny hour into a disaster.

### M3 · The ladder, and way four: the cold frame
Back to that thin fabric and its two degrees. Here's why it's enough. Put the numbers side by side, and you get a ladder. [GFX: Cover Ladder — nguồn SC1, SC3, SC4; nhãn «CHANNEL ARITHMETIC · AIR TEMP · GUIDANCE ONLY»; cột vải dày = 4 (đầu thấp), ô cold frame «WARMER», không số cho rơm, KHÔNG ô âm]

Hardy plants already carry most of the cold themselves. The cover only has to close the last few degrees. [C01, C03, C04, C12] That's why two degrees can be enough for a plant that's only two degrees past its limit. Lettuce sits lower on the ladder, so it needs a rung on most cold nights. [C03] Carrots and leeks need one only on the nastiest nights. Kale barely needs one. [C01, C04, C06]

Remember lettuce's three-degree gap? A light cover won't close it, and a medium one cuts it close. Reach for a heavy cover or a low tunnel. [C12, C14, C13, C17] So don't pick a cover that just meets the gap. Pick one that closes it with room to spare. This is simple arithmetic on the numbers on screen. It isn't a test. But it shows you why you start with the plant, and then add a cover with room to spare.

Now, way four, the cold frame. Remember that sunny hour? It's a bottomless box with a clear lid, set right over the row. No door, no walking in, no heater. Just a lid. Face the lid south, so it catches the most sun. [C22] Make the back about eighteen inches tall and the front about twelve, so the lid tilts toward the light. [C20]

And the lid? An old window works great. So does a storm window, or even a shower door. [C21] Free, if you've got one in the shed. One safety note. Paint made before nineteen seventy-eight can contain lead. So if an old window has chipping paint, skip it, or ask your local extension office first. [C38]

Here's why this works. Glass lets the sun in and keeps the warmth from escaping. That's also the danger. A cold frame can fail two ways. Too cold at night. Too hot in the day. With just one hour of sun, the air under a closed lid can shoot past one hundred thirty degrees. [C19] That's no place for winter lettuce. But get it right, and the same frame keeps working into early spring. [C37]

So raise the lid whenever the air inside climbs above eighty-five degrees. [C18] Then close it again in the late afternoon, before the air cools off. [C24]

That's four ways. Yet there's one thing none of them can do. It decides what you'll actually pick in January.

### M4 · Way five: straw for the roots
Way five is straw. It's for roots, like carrots, that stay in the ground. [C04]

Pile straw over the row, and you can keep harvesting until the ground freezes solid. [C26] One extension service says to use a heavy layer, about twelve inches thick. [C25] That advice is for other root crops, though. So treat it as a starting point for carrots, not a rule.

How many degrees does straw add? That's harder to say. Count it as help, not a guarantee.

Timing matters, too. Wait a couple of weeks after the first frost before you mulch. [C28] Voles need cover to survive, and a thick blanket of straw is exactly that. [C27] Pile it on too early, and you've rolled out the welcome mat.

Picture a cold morning, before the ground locks up. The row looks like nothing but a bump of straw. You peel it back, and there they are. Carrots, cold and ready. [C26] Pull what you need for dinner, then tuck the straw back in.

That's all five. But the numbers behind them don't line up perfectly from one guide to the next. And you should hear exactly where. After that, the one thing no cover can fix.

### M5 · Which way for which crop, and where the numbers differ
Now, where the numbers differ. You've already seen why kale's limit shifts after a cold autumn. Heavy fabric varies, too. One guide says six to ten degrees. Another says four to eight. [C13, C15] Brands and weights differ. So plan on the low end, and leave yourself a margin.

That's the real picture. These numbers are a guide, and your yard decides the rest.

Now, the plan. Hardy greens? Way one, plus a cover that fits the gap on the coldest nights. Lettuce? Fabric or hoops. Carrots? Straw. And a cold frame earns its keep when you're around on sunny days to vent the lid if the heat builds. [C18, C19] Start small. Pick one crop that's already in your bed, and one way, this week.

Which brings us to the thing no cover can fix.

### M6 · What to expect: the light
Light. Most plants need at least ten hours of it to actively make food. [C34] When the days drop below that, growth largely stops. [C30] In Chicago, that stretch runs from about the ninth of November to the first of February. [C31] Your dates shift with where you live. [C32]

So here's what to expect. These plants aren't growing during the darkest weeks. They're living in cold storage, waiting for the light to return. [C33] Kept within its cold limit, a mature plant stays dormant in the cold ground, and you can keep picking through the winter. For roots, that lasts until the ground freezes solid. [C36, C26] You grew it in the fall. Now you pick it.

That's also why timing matters. Plan your seeding so plants are mature before the light runs short where you live. [C35] If it's too late for that this year, jot a note for next summer. Start counting back from your area's normal first frost date. [C40]

So don't judge your winter garden by how much it grows. Judge it by what's still alive and ready to pick.

And one last thing. Everything here is for cold-hardy crops. Summer-loving plants are a different story.
