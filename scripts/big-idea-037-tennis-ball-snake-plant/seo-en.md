# YouTube SEO Description — Tennis Ball Snake Plant Myth

## DESCRIPTION

**Snake plant root rot** — Discover why the viral tennis ball drainage trick is actually rotting your roots (and the two-part fix that works).

The tennis ball under your pot sounds like good engineering. But soil physics doesn't cooperate. Extension researchers at Illinois, Missouri, and Wisconsin independently confirmed the same finding: that filler at the bottom creates a "perched water table" where soil stays wet longer, not drier—and your roots suffer for it.

In this video, you'll learn:
- Why the tennis ball trick backfires: soil physics, capillary action, and root oxygen starvation
- How a perched water table works and why it kills roots faster than overwatering
- The two-part fix that actually prevents houseplant root rot: perlite soil mix + proper drainage holes
- The finger test to know when your snake plant soil is dry enough to water
- Why this works for other succulents too (jade, aloe, echeveria)

For years, gardeners filled pot bottoms with rocks, packing peanuts, and tennis balls. It's basically "crocking"—a drainage myth from the 1960s in new clothes. But research shows it traps water right above your plant's roots instead of letting it drain. Roots need air pockets to breathe. Saturate that zone for just 4–8 hours, and opportunistic fungi move in (University of California research). An unglazed terracotta pot with several drainage holes beats plastic every time—it lets moisture evaporate right through the sides. Mix perlite throughout your soil (about 25% for snake plants specifically, higher for other succulents if you live somewhere humid), and you've solved the drainage problem once at planting time. No more digging around under the pot every few weeks. No more wrestling heavy planters off shelves just to inspect what's hiding below. The real fix is lighter, cheaper, and faster than chasing the viral trick. A bag of perlite weighs a fraction of gravel and costs less too.

Watch to understand why this houseplant drainage trick spread—and exactly how to save your sansevieria from soggy, oxygen-starved roots.
