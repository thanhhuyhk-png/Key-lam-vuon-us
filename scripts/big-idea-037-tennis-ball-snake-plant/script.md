## TTS SCRIPT

Pull that snake plant out of its pot right now, and look what's sitting under the roots. A tennis ball. The soil just above it? Dark, heavy, soaked through, not dry like you'd expect. Don't panic. This isn't your fault. Extension researchers at Illinois, Missouri, and Wisconsin all found the same thing. That soil gets wetter, not drier. And there are three reasons it's quietly rotting your plant's roots.

Here's the thing. Folks are swapping in rocks, bottle caps, packing peanuts, tennis balls. Anything to fill the gap at the bottom of the pot. It's basically the modern version of something gardeners did with broken clay pot pieces back in the nineteen sixties. They called it crocking. Same mistake, just a different material. And reason number one isn't even about the tennis ball itself. It's about something gardeners have been getting wrong for sixty years.

Reason number one. Water doesn't drip down into a solid object the way you'd think. Here's why this feels true, but isn't. Picture a kitchen sponge sitting on a dry countertop. The sponge holds onto its water until it's completely soaked through. Only then does water spill onto the counter below. Potting soil behaves the same way. It clings to moisture through something called capillary action. It won't release a drop into a tennis ball, a rock, or gravel. Not until the soil above is fully saturated. Extension researchers call this a perched water table, and it's not just one opinion. Illinois, Missouri, and Wisconsin-Madison extension programs all confirm it independently. The water doesn't drain past that solid object. Instead, it pools right above it, closer to your plant's roots than ever. It sits there long after you thought the pot had drained, and that's the quiet part nobody warns you about.

That's reason one. But a soggier root zone isn't the only problem. The root zone itself just got smaller, and that's its own kind of trouble.

Reason number two. That tennis ball is taking up space your roots used to have. Less soil means the same watering now saturates it faster, with less room for error. And a saturated root zone is an oxygen-starved root zone. Roots need air pockets between soil particles to breathe. Flood those pockets with water, and the tissue starts breaking down within hours, not days. You'll see it as roots that have gone soft and dark, instead of firm and white. That's when opportunistic fungi move in. University of California researchers found soil sitting saturated for just four to eight hours is enough to trigger disease. Four to eight hours. Not weeks. Not even a full day. That's less time than it takes a package to arrive at your door.

So the ball traps water and shrinks the safe zone. But here's the third reason, and honestly, it's the one that really matters.

Reason number three. A tennis ball doesn't fix what's actually broken. Say your pot doesn't have real drainage holes. Or your soil holds onto water too long. No amount of filler at the bottom changes that. Your plant will still sit in soggy soil, ball or no ball. If you check again in a month, you'll likely find the exact same soggy soil. The exact same slow decline. Because the ball never touched the real cause. That's it. Three reasons, and none of them have anything to do with the tennis ball itself. They're about physics, plain and simple.

So that ball isn't some magic oxygen pocket. It's just the modern version of crocking. Same mistake, different object, sixty years apart. And like crocking, it took real research to finally put it to rest.

Here's what makes this myth so sticky, and it's kind of fascinating. It's not a lazy idea. It actually sounds like good engineering. A gap at the bottom feels like it should give water somewhere to go. That's exactly why so many well-meaning gardeners keep passing it along. The instinct isn't the problem. The soil physics just doesn't cooperate, no matter how logical it feels.

Now here's what actually works, and it's simpler than digging around for a spare tennis ball.

The real fix has two parts. Part one is about mixing perlite through the entire pot, not just the bottom. Clemson University extension points to about twenty-five percent perlite for snake plants specifically. Iowa State extension goes as high as two-thirds mineral material for succulents in general, more if you live somewhere humid. Somewhere in that range works. You don't need a kitchen scale for this. Just eyeball it. You're not filling a gap. You're changing the whole mix so water moves through evenly.

Part two is just as important: your pot needs real drainage holes. Not one small hole, several. Illinois extension guidance is blunt about it. Pots without drainage holes carry a real risk of root rot, no matter what you put inside them. If you can, unglazed terracotta beats plastic here. It's porous, so it lets moisture evaporate right through the sides, not just out the bottom.

And here's a nice side effect for anyone tired of heavy lifting. A bag of perlite weighs a fraction of a bag of gravel or rocks. It's dirt cheap compared to either one, too. You're not just fixing the drainage. You're making the whole pot lighter to carry.

One more thing before we move on. If you've got a cat or dog who loves digging in soil, tuck the newly repotted plant out of reach. Give it a few days, until the mix settles.

This is where the tennis ball idea actually had the right instinct, just the wrong tool. You want less physical work, not more trips to repot and check for rot. Mixing the perlite in once, at planting time, takes care of it. No digging around every few months to inspect a ball at the bottom. No wrestling a heavy pot off the shelf just to peek at the roots. For watering, the fixed calendar can go out the window. Colorado State and North Carolina State extension both agree. A well-mixed snake plant pot can take anywhere from two to eight weeks to dry out completely. It depends on the season, your home's humidity, and how much light the plant gets. Instead of guessing, try pushing a finger two to three inches into the soil. Bone dry means it's time to water. Anything else, and a few more days of waiting usually does the trick. One viewer told us that finger test was the first watering trick that actually clicked.

A few weeks in, and you'll start to see it working. Healthy roots show up as firm, upright leaves with good color, not the soft, yellowing kind that signals trouble. If a leaf does go soft near the base, that's your early warning. Check the roots before things get worse. It's not a reason to panic. And if you've got other succulents prone to the same overwatering mistake, jade plants, aloe, echeveria. The same two-part fix works for them too. Perlite through the mix, real holes in the pot. You don't need a different trick for every plant on the windowsill. Just the same two habits, repeated.

By your next watering day, you'll already be checking soil with your finger. No more worrying about what's hiding at the bottom of the pot.
