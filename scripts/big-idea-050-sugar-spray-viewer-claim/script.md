# 4 Kitchen Bug Sprays Ranked — 1 of Them Feeds the Aphids

A spoon of sugar. A glass of water. Thousands of gardeners spray this on their plants every week, hoping it will kill aphids.

Here's the part nobody mentions.

An aphid already makes sugar. It's called honeydew, and they leak it all day long. It is the exact reason ants climb the stem in the first place.

So when you spray sugar on a leaf, you are not poisoning anything. You're setting the table.

Four kitchen sprays. I've gathered the research on all four, and today we're ranking them.

Dish soap. Garlic. Sugar. Baking soda.

One comes close to working. One works partway. One feeds the very insect you're trying to get rid of. And one is aimed at a completely different problem.

And I'll say this now so it isn't a surprise later: the thing that works best may not be on this list at all. Stay to the end for that one.

Because the sugar one doesn't just fail. It calls in a second insect, and that second insect arrives to protect the aphids from everything that would have eaten them.

That's coming up in about two minutes.

Does it kill, or does it feed?

This one kills. And we know exactly how.

Colorado State University Extension puts it plainly. Insecticidal soaps kill insects by disrupting cell membranes, so the insect dies from rapid dehydration. Soap also strips the protective wax off soft-bodied insects. Aphids are about as soft-bodied as it gets.

But there are two catches, and most people miss both.

The first is contact. Soap only kills what it physically touches. There is no residue working for you tomorrow. Colorado State says sprays must thoroughly cover the insect targets, and that means the undersides of leaves, where aphids actually sit. Spraying the top of a leaf and walking away does nothing.

The second is heat. High concentrations of soap can burn foliage, especially when plants are stressed, when temperatures are above ninety degrees, and when humidity is high. That damage builds up over repeated sprays.

One more thing about the bottle in your sink, and it sounds like hair-splitting until you look at it. Dish detergent is not soap. Colorado State describes household products as a mixture of detergents, degreasers, emulsifiers, and preservatives. A registered insecticidal soap is one tested chemistry, chosen to minimize plant injury.

First place. With conditions.

Does it kill, or does it feed?

Partway.

In a peer-reviewed screening study on banana aphids, garlic botanicals used alone caused more than fifty percent mortality at ninety-six hours. That is a real effect. It is also, to be precise, one aphid species, in a laboratory and a screenhouse. It is not your rose bush.

The more useful number came when garlic stopped working alone. Garlic combined with neem oil and insecticidal soap brought populations under twenty aphids per plant. The water-treated plants had two hundred.

There's a catch with the homemade version, though. When you crush garlic in your kitchen, you have no idea what concentration you ended up with. Two heads of garlic vary. The study used a standardized extract. Your jar did not.

Second place. It does something. It just doesn't finish the job.

Does it kill, or does it feed?

It feeds. And now I can show you the whole mechanism.

Start with the aphid. University of Minnesota Extension describes honeydew as a sugar-rich material that attracts ants, yellowjackets, and other insects that feed on it. The aphid leaks it constantly. Waste to the aphid, food supply to everything else.

Now the ants. This is the part that changes how you look at a plant.

The University of Maryland Extension puts it this way. Ants feed on honeydew and will defend the insects against their natural enemies, not unlike herders tending and protecting their livestock.

The ants are not eating your aphids. They are ranching them. Ladybug larvae arrive to eat the aphids, and the ants drive them off.

University of Wisconsin Extension adds the detail that finishes it. When the food runs out, ants will carry the aphids to new plants. They relocate the herd.

There's a second cost too. Sugar on a leaf feeds sooty mold, the grayish film that grows on honeydew. It doesn't kill the plant, but it blocks light and it looks terrible.

So, the honest statement. Sugar is not a poison, and nobody is claiming it kills your plant. The narrower point is the useful one: it is not an insecticide. It's food. And no extension study shows sugar water controlling aphids, because the mechanism runs the other way.

Last place. Not because it's dangerous. Because it's working for the other side.

Does it kill, or does it feed?

Neither. And I want to clear this one up, because baking soda has a real job in the garden. It is simply not this job.

Baking soda is a fungicide. It works on powdery mildew, that white dust on squash leaves and phlox. University of Wisconsin Extension publishes a mix of about one and a half tablespoons of baking soda with three tablespoons of lightweight horticultural oil per gallon of water.

Notice the oil. It is not optional. Sodium bicarbonate on its own is inferior in adhesiveness and spreadability, which is a technical way of saying it slides off the leaf. And Colorado State still classifies this mix as experimental.

Against aphids, it's the right tool aimed at the wrong problem. So it isn't ranked last. It just isn't in this race.

Now, the thing I promised at the start.

Rank these four honestly and you end up somewhere uncomfortable. Sugar works against you. Baking soda is aimed elsewhere. Garlic is partial. Dish soap works, but the research keeps adding conditions.

And the extension services do not agree with each other here, which I'd rather show you than hide.

Iowa State Extension has published a roughly two percent solution made from liquid dish detergent. Penn State Extension takes the opposite position, in an article titled, and I am not paraphrasing, "Are Home Remedies a Good Solution? Penn State Extension Says No."

Their reasoning is worth hearing. Home remedies come with no precise instructions, they may not be effective, and spraying a mix containing dish detergent on a sunny day can injure the plant, essentially burning it.

So the best answer isn't in your kitchen. It's a registered insecticidal soap. A few dollars, a label that tells you the concentration, and a mixture tested so it doesn't burn your leaves.

That's a strange ending for a video about kitchen sprays, and I'd rather give you that than a tidy one.

But there is a free option, and it beats three of the four we just covered. A strong jet of plain water knocks aphids off, and most never make it back. No mixing, no burn risk, nothing that attracts ants.

And if you see ants marching up the stem, that isn't a separate problem. That's the aphid problem, with security.

Three questions I expect.

Is any of this safe around pets? Here's the straight answer. Keep cats and dogs away from a treated plant until the leaves are completely dry, then it's fine. If your cat chews leaves as a habit, spray that plant when the cat is out of the room and give it an hour. And read the label, because the label is written for exactly this question. If you were only ever spraying sugar water, your pets were never the issue. The ants were.

Can you spray this on vegetables you plan to eat? Only if the label says so. That is the whole point of a label, and it is the single thing a kitchen mix cannot give you.

How often? Coverage matters more than frequency. Soap kills on contact, so one thorough spray including the undersides of leaves, repeated at five to seven day intervals, beats a heavy soaking once.

So, ranked. Dish soap first, with conditions. Garlic second. Sugar last, and working against you. Baking soda in a different race entirely.

If you want the one-sentence version: aphids already make sugar, and the ants are already collecting it. Adding more just tells them the herd is doing fine.

Next time you're out there in the morning, before you mix anything, turn one leaf over and look at the underside. Whatever is happening on your plants is happening down there, not on top.
