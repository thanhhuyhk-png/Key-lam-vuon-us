## TTS CLEAN EXPORT — Self-Audit v2

Stop growing tomatoes on your balcony. Five compact herbs, no yard needed, and some of them fetch thirty dollars a pound or more.

Most folks default to tomatoes and basic herbs. Rain or shine, you're saving maybe five dollars a season. That's not a return. That's a hobby that barely covers the seed packet.

Here's the truth: according to the Botanical Adulterants Prevention Program at the American Botanical Council, ashwagandha root is among the most adulterated herbs on the specialty market. Grow it yourself and you know exactly what you've got.

One plant on this list doesn't even need soil. And it might be the most profitable one here.

**Look, your grandmother probably didn't grow herbs for the money. She grew them because she could. Lemon balm by the kitchen door. A pot of sage on the windowsill. She knew their names without thinking about it. But here's the thing — those same plants she knew, the ones our grandparents had for free, are now selling for thirty to fifty dollars a pound at the specialty market. Same plants. Different times. Worth knowing.**

In the next twelve minutes, I'll walk you through five high-value herbs that genuinely thrive in containers, ranked by reported market price per pound, every figure sourced. I'll show you how to arrange them by sunlight on a standard balcony, and the one post-harvest step that can multiply what your harvest is worth. Fair warning: these are potential values, not guaranteed income. I'll be upfront about that every step of the way.

Three things: the five plants and what they're worth, how to place them by sunlight, and how to dry and sell them legally, no business license needed.

**And the plant most folks overlook? It's the one ranked dead last on dollar-per-pound. But it scales when nothing else will, and it grows in the spot you probably ignore. I'll explain why that matters more than the price tag at plant number five. Stick around for that one.**

That's it. Let's go.

---

Alright, let's get into it. Number one is a plant a viewer from the Pacific Northwest messaged me about. She'd been running it in stacked water trays for two summers before she realized what she had. It's duckweed. Lemna minor, if you want the science name. Don't let the size fool you. This stuff is tiny, but the numbers aren't.

Carolina Biological Supply lists Lemna minor at around twelve dollars and forty cents per hundred to a hundred and fifty fronds. When you convert that to fresh weight, specialty aquatic plant retailers report ranges of thirty to sixty dollars per pound equivalent. That's the reported range. Actual prices vary by buyer and region. I'll link the source in the description.

Here's why this works in a container: duckweed doesn't need soil at all. A shallow tray, five to ten centimeters deep, and at least sixteen hours of light per day. That's the whole setup. In warm summer conditions, the colony can roughly double its mass every forty-eight hours. You're not waiting for a harvest window. The harvest is constant.

One container note: duckweed runs best solo. It needs its own separate tray. It's a water-based system that doesn't mix with your potted plants. Now, number two is a plant you might've seen growing as a tall tree. On a balcony, you keep it small on purpose. And that's actually the smarter move.

---

Number two: moringa. Moringa oleifera. The U-S-D-A Agricultural Marketing Service has tracked dry moringa leaf at around a dollar ten per pound at the farm gate. But organic leaf powder moving through specialty wholesale channels, documents from herb suppliers show ranges up to forty-two, forty-three dollars per pound. That gap between raw leaf and processed powder? That's where the opportunity lives.

The balcony strategy for moringa is counterintuitive. You want a container that holds at least four and a half liters, deep enough for the tap root. Then you prune it hard. Keep it at shrub height, three to four feet. That gives you dense leaf growth for harvest instead of one tall trunk. It's actually less work, not more.

---

Number three is actually two plants together, because they share a container, they share the same soil preference, and they're both in a market that's genuinely strong right now. I'm talking about lemon balm and holy basil. What most people call tulsi.

Frontier Co-op lists organic cut-and-sifted lemon balm at around twenty-two dollars per pound. Organic tulsi leaf from the same supplier runs over fifty dollars a pound. These are reported market figures. Actual prices vary by region and season, and I'll link sources in the description. The reason both plants command those prices is their volatile oil content: geranial and neral in lemon balm, and eugenol as one of the primary compounds in holy basil, though the exact percentage shifts significantly by cultivar. Home-dried tulsi is genuinely variable in potency. But here's the truth. That's exactly why home-grown is worth something. You know your source.

---

We're halfway through the list. But the next two plants are where this gets genuinely interesting. One of them thrives on neglect, and the other one loves the shadiest corner of your balcony where nothing else will grow. Don't go anywhere.

---

Number four. And this is the one where I'll tell you something that sounds completely wrong: the lazier you are with this plant, the better it performs. I'm talking about ashwagandha. Withania somnifera. And there's a documented biological reason for that, not just a gardener's saying.

Ashwagandha produces its active compounds, withanolides and withaferins, as a stress response. When the soil's dry and nutrient-poor, the plant activates secondary metabolite synthesis pathways. When you water generously and fertilize heavily, it shifts energy into leaf growth instead. You end up diluting the very compounds that give it value. The science on this is documented biology. It's not folk wisdom.

On price: the American Botanical Council's HerbalGram documented ashwagandha root at thirty-nine to fifty-seven dollars per pound for high-quality dried root. That's from their two-thousand-eighteen market data. I want to be clear about that date. Prices shift. But it gives you a verified historical baseline for what this root category has fetched. And we're talking root here, not leaf.

Container setup: you need a deep pot, at least eight inches deep, about eight inches across, with sandy, well-draining soil mix. This plant's tap root won't tolerate wet feet. Water only when the top two inches of soil are bone dry. And here's the thing: if you can go five days without thinking about it, you're doing it right.

**Quick note before we move on: nothing in this video is medical advice. Ashwagandha, tulsi, and several herbs we're covering have documented physiological effects. If you're considering them for personal use, talk to your doctor first — especially if you're on any medication or managing a chronic condition. This video is about growing them and the market they sell into. Not about consumption.**

---

Number five. Gotu kola. Centella asiatica. This one's for the shadiest spot on your balcony. That corner you've been ignoring? That's where gotu kola earns. Organic Way lists it at around thirty-five dollars per pound, cut and sifted. Marketplace data from specialty herb sellers shows similar ranges. I'll caveat that those prices shift, so screenshot your sources when you research this.

Here's the setup map, and this is the part most balcony guides skip completely. You've got two zones on almost every balcony: the bright outer edge and the shaded inner corner. Moringa and tulsi go on the outer edge. They both want six or more hours of direct sun. Lemon balm goes mid-position, four to six hours of partial shade. Ashwagandha takes the driest, sunniest pot you've got. And gotu kola goes straight into that shady corner, three to five hours of diffused light. Place it under the moringa's drip tray and it catches the overflow moisture. You just turned a problem corner into a productive one.

---

That's the planting map. But here's the thing. Growing these five herbs is actually the easy part. What most people miss is what happens after the harvest. Coming up, I'll show you the one drying method that preserves what makes these herbs valuable in the first place. Miss that step and you're selling raw material at commodity prices. Nail it and you're in a completely different margin category.

Alright. Let's talk about what happens after you harvest. Because this is where the real difference between selling herbs for pennies and selling them for dollars actually lives.

Most drying guides say hang your herbs upside down. Fine for fragrance. But if you want to preserve the compounds buyers are actually paying for, rosmarinic acid in lemon balm, the volatile oils in tulsi, temperature is everything. Research published in I-O-P Conference Series on drying temperature effects found that rosmarinic acid degrades significantly above forty to fifty degrees Celsius during drying. Practically: don't use a food dehydrator on high heat, and don't dry in a hot garage in July.

Here's the seven-step shade-dry process. One: harvest in the morning after dew dries. Two: rinse in cold water, blot dry. Three: spread stems on a mesh drying rack, single layer, no stacking. Four: you want a dark room with airflow, between sixty-four and seventy-five degrees Fahrenheit, humidity below forty-five percent. Five: dry seven to fourteen days until the leaves crumble between your fingers. Six: strip leaves by hand. Don't grind yet. That protects the essential oil glands. Seven: pack into kraft bags with a one-gram silica packet per bag. Plain as day once you've done it once.

The full equipment list runs under fifty dollars. A four-tier mesh drying rack, around fifteen dollars. Stainless pruning shears, nine. A fifty-pack of kraft bags with windows, twelve. A hundred silica packets, six dollars and fifty cents. A digital thermometer and hygrometer for the drying room, seven. Total: about forty-nine dollars and forty cents. You don't need a greenhouse. You don't need equipment you don't already half-own.

---

Now here's the part that surprises most people: selling your dried herbs locally is legal in most states without a business license. Dried herbs, tea-grade, cut and sifted, qualify as non-potentially-hazardous food under cottage food laws across most U.S. states. Georgia's H-B three-ninety-eight, effective July first twenty-twenty-five, lets you sell direct, online, at farmers markets, and through consignment. No permit required. Michigan allows the same up to fifty thousand dollars in annual sales without registering with the state agriculture department.

The objection I hear most: I can't grow enough to make it worth it. Here's the honest answer. You probably won't replace your income with five balcony containers. That's not the claim. But a gardener in Ohio ran eight gotu kola and lemon balm containers through one season. By fall, she had roughly two pounds of dried herb ready to sell at a local market. At reported market rates, that's a real return on a fifty-dollar setup investment. And her neighbors started asking what was in those little kraft bags. That's the proof in the pudding.

Lemon balm and tulsi sharing a container in partial shade, both in strong tea-market demand. Ashwagandha in the driest, sunniest pot you've got. The lazier you are, the better the root. And gotu kola in the shaded corner you thought was useless. Work smarter, not harder. That's Mother Nature's way of running a tight operation.
