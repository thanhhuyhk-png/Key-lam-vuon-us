## TTS SCRIPT

A rototiller turns a garden bed into soft, fluffy powder. It looks perfect.

That fluff is broken soil. All summer, tiny fungi glue your dirt into crumbs. One pass of the tines snaps them apart. And it drags buried weed seeds up into the top two inches, where most of them sprout.

Amish farmers still plow, with horses. But one of their oldest habits points to an easier way for your backyard bed. Four fall habits. No machine to buy. Over winter, the worms do the digging.

And there's one garden where pulling out a tiller, just once, is the right call. You might be standing in it.

Here's the thing. The real work happens in fall. Spring turns into the easy part, because you mostly just plant.

But let's clear something up first, because a lot of what's online gets it wrong. Amish farms do turn their soil. In Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, one Amish farmer put it plainly to a reporter: "I know we farm with horses." That means horse-drawn equipment, not a tiller with a motor.

What's worth borrowing is one horse-era habit: putting the farm's own manure back on the land. The rest of the rhythm comes from extension research. Cut, cover, and leave the soil alone over winter.

Here's why that rhythm works. Picture two loaves of bread. One is a sponge, full of holes. The other has been ground into flour. Healthy soil is the sponge. Fungi and worms hold it together and leave holes for air, water, and roots. Tilling turns it into flour, and a hard rain can seal the surface.

Keep the sponge, and you've done most of the job. So what do you do with this summer's dead plants, if you're not turning them under?

Habit one: snip, don't pull.

Walk out to that tired tomato bed. The vines are brown and tired, and the bed looks ready for a rest. Your first thought is to yank everything out by the roots and leave the bed clean.

Instead, cut each plant at the soil line. Leave the roots right where they are. All you need is a pair of pruners. No digging.

Here's why this works. Those roots rot in place over winter and add organic matter. And as they break down, they leave little channels in the soil. Earthworms use those channels, and so does water. It's free plumbing, and you didn't lift a shovel.

Now, one exception, and it matters. If a plant had a disease, like spotted, wilting leaves, pull it and bag it. Put it in the trash, not the compost pile. Some soil diseases hang on for years, so for a few years, plant something there that isn't prone to that disease.

So here's your first step. This weekend, snip your spent plants at the soil line instead of pulling them. It takes a few minutes per bed. And yes, we're still coming to that one garden where a tiller earns its keep.

Next comes manure. And one date on your calendar decides whether it's safe for next spring's lettuce.

Habit two: manure, in fall.

Fall is the most common time to add manure to a vegetable garden. In one Missouri survey, Amish horse farmers often spread manure straight onto their fields for their crops. You can do the same on your beds. Spread it on top. No need to dig it in. Just mind the calendar. That part's coming up in a minute.

Which kinds? Cow, horse, sheep, and goat manure all work. Chicken manure is hot. It's very high in nitrogen, so it should be composted before it goes on a food garden.

And one hard rule. Never use pig, dog, or cat waste in a food garden. Not even a little. Parasites in that waste are more likely to survive and infect people. Keep pets out of the vegetable garden, too. And after handling any manure, wash your hands, and the kids' hands, for twenty seconds with hot, soapy water.

Now, the first mistake, and it's a common one. A pile that sat in the corner for a year looks like compost. It isn't. Aged manure is not the same as composted manure. Real compost heats up to between one hundred thirteen and one hundred forty degrees, and stays hot for weeks. At one hundred forty, the weed seeds in it die too. If your pile never got hot, it counts as raw.

How much? For an average garden, about an inch a year of cow or horse manure. About half an inch of chicken.

Here's the timing wall. This is the part that keeps fall manure safe. Penn State Extension passes along the USDA organic rule. Raw manure needs one hundred twenty days on the soil before you harvest anything that grows in the ground or touches it. Think carrots, radishes, and lettuce. For crops that don't touch the soil, it's ninety days. The rule is written for organic farms, and it's a smart habit for a home garden too. If you're unsure, your county extension office can help.

So count backward from your first salad. If you want lettuce in early April, raw manure should be on the bed by the end of November. Tomatoes picked in July give you a lot more room. And that's the whole calendar.

That's why fall wins. The clock runs while you sleep.

And remember that one garden where a tiller still earns its keep? We're getting there.

But bare soil has a problem. Winter rain and wind beat on it all season. It needs a blanket.

Habit three: a blanket of leaves. And this one is for anyone who rakes leaves to the curb every November.

Those bags are free mulch. Those same bags can go right onto your beds instead.

How deep? Here the guides don't quite match. Nebraska Extension says to keep eight to ten inches of mulch on beds through fall and winter. Colorado State suggests six to eight inches of shredded leaves each year. Both are home garden advice. Start with six to eight inches of shredded leaves. If yours pack down fast, go toward ten.

Now the second mistake. Whole leaves stuck together turn into a soggy mat. Water and air can't get through. So shred them first. A couple of passes with the mower over a pile will do it. That's it.

Now the third mistake. Some weeds come back from their roots. Field bindweed, Canada thistle, and dandelions can get worse when you stop tilling. Dig those out, roots and all, before the leaves go down.

Timing matters too. For a vegetable bed, the leaves can go on once your fall cleanup is done, and stay on through winter. You may have heard to wait for a few hard freezes first. That rule is meant for perennial flowers, not an empty vegetable bed.

While you're at it, beds about four feet wide work nicely. That way you can reach the middle from either side and never step on the soil. Footsteps press out the holes you're trying to keep.

Here's why the blanket works. Over winter, the leaves start to break down. Earthworms and other soil life work that organic matter into the soil below. In one field comparison, ground that wasn't plowed held about twice as many earthworms. The worms do the digging, and they work for free.

And the cost? The leaves fell on your yard. A rototiller is something you'd have to buy or rent.

So come April, you'll be staring at a deep pile of leaves. How do you plant in that? And the weeds that show up will tell you if it worked.

Habit four: plant right through it.

In spring, push the mulch aside where you want to plant. Make a furrow for seeds, or dig a small hole for a transplant. Once the plants are up, pull the mulch back around them.

One honest catch. A covered bed warms up more slowly in spring. So it helps to pull the mulch back a few weeks early on the rows you plant first.

Now the weed test. Most weed seeds sprout only from about the top two inches of soil. On farm fields, a plow can bring up roughly a third of the older seeds buried deeper down. A tiller in a garden is shallower, but the idea is the same. You flip buried seeds into the light.

Leave the soil still, and those deep seeds stay in the dark. A thick mulch blocks light from the ones near the top. Year by year, the weed seeds in that top layer gradually run down, as long as you don't let weeds go to seed.

So in April, look at where weeds pop up. The first year, you may still see a flush near the surface. Over the seasons, fewer should come up, and mostly at thin spots in the mulch. That's the sign it's working. Weeds pushing up from old roots don't count. That's the third mistake, and you already dug those out.

That's four. Now the garden where a tiller still makes sense.

It's a brand-new bed on hard, packed ground. Maybe old lawn, maybe a spot where cars used to park. The soil's compacted and low in organic matter.

There, tilling once can be the right call. Use it sparingly, then switch to the four habits. One pass to loosen that packed ground. Then cover it with four to six inches of organic matter, and let the worms take over.

There's one more option for a bed you've already built, and it's a job for next fall. A cover crop. Winter rye is the classic one. In Pennsylvania, for example, the window is September through the first of November, and it survives as far north as zone three. Your county extension office can give you dates for your area. It gets cut down two to four weeks before you plant in spring.

Two honest notes. First, as rye breaks down, it can tie up nitrogen for a while. So your spring crops may want a little extra feeding.

And second, a claim you'll hear online. One popular video says rye's natural chemicals block up to ninety-five percent of weeds for a month or two. Penn State's own cover crop guide says that chemical effect fades within about a week, and likely plays only a small role. Rye helps mostly the plain way. It covers the ground and blocks the light.

So here's your fall plan. This weekend, snip your spent plants at the soil line instead of pulling them. Spread your manure, and check the calendar. Shred your leaves, and tuck the beds in snug for winter. Then let the soil rest and enjoy the quiet.

Picture an April morning. You push your hand into that bed, and the soil's dark, cool, and crumbly. A worm slides out of sight. And you never started a motor.

You're the kind of gardener who lets the soil do the work.
