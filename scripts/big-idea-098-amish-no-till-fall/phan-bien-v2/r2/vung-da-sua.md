## Vùng 1
> (trước) But let's clear something up first, because a lot of what's online gets it wrong. Amish farms do turn their soil. In Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, one Amish farmer put it plainly to a reporter: "I know we farm with horses." [C07] That means horse-drawn equipment, not a tiller with a motor.
**MỚI:** What's worth borrowing is one horse-era habit. Manure from the farm's own animals goes straight onto the fields. [C45] The rest of the rhythm comes from extension research. Cut, cover, and leave the soil alone over winter. [C31, C17]
> (sau) Here's why that rhythm works. Picture two loaves of bread. One is a sponge, full of holes. The other has been ground into flour. Healthy soil is the sponge. Fungi and worms hold it together and leave holes for air, water, and roots. [C01, C05] Tilling turns it into flour, and the first hard rain seals the top. [C02, C44]

## Vùng 2
> (trước) Here's why this works. Those roots rot in place over winter and add organic matter. [C31] And as they break down, they leave little channels in the soil. Earthworms use those channels, and so does water. [C34] It's free plumbing, and you didn't lift a shovel.
**MỚI:** Now, one exception, and it matters. If a plant had a disease, like spotted, wilting leaves, pull it and bag it. Put it in the trash, not the compost pile. [C32] Some soil diseases hang on for years, so keep that crop out of that spot for a few years. [C33]
**MỚI:** So here's your first step. This weekend, snip your spent plants at the soil line instead of pulling them. It takes a few minutes per bed. And yes, we're still coming to that one garden where a tiller earns its keep.
> (sau) Next comes manure. And one date on your calendar decides whether it's safe for next spring's lettuce.

## Vùng 3
> (trước) Habit two: manure, in fall.
**MỚI:** Fall is the most common time to add manure to a vegetable garden. [C08] In one Missouri survey, Amish horse farmers more often spread manure straight onto their fields than commercial horse barns did. [C45] You can do the same on your beds. Spread it on top. No need to dig it in. [C50]
**MỚI:** Which kinds? Cow, horse, sheep, and goat manure all work. [C11] Chicken manure is hot. It's very high in nitrogen, so it should be composted before it goes on a food garden. [C12]
**MỚI:** And one hard rule. Never use pig, dog, or cat waste in a food garden. Not even a little. [C11] Parasites in that waste are more likely to survive and infect people. [C48] Keep pets out of the vegetable garden, too. And after handling any manure, wash your hands for twenty seconds with hot, soapy water. [C47]
**MỚI:** Now, the first mistake, and it's a common one. A pile that sat in the corner for a year looks like compost. It isn't. Aged manure is not the same as composted manure. [C15] Real compost heats up to between one hundred thirteen and one hundred forty degrees, and stays hot for weeks. [C13] At one hundred forty, the weed seeds in it die too. [C14] If your pile never got hot, it counts as raw.
> (sau) How much? For an average garden, about an inch a year of cow or horse manure. About half an inch of chicken. [C16]

## Vùng 4
> (trước) How much? For an average garden, about an inch a year of cow or horse manure. About half an inch of chicken. [C16]
**MỚI:** Here's the timing wall. This is the part that keeps fall manure safe. Penn State Extension passes along the USDA organic rule. Raw manure needs one hundred twenty days on the soil before you harvest anything that grows in the ground or touches it. Think carrots, radishes, and lettuce. For crops that don't touch the soil, it's ninety days. [C09, C10] The rule is written for organic farms, and it's a smart habit for a home garden too. If you're unsure, your county extension office can help.
**MỚI:** So count backward from your first salad. If you want lettuce in early April, raw manure should be on the bed by the end of November. Tomatoes picked in July give you a lot more room. And that's the whole calendar.
> (sau) That's why fall wins. The clock runs while you sleep.

**MỚI:** How deep? Here the guides don't quite match. Nebraska Extension says to keep eight to ten inches of mulch on beds through fall and winter. Colorado State suggests six to eight inches of shredded leaves each year. [C17, C20] Both are home garden advice. Six to eight inches of shredded leaves is a fair middle. If yours pack down fast, go toward ten.
> (sau) Now the second mistake. Whole leaves stuck together turn into a soggy mat. Water and air can't get through. [C19] So shred them first. A couple of passes with the mower over a pile will do it. That's it.

## Vùng 7
> (trước) Now the second mistake. Whole leaves stuck together turn into a soggy mat. Water and air can't get through. [C19] So shred them first. A couple of passes with the mower over a pile will do it. That's it.
**MỚI:** Now the third mistake. Some weeds come back from their roots, no matter how deep the mulch. Field bindweed, Canada thistle, and dandelions can get worse when you stop tilling. [C37] Dig those out, roots and all, before the leaves go down.
**MỚI:** Timing matters too. One common rule for winter mulch is to wait for a few hard freezes, once the ground is cold. In a place like Indiana, that's usually between Thanksgiving and Christmas. [C21] Treat that as a rule of thumb for a vegetable bed. And if hard freezes don't come where you live, ask your county extension office when to mulch.
> (sau) While you're at it, beds about four feet wide work nicely. That way you can reach the middle from either side and never step on the soil. [C18] Footsteps press out the holes you're trying to keep.

## Vùng 8
> (trước) While you're at it, beds about four feet wide work nicely. That way you can reach the middle from either side and never step on the soil. [C18] Footsteps press out the holes you're trying to keep.
**MỚI:** Here's why the blanket works. Over winter, the leaves start to break down. Earthworms and other soil life work that organic matter into the soil below. [C49] In one field comparison, ground that wasn't plowed held about twice as many earthworms. [C29, C30] The worms do the digging, and they work for free.
> (sau) And the cost? The leaves fell on your yard. A rototiller is something you'd have to buy or rent.

## Vùng 9
> (trước) One honest catch. A covered bed warms up more slowly in spring. So it helps to pull the mulch back a few weeks early on the rows you plant first. [C36]
**MỚI:** Now the weed test. Here's why this works. Most weed seeds sprout only from about the top two inches of soil. [C28] On farm fields, a plow can bring up roughly a third of the older seeds buried deeper down. [C27] A tiller in a garden is shallower, but the idea is the same. You flip buried seeds into the light.
> (sau) Leave the soil still, and those deep seeds stay in the dark. A thick mulch blocks light from the ones near the top. Year by year, the weed seeds in that top layer get used up, as long as you don't let weeds go to seed. [C22]

## Vùng 10
> (trước) Leave the soil still, and those deep seeds stay in the dark. A thick mulch blocks light from the ones near the top. Year by year, the weed seeds in that top layer get used up, as long as you don't let weeds go to seed. [C22]
**MỚI:** So in April, look at where weeds pop up. The first year, you may still see a flush near the surface. Over the seasons, fewer should come up, and mostly at thin spots in the mulch. That's the sign it's working. [C22, C28]
> (sau) That's four. Now the garden where a tiller still makes sense.

## Vùng 11
> (trước) It's a brand-new bed on hard, packed ground. Maybe old lawn, maybe a spot where cars used to park. The soil's compacted and low in organic matter.
**MỚI:** There, tilling once can be the right call. Use it sparingly, then switch to the four habits. [C43] One pass to loosen that packed ground. After that, let the worms take over.
**MỚI:** There's one more option for a bed you've already built, and it's a job for next fall. A cover crop. Winter rye is the classic one. In Pennsylvania, for example, the window is September through the first of November, and it survives as far north as zone three. [C40] Your county extension office can give you dates for your area. It gets cut down two to four weeks before you plant in spring. [C42]
> (sau) Two honest notes. First, as rye breaks down, it can tie up nitrogen for a while. [C35] So your spring crops may want a little extra feeding.
