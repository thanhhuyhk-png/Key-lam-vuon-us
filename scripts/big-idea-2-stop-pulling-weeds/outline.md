### MAIN CONTENT — 4 Plants (3:00–11:00)

#### Plant 1 — Mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris) ~2 min
- **What it is:** Native to Europe/Asia, naturalized across North America. Grows 3–6 ft. Silver-green leaves, distinctly aromatic.
- **Chemistry:** 1,8-cineole (46.5% of essential oil), camphor (6.2%), borneol — volatile terpenes that disperse into surrounding air.
- **Mechanism:** US Patent #6,689,397 confirms camphor and 1,8-cineole cause behavioral inhibition in snakes — "movement latency." Disrupts chemoreception (how snakes "smell" with their tongues).
- **History:** "Mother of Herbs" in Anglo-Saxon tradition. Part of Nine Herbs Charm (10th century). Planted at garden edges in folk tradition — not removed.
- **Practical tip:** INVASIVE without containment. Plant in buried container (12" deep minimum) OR install 18–24" rhizome barrier. Full sun, lean soil, skip fertilizer.
- **Transition:** "Mugwort handles the perimeter above ground. The next plant wages chemical warfare below it."

#### Plant 2 — Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) ~2 min
- **What it is:** Silver-gray feathery foliage. Zones 3–8. The plant behind absinthe — banned in many countries for decades due to potency.
- **Chemistry:** Absinthin (bitter alkaloid), thujone (terpenoid), volatile oils. Absinthin detected in rainwater runoff from leaves — disperses passively from rain.
- **Mechanism (allelopathy):** Roots secrete absinthin into soil — suppresses germination and growth of competing plants. Documented: ResearchGate "Allelopathic plants XVI: Artemisia species."
- **Snake angle:** Same terpene family as mugwort. Thujone confirmed in snake repellent literature alongside camphor and cineole.
- **Practical tip:** Plant in its own dedicated zone — allelopathic effects will suppress neighbors. Best for: garden edge, between garden and wild area, under fruit trees. Give 2–3 ft radius from anything you want to keep.
- **Transition:** "Both Artemisia plants work through smell and soil chemistry. The next one works through the sensory equivalent of sound."

#### Plant 3 — Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) ~1:30
- **What it is:** Tropical perennial, Zones 9–11 (annual in colder zones, overwinter indoors). Grows 3–5 ft, clumping habit.
- **Chemistry:** Citral (geranial + neral) and geraniol — same compounds in many commercial insect repellents.
- **Snake angle:** Cited in Toxicon (North American Society of Toxinology): citral "causes pain in snakes" and triggers avoidance. Caveat: varies by snake species — not universal.
- **Honest framing:** "The science here is promising but not as locked-down as the Artemisia compounds. What IS documented: highly effective against mosquitoes and flies — reduces pest pressure that attracts reptiles hunting prey."
- **Practical tip:** Plant in clusters of 3 along south-facing border. Dense clumps = physical + chemical barrier. Harvest stalks for cooking — pays rent multiple ways.
- **Transition:** "The first three plants protect your borders. The last one works underground — in ways we're only now beginning to map."

#### Plant 4 — Marigold (Tagetes spp.) ~2 min
- **What it is:** Everyone knows marigolds. Most think: "pretty, maybe keeps aphids away." That's a significant underestimate.
- **Chemistry:** Root exudates contain alpha-terthienyl (α-T). Published Biology Open 2019: α-T penetrates nematode hypodermis, induces oxidative stress — "one of the most toxic naturally occurring compounds found to date."
- **What it kills:** Meloidogyne (root-knot nematodes), Heterodera, Pratylenchus — the parasites that destroy tomato, pepper, squash roots silently. Effect persists in rhizosphere after plant is gone.
- **Mechanism:** Phototoxic under UV AND oxidative in dark. Disrupts cholinesterase — same enzyme targeted by some synthetic pesticides.
- **Authority:** University of Florida IFAS Extension recommends marigold as cover crop specifically for nematode management before planting vegetables.
- **Practical tip:** For nematode suppression: plant densely 1 full season BEFORE your vegetable beds. French marigold (T. patula) most studied. Let grow all season, then till in.
