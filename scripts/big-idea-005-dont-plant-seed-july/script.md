## TTS SCRIPT

Somewhere in your kitchen drawer, there's an old packet of seeds. Maybe from last spring. Maybe one your mother left behind, tucked away in a coffee tin with the rubber bands and the spare buttons.

Here's what catches a lot of gardeners off guard: those seeds could already be dead, and you'd never know it just by looking. A seed's DNA breaks down slowly in storage, year after year, whether the outside looks fine or not — and once that damage sets in, it can't be undone.

But here's the good news: one damp paper towel can tell you the truth in a few days, before you waste your last good week to plant this July.

Here's the thing: the test itself is almost funny in how simple it is. One paper towel. Ten seeds. A zip bag. That's it. But here's the part almost nobody gets right: some seeds show their true colors in three days flat. Others need three full weeks before they'll say a word. Get that timing wrong, and you'll toss out seeds that were never dead to begin with. We'll walk through both, so you don't make that call too soon.

One quick safety note: keep the test bag out of reach of kids and pets while it sits out. Plain vegetable seeds usually aren't a big danger in tiny amounts, but there's no reason to take a chance.

Here's the setup. Get a paper towel thoroughly damp, not dripping — if water runs out when you squeeze it, wring it out a bit. Lay ten seeds along the bottom edge, spaced apart so they're not touching, fold the towel over them, then roll it up like a little log. Slide it into a zip bag, left open just a crack for air. Set that bag somewhere warm and steady — around seventy degrees, give or take. Not a sunny windowsill, not a cold basement shelf. That's the whole setup, maybe five minutes, and you already had everything you needed sitting in a drawer.

Now here's why this works, and it's actually pretty fascinating biology. Seeds don't die the way a plant wilts or a leaf browns at the edges — there's no warning sign on the outside. Inside, though, the seed's DNA and cell membranes are slowly breaking down, year after year. Fats oxidize, sort of like oil going rancid on a shelf, and enzymes stop working the way they should. Once that damage adds up, it can't be undone. Think of it like an old flashlight battery sitting in a junk drawer. Same size, same shine, nothing wrong on the outside — but the second you try to use it, nothing happens. That's a seed with age catching up to it. That's exactly why this little test matters more than whatever the packet promises.

So how long can a seed really hold on before it's done for good? That depends completely on what's sitting in that packet, and the answer might surprise you. University extension research on seed storage shows cucumber seeds, give or take, are still good after five years. Tomato seeds last about four. Carrots and beans usually hold on for around three. Peppers and onions fade quicker, often down to just one or two years. And lettuce is the one where sources don't quite agree — some say a year, others say longer. Check your own packet's date, and when in doubt, just run the test.

Here's why July makes all of this feel urgent. If you're planting for a fall harvest, you're generally working with about ninety days before the first frost shows up in most regions. That's not a lot of room for error — every seed that doesn't come up is a few square feet of your garden sitting empty while that window keeps closing.

Not every seed moves at the same speed, and that's what trips people up more than anything else. Radish, lettuce, cucumber, and squash are quick — you can see a little white root poking through in as few as three days. Beans and tomatoes typically need six to eight days; don't count them out at day four. Carrots are slower, anywhere from six days out to seventeen. And peppers, the slowest of the bunch, can need two to three full weeks before they sprout, even in warm, ideal conditions.

Picture this: a neighbor hands you down a jar of old bean seeds from their own garden. You check at day three, see nothing, and toss the whole jar. Here's the problem: beans need six to eight days, minimum. Perfectly good seeds, gone, just because the clock ran out too soon. And the second common slip is trusting how a seed looks instead of running the test. A seed can be plump and perfectly colored and still be dead on the inside. Looks lie. The paper towel doesn't. Ask any extension agent — they'll tell you the same story every single spring.

Now let's talk about reading your results, because this is what actually saves your garden, not just your seeds. Count how many of your ten seeds sent out a root and a little shoot. Seven or more, that's seventy percent or better, and your seeds are strong — go ahead and plant that packet exactly the way the instructions say. In the fifty to seventy percent range, the seeds are still usable, just a little tired — sow them heavier than normal to make up for the ones that won't come up. But if fewer than half sprouted, that packet has probably given all it's got, and spending three to five dollars on a fresh packet beats gambling your whole fall garden on seeds that are already struggling.

Here's a real example. Say you tested ten old pepper seeds and waited the full three weeks like you should. Only four came up — that's forty percent, well under the line. Now you know, for a fact, to pick up a fresh packet instead of hoping for the best and finding out too late that half your bed is empty.

Here's a fun bit of trivia worth knowing: this exact test is what extension offices across the country recommend when someone calls in asking if a seed's still any good. It's not a grandma trick pulled out of thin air — it's the same method university extension programs teach, just scaled down to fit right on your kitchen counter.

One thing worth clearing up: this test is about seeds going stale in storage from age. That's a completely different problem from seeds that won't sprout because the soil outside is too hot when you plant them. And no matter what USDA zone you're gardening in, this test works exactly the same way — the only thing that changes is how much of that ninety-day fall window you've actually got left.

Before you go, here's the bonus that'll save you seeds for years to come. If your test comes back strong, store any extra seeds somewhere cool and dry, not a hot garage or a humid shed. Extension research uses a simple rule of thumb: add your storage temperature in Fahrenheit to your humidity percentage, and keep that total under one hundred. Sixty degrees and thirty percent humidity, for example, adds up to ninety — safe territory. And here's a fun number worth holding onto: every time you drop the temperature by ten degrees, or cut the moisture by just one percent, you roughly double how long that seed stays viable. A sealed jar tucked into a cool closet or the fridge will treat your seeds far better than a plastic bag on a shelf — and keep them away from ripening fruit on the counter, since the gas it gives off can quietly shorten a seed's life.

So here's where that leaves you. That packet sitting in your drawer isn't a mystery anymore. Grab ten seeds tonight, wrap them in a damp paper towel, and give them their fair amount of time to show you what they've got.

Test the seeds, trust the numbers, save the season. Happy gardening.
