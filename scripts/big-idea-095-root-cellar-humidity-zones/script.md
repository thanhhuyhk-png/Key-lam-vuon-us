## TTS SCRIPT

Open the crate in March, and one vegetable is rotten while the next one is perfect. That isn't luck. It's the air. Carrots want wet air, ninety-eight to one hundred percent humidity. Onions want dry air, sixty-five to seventy. Put them on one shelf, and one of them loses.

That's why a root cellar isn't one room. It's three zones. Cold and wet, for carrots, beets and cabbage. Cold and dry, for onions and garlic. And a damp middle zone for one vegetable that needs a spot of its own.

You don't need a cellar. A buried can and a cool corner of the shed can stand in for one.

But that middle vegetable has a problem, and it's the fruit people store right beside it.

Here's the thing about cold. These vegetables all want about the same range, thirty-two to forty degrees. Get the air right inside that range, and the numbers get big. Mature carrots can last seven to nine months. Late-crop potatoes, five to ten. Dry onions, anywhere from one to eight. At the top of those ranges, that's a whole winter, and then some. You'll hear videos promise a full year. The charts put the ceiling closer to eight months for onions, and ten for potatoes. So plan on spring, and count anything past that as a bonus.

So if the cold is the same, what changes? Moisture. Carrots, beets and cabbage want air that's almost wet. Onions and garlic want it dry. One crop sits in the middle. Three levels of moisture. Three zones.

Here's why this works. A shelf has only one kind of air, and everything on it gets that air. Two vegetables that need opposite air can't share it.

Here's a one-minute job. Hang a thermometer in the garage, enclosed porch or shed tonight, and look for the spot that stays between thirty-two and forty degrees. That's it. The thermometer is the only tool you need. Check it again after your first hard freeze. If it dips below thirty-two, that spot won't work for vegetables. A spot that holds that range is where your cellar goes.

Knowing the three zones is the easy part. The hard part is making the air almost wet inside a plain plastic tub.

Wet zone first. This is where carrots, beets, parsnips and cabbage live. They all want about thirty-two degrees and ninety-eight to one hundred percent humidity. Cabbage holds five to six months. Beets and parsnips, four to six. Carrots go longest. Turnips sit on the border, a touch drier at ninety-five percent. The edge of the wet zone suits them nicely.

Here's how to get that wet air. Start by cutting the tops off your carrots. Beets get the same treatment. Then bury the carrots in moist sand. The sand holds moisture right up against the carrots, so the air around them stays damp. Think of it as a damp towel wrapped around each one.

No sand? Don't panic. Sawdust works just as well for a big carrot harvest. All you need is a plastic tub and something to bury the carrots in.

In the corner of the shed, a plastic tub sits full of moist sand, with carrots tucked down inside. It looks like nothing at all. It's a wet zone.

Cabbage wants the same wet air. Give it the coldest, dampest spot you have.

Wet is only one zone. The onions in the next zone over want the exact opposite. And in the middle sits one crop that needs a warm, dark start before it ever goes into the cold. It's the same crop that has a problem with the fruit beside it.

Now the dry zone. Onions and garlic live here, at sixty-five to seventy percent humidity. That's the opposite of the carrots. And it's the reason the shelf plan breaks. Put onions in that wet carrot air, and they're in the wrong zone from day one.

Put the two zones side by side on the chart, and one rule of thumb falls out. Match the air first. Then pick the neighbors.

Before onions go in, they have to cure. Cure them in a warm, dry, airy spot, in a single layer on a screen. One guide says two to three weeks. Another says up to four. Garlic gets a shaded, airy spot, laid out or tied in bunches. Then move both to the cool, dry zone. Onions get a warm start too, but out in the air. The one that needs the dark is still coming.

And if your onions have ever gone soft by February, skipping the cure could be why.

February morning. You reach into the basket, and the onions feel dry and firm. That's what a dry zone buys you.

Keep those onion and garlic racks out of reach of pets. The ASPCA lists onions and garlic as toxic to dogs and cats.

Once it's dry, garlic holds six to seven months. That's a long stretch for a vegetable that costs you almost nothing to store.

That leaves the vegetable in the middle. And the neighbor you've been waiting on.

Last, the damp middle. This one is the potato. Potatoes want ninety to ninety-five percent humidity, and about forty degrees. A few guides put them closer to forty-five. Aim for about forty. That's the warm end of the range, so potatoes get the warmest of your cold spots. Go below that, and they can start to taste sweet. If that happens, a few days at room temperature brings the flavor back.

Here's the warm start I promised. Before potatoes go cold, they cure. Fifty to sixty degrees, in the dark, with air moving, for one to two weeks. Then move them to the cold. Do that, and the five-to-ten-month number is within reach for late-crop potatoes.

Dark matters for another reason. Light turns potatoes green, and green means a toxin called solanine may be present. Throw out the green ones. Toss them where kids and pets can't reach them. If you're unsure whether a potato is safe to eat, your local extension office can help.

Now the neighbor. Apples. Apples want ninety to ninety-five percent humidity too. Some varieties keep just a few months. Others last all winter. That's the same humidity as potatoes, which is why they end up on the same shelf. Same air, so it seems like a natural fit.

It isn't. Apples give off ethylene, a gas that ripening fruit releases. Potatoes start sprouting when it's around. Pears, plums and melons give it off too. And that same gas can turn carrots bitter. Cabbage is often affected by ethylene too. So keep apples and pears away from potatoes, carrots and cabbage.

It works the other way, too. Potatoes can make apples taste musty. So keep them in separate spaces.

Same air, bad neighbors. That's the trap, and now you know where it is.

One more pairing to settle. Can potatoes and onions share a bin? They both like it cool, but they want very different air. So give them separate spots.

Picture opening the bin in January. The potatoes are dark, cool and firm. The apples are somewhere else entirely. That's the whole idea.

Everything so far assumes you've got somewhere cold and dark. What if your house has no cellar at all?

You've got three options. None of them needs a dug cellar.

Option one, a buried can. A twenty-gallon metal or plastic can sunk into the ground works as a cellar. Use a clean one that's never held chemicals. Drill a few small drainage holes in the bottom. Keep the lid tight, because rodents can squeeze through a hole the size of a dime. The research we have doesn't say how deep to bury it, or how well it works where winters get bitter cold. Check for buried lines before you dig. Then keep a thermometer in it. If it heads toward freezing, move the vegetables to your coolest spot indoors. A tight lid helps keep little hands and pets out, too.

Picture a fall Saturday, before the ground freezes, a shovel, and one metal can. That's a cold spot in the yard.

Option two, an unheated space. An unheated back hall, enclosed porch or shed can be adapted for cold storage. Vents are the trick. Open them when it's colder outside than inside, but not when it's freezing out. Shut them tight when it's warmer outside. Keep an eye on them when the weather swings, because the rule flips with it. Dry air in there? A pan of water on the floor adds moisture. So does wet burlap. Just keep that damp air away from the onions.

Here's how all three zones could sit in one unheated space. The wet zone is a tub of moist sand. The dry zone is a screen or shelf with good airflow, set well away from anything damp. The damp zone is a covered, dark bin that still breathes, with the potatoes in perforated plastic bags. The room air is the dry zone, so skip the pan of water when onions share the room. The sand and the bin hold the moisture. Three jobs, three spots, no cellar.

Option three, a cool basement corner, if your home has a basement. Some basements hold between forty and fifty degrees in winter. That's warmer than ideal, so check what's stored there more often.

Whichever you pick, the goal is cold, not frozen. That's why the thermometer comes first.

You've got the zones and the space. What's left is the order to set it all up.

Three mistakes can sink a cellar. One shelf for everything. Apples next to potatoes. And no thermometer to tell you what's going on. The fix for all three is an order of work.

Here's the order. First, the thermometer. If you haven't yet, hang a thermometer in the garage, enclosed porch or shed tonight, and look for the spot that stays between thirty-two and forty degrees. A tub, some sand and a thermometer. That's most of the shopping list.

Second, sort by zone. Carrots go in moist sand. Beets, parsnips and cabbage take the coldest, dampest spot you have. Onions get cured warm and dry, garlic in a shaded, airy spot. Then both move to the cool, dry zone. Potatoes get cured in the dark, then stay dark and cold. Apples go somewhere else.

Third, check once a week. Pull out anything rotten or moldy, so it doesn't spread.

By next weekend, the zones can be set up and labeled. Once the curing is done, they fill. By March, you'll open that crate expecting good news.

Match the air. Pick the neighbors. Check once a week.
