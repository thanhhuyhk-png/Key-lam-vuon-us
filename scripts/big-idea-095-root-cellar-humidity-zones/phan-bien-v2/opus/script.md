Open the crate in March, and one vegetable is rotten while the next one is perfect. That isn't luck. It's the air. Carrots want wet air, ninety-eight to one hundred percent humidity. [C01] Onions want dry air, sixty-five to seventy. [C03] Put them on one shelf, and one of them loses.

That's why a root cellar isn't one room. It's three zones. Cold and wet, for carrots, beets and cabbage. Cold and dry, for onions and garlic. And a damp middle zone for one vegetable that needs a spot of its own.

You don't need a cellar. A buried can and a cool corner of the garage can stand in for one. [C26, C28]

### M1 · The one rule

Here's the thing about cold. These vegetables all want about the same range, thirty-two to forty degrees. [C01–C09] Get the air right inside that range, and the numbers get big. Mature carrots can last seven to nine months. [C25] Late-crop potatoes, five to ten. [C02] Dry onions, anywhere from one to eight. [C03] That's a whole winter, and then some. You'll hear videos promise a full year. The charts put the ceiling closer to eight months for onions, and ten for potatoes. [C03, C02] So plan on spring, and count anything past that as a bonus.

So if the cold is the same, what changes? Moisture. Carrots, beets and cabbage want air that's almost wet. [C01, C05, C06] Onions and garlic want it dry. [C03, C04] One crop sits in the middle. Three levels of moisture. Three zones.

Here's why this works. A shelf has only one kind of air, and everything on it gets that air. Two vegetables that need opposite air can't share it.

Here's a one-minute job. Hang a thermometer in the garage, porch or shed tonight, and look for the spot that stays between thirty-two and forty degrees. [C28] That's it. No tools needed. If that spot dips below thirty-two on your coldest nights, it isn't ready yet. Keep looking. That spot is where your cellar goes.

Knowing the three zones is the easy part. The hard part is making the air almost wet inside a plain plastic tub.

### M2 · The wet zone

Wet zone first. This is where carrots, beets, parsnips and cabbage live. They all want about thirty-two degrees and ninety-eight to one hundred percent humidity. [C01, C05, C06, C07] Cabbage holds five to six months. [C05] Beets and parsnips, four to six. [C06, C07] Carrots go longest. Turnips sit on the border, a touch drier at ninety-five percent. [C08] The edge of the wet zone suits them nicely.

Here's how to get that wet air. Start by cutting the tops off your carrots. [C24] Beets get the same treatment. [C06] Then bury the carrots in sand or sawdust, and keep it moist. [C31] The sand holds moisture right up against the carrots, so the air around them stays damp. Think of it as a wet towel wrapped around each one.

Don't panic if you haven't got sand. Sawdust works too, and either one is the way to go for a big carrot harvest. [C31] All you need is a plastic tub and something to bury the carrots in.

In the corner of the shed, a plastic tub sits full of moist sand, with carrots tucked down inside. It looks like nothing at all. It's a wet zone.

Cabbage wants the same wet air. Give it the coldest, dampest spot you have. [C05]

Wet is only one zone. The onions on the other side of the shelf want the exact opposite. And in the middle sits one crop that needs a warm, dark start before it ever goes into the cold. It's the same crop that has a problem with the fruit beside it.

### M3 · The dry zone

Now the dry zone. Onions and garlic live here, at sixty-five to seventy percent humidity. [C03, C04] That's the opposite of the carrots. And it's the reason the shelf plan breaks. Put onions in that wet carrot air, and they're in the wrong zone from day one.

### M4 · The damp middle

Last, the damp middle. This one is the potato. Potatoes want ninety to ninety-five percent humidity, and about forty degrees. [C02, C20] A few guides put them closer to forty-five. [D1] Aim for about forty. Go below that, and potatoes can start to taste sweet. [C35] If that happens, a few days at room temperature brings the flavor back. [C35]

Here's the warm start I promised. Before potatoes go cold, they cure. Fifty to sixty degrees, in the dark, with air moving, for one to two weeks. [C18, C19] Then move them to the cold. [C20] Do that, and the five-to-ten-month number is within reach. [C02]

Dark matters for another reason. Light turns potatoes green, and green means a toxin called solanine may be present. [C33] Throw out the green ones. Toss them where kids and pets can't reach them. If you're unsure whether a potato is safe to eat, your local extension office can help.

Now the neighbor. Apples. Apples want ninety to ninety-five percent humidity too. [C09] Some varieties keep just a few months. Others last all winter. [D5] That's the same humidity as potatoes, which is why they end up on the same shelf. Same air, so it seems like a natural fit.

[C13] And that same gas can turn carrots bitter. [C14, C16] Cabbage is often affected by ethylene too. [C14] So keep apples away from potatoes. [C11, C12, C15]

It works the other way, too. Potatoes can make apples taste musty. [D6] So keep them in separate spaces. [C15]

Same air, bad neighbors. That's the trap, and now you know where it is.

One more pairing to settle. Can potatoes and onions share a bin? They both like it cool, but they want very different air. [C02, C03] So give them separate spots.

Everything so far assumes you've got somewhere cold and dark. What if your house has no cellar at all?

### M5 · No cellar? Three stand-ins

You've got three options, and extension services list the first two. None of them needs a dug cellar. [C26, C28]

Option one, a buried can. A twenty-gallon metal or plastic can sunk into the ground works as a cellar. [C26] Use a clean one that's never held chemicals. Drill a few small drainage holes in the bottom. [C27] Keep the lid tight, because rodents can squeeze through a hole the size of a dime. [C27] The research we have doesn't say how deep to bury it, or how it holds up in the harshest winters. So keep a thermometer in it, and keep it from freezing. A tight lid helps keep little hands and pets out, too.

Picture a Saturday morning, a shovel, and one metal can. By lunch, you've got a cold spot in the yard.

Option two, an unheated space. An unheated back hall, enclosed porch or shed can be adapted for cold storage. [C28] Vents are the trick. Open them when it's colder outside than inside, and shut them tight when it's warmer outside. [C29] Keep an eye on them when the weather swings, because the rule flips with it. Dry air in there? A pan of water on the floor adds moisture. So does wet burlap. [C30] Just keep that damp air away from the onions.

Here's how all three zones could sit in one unheated space. The wet zone is a tub of moist sand. [C31] The dry zone is a screen or shelf with good airflow, set well away from anything damp. [C22] The damp zone is a closed, dark bin, with the potatoes in perforated plastic bags. [C30, C33] The bin keeps that damp air to itself. Three jobs, three spots, no cellar.

Option three, a cool basement corner, if your home has a basement. Some basements hold between forty and fifty degrees in winter. [C32] That's warmer than ideal, so check what's stored there more often.

Whichever you pick, the goal is cold, not frozen. That's why the thermometer comes first.

You've got the zones and the space. What's left is the order to set it all up.
