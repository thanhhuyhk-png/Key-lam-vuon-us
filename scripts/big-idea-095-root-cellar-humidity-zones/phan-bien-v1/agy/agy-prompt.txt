Open the crate in March, and one vegetable is rotten while the next one is perfect. That isn't luck. It's the air. Carrots want wet air, ninety-eight to one hundred percent humidity. [C01] Onions want dry air, sixty-five to seventy. [C03] Put them on one shelf, and one of them loses.

That's why a root cellar isn't one room. It's three zones. Cold and wet, for carrots, beets and cabbage. Cold and dry, for onions and garlic. And a damp middle zone for one vegetable that needs a spot of its own.

You don't need a cellar. A buried can and a cool corner of the garage can stand in for one. [C26, C28]

But that middle vegetable has a problem, and it's the fruit people store right beside it. [C09]

### M1 · The one rule

Here's the thing about cold. Almost everything you'll store wants the same range, thirty-two to forty degrees. [C01–C09] Get the air right inside that range, and the numbers get big. Carrots can last seven to nine months. [C25] Potatoes, five to ten. [C02] Dry onions, up to eight. [C03] That's a whole winter, and then some. You'll hear videos promise a full year. The charts put the ceiling closer to eight months for onions, and ten for potatoes. [C03, C02] So plan on spring, not next summer.

So if the cold is the same, what changes? Moisture. Carrots, beets and cabbage want air that's almost wet, ninety-eight to one hundred percent. [C01, C05, C06] Potatoes want ninety to ninety-five. [C02] Onions and garlic want sixty-five to seventy. [C03, C04] Three levels of moisture. Three zones.

Here's why this works. A shelf has only one kind of air, and everything on it gets that air. Two vegetables that need opposite air can't share it.

Here's a one-minute job. Hang a thermometer in the garage, porch or shed tonight, and look for the spot that stays between thirty-two and forty degrees. [C28] That's it. No tools needed. That spot is where your cellar goes.

Knowing the three zones is the easy part. The hard part is making the air almost wet inside a plain plastic tub.

### M2 · The wet zone

Wet zone first. This is where carrots, beets, parsnips and cabbage live. They all want about thirty-two degrees and ninety-eight to one hundred percent humidity. [C01, C05, C06, C07] Cabbage holds five to six months. [C05] Beets and parsnips, four to six. [C06, C07] Carrots go longest, seven to nine. [C25] Turnips sit on the border. They want a touch less, ninety-five percent, and keep four to five months. [C08] The edge of the wet zone suits them nicely.

Here's how to get that wet air. Start by cutting the tops off your carrots. [C24] Then bury them in sand or sawdust, and keep it moist. [C31] The sand holds moisture right up against the carrots, so the air around them stays damp. Think of it as a wet towel wrapped around each one.

Don't panic if you haven't got sand. Sawdust works too. [C31] It's the way to go if you've got a lot of carrots to store. [C31] All you need is a plastic tub and something to bury the vegetables in.

Picture it. A plastic tub in the corner of the garage, filled with moist sand, with carrots tucked down inside it. It looks like nothing at all. It's a wet zone.

Cabbage wants the same wet air. Give it the coldest, dampest spot you have. [C05]

Wet is half the story. The onions on the other side of the shelf want the exact opposite. And in the middle sits one vegetable that needs a warm start before it ever goes into the cold.

### M4 · The damp middle

Last, the damp middle. This one is the potato. Potatoes want ninety to ninety-five percent humidity, and about forty degrees or a little less. [C02, C20] A few guides put them closer to forty-five. [D1] If your spot allows it, the colder end is a nice goal. Much colder than forty, though, and potatoes can turn sweet. [C35] If that happens, a few days at room temperature brings the flavor back. Same humidity, opposite needs. That's the trap, and now you know where it is.

One more pairing to settle. Can potatoes and onions share a bin? They want the same cold, but not the same air. Potatoes want ninety to ninety-five percent. Onions want sixty-five to seventy. [C02, C03] So give them separate spots.

### M5 · No cellar? Three stand-ins

You've got three options. It's an old trick that still works.

Option one, a buried can. A twenty-gallon metal or plastic can sunk into the ground works as a cellar. [C26] Drill a few small drainage holes in the bottom. [C27] Keep the lid tight, because rodents can squeeze through a hole the size of a dime. [C27] Bury it so the top sits just above the ground, and pile straw on the lid. A tight lid keeps little hands and pets out, too.

Picture a Saturday morning, a shovel, and one metal can. By lunch, you've got a wet zone in the yard.

Option two, an unheated space. An unheated back hall, enclosed porch or shed can be adapted for cold storage. [C28] Vents are the trick. Open them when it's colder outside than inside, and shut them tight when it's warmer outside. [C29] Keep an eye on them when the weather swings, because the rule flips with it. Dry air in there? A pan of water on the floor adds moisture. So does wet burlap. [C30] That's a damp zone, built from a pan.

Here's how all three zones could sit in one garage. The wet zone is a tub of moist sand. [C31] The dry zone is a screen or shelf with good airflow. [C22] The damp zone is a dark box with a pan of water beside it. [C30, C33] Three jobs, three spots, no cellar.

Option three, a cool basement corner. Some basements hold between forty and fifty degrees in winter. [C32] That's warmer than ideal, so check what's stored there more often.

Whichever you pick, the goal is cold, not frozen. That's why the thermometer comes first.

You've got the zones and the space. What's left is the order to set it all up.

Three mistakes can sink a cellar. One shelf for everything. Apples next to potatoes. And no thermometer to tell you what's going on. The fix for all three is an order of work.
