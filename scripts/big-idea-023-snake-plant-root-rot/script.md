## TTS SCRIPT

Go touch your snake plant right now, right where the base meets the soil. If it feels even a little soft, here's the uncomfortable truth: root rot is still the number one reason this "unkillable" plant dies. Your grandmother's snake plant probably survived twenty years of neglect — dark corners, weeks with no water, total forgetfulness. It's almost unfair, honestly. The plant that's supposed to forgive everything, undone by five habits that actually look like good care.

Here's the strange part: it's usually not neglect that does it. Root rot's quiet about it, too — it starves the roots of oxygen underground, and by the time the leaves show it, the damage below is often already done.

Five habits are behind it: the pot, where the water lands, the soil mix, the light in the room, and the clock you water by. The first one is probably sitting under your plant right now — the pot itself.

Mistake one is a pot that's too big. It holds moisture far longer than the roots actually need. Picture a small root ball sitting in a big block of soil, staying damp for days after the roots nearby already had their fill. That leftover dampness is exactly what root rot is looking for. Here's the fix: only size up one to two inches in diameter, never more. A snake plant actually does better a little snug in its pot. It's the one time bigger isn't better, and it costs you less potting mix, not more.

Mistake two happens right at the base of the leaves. When water goes straight into that tight center rosette instead of the soil, it sits there and stays damp a lot longer. Many gardeners and plant clinics report this is often the first place trouble shows up — that constant dampness at the crown just isn't a friendly spot for a snake plant. Fix: water the soil off to the side, and let the crown stay bone dry.

Mistake three is the soil itself. Regular potting mix holds moisture for thirsty houseplants — the exact opposite of what a snake plant wants. Switch to a fast-draining cactus or succulent mix with extra perlite mixed in, and water moves through in seconds instead of pooling around the roots for days. A bag of cactus mix costs about the same as a bag of regular potting soil — this isn't a money upgrade, it's just the right tool for the job.

Here's why those three actually connect. Picture the soil like a bathtub. When it drains fast, air moves back in behind the water. When it can't drain, that tub stays full, and the roots sit there like they're holding their breath. That waterlogged, airless pocket is exactly what fungi like Pythium and Phytophthora need to spread — some even produce spores that swim through standing water to reach the roots. No standing water, no way for them to travel. Every one of these fungi needs the same thing to survive. Kill the standing water, and you've beaten all of them at once.

Mistake four is watering the same amount in a dim corner as you would in a bright spot. Low light slows everything down — the plant pulls up less water, and the soil stays damp much longer. The same routine that's perfectly fine on a sunny windowsill can end up drowning a plant in a dark hallway. Same water, same schedule, completely different outcome, because the room changed, not the plant.

Mistake five is watering on a fixed schedule instead of checking the soil. Snake plants are succulents — they store water right in those thick leaves. Push a finger two inches down, and if it's dry all the way, that's your cue. Some months that means going a full month without watering, and that's completely fine. Extension hotlines and plant clinics hear this same story every year: a healthy-looking snake plant collapses, and the owner insists they were watering "responsibly" the whole time. Almost always, "responsibly" just meant "on schedule," not "when it actually needed it."

None of these five habits look like neglect. They all look like care — that's exactly why root rot catches so many people off guard. Here's how to catch it early, since the leaves are usually the last thing to show trouble: a base that feels softer than usual, leaves that pull away with almost no resistance, or a sour, swampy smell from the pot. Any one of those, and it's time for the rescue.

One safety note first, because it matters for plenty of households: snake plants are toxic to both dogs and cats. The concern is saponins in the leaves, and a pet that chews on one can get vomiting, nausea, or diarrhea. That risk goes up during a rescue, since you'll have cut roots and loose soil sitting out in the open for a while. Keep pets and kids away from the mess while you work, and if you ever suspect a pet or curious toddler ate part of the plant, call ASPCA Animal Poison Control at eight eight eight, four two six, four four three five.

Now the rescue. Take the plant out of its pot and rinse the soil away from the roots so you can see what you're working with. Any root that's black, brown, or mushy needs to go — cut back to healthy, firm, white root, using scissors you've cleaned first with rubbing alcohol.

Some gardeners dust the cut ends with cinnamon powder. Here's why this works: a twenty twenty-five lab study found a five percent cinnamon bark extract suppressed the fungus Fusarium sporotrichioides by seventy percent after seven days, and separate research in the European Journal of Plant Pathology found a one percent cinnamon rinse cut Botrytis mold growth by over eighty percent after six days. So the antifungal effect is real, not just folklore. But cinnamon isn't a rooting hormone — it won't make new roots grow faster. What it can do is help protect that freshly cut surface while the plant heals on its own.

Let the roots air-dry for a while, then repot into a clean container with fresh, fast-draining mix — the same cactus and perlite blend from before. Don't reuse the old soil; it's often still holding the exact fungus that caused the problem.

Doesn't matter what USDA zone you're in or what the climate's like outside — this is entirely an indoor problem, so all five habits apply the same way everywhere.

So here's where that leaves you. A snake plant's "unkillable" reputation is mostly true, right up until one of these five habits quietly works against it. Check your pot size, keep water off the crown, use a fast-draining mix, adjust for low light, and water by the soil, not the calendar. None of it costs extra money — it's just a handful of small habits pointed in the right direction.

Go take a look at yours tonight. Feel the soil, check the pot, give that crown a quick look — you'll know within a minute.

Fix the habit, save the plant, keep it thriving for years. Happy gardening.
