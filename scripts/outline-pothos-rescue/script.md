If your pothos is wilting and turning yellow, even though the soil still feels wet — here's the twist. More water is not the fix. Try this — push a finger one to two inches into that soil. If it's still damp down there, watering again could finish this plant off by next week. That thought alone is enough to make any plant parent's stomach drop. In the next few minutes, I'll walk you through five steps that give it the best shot — even when the roots have gone black in places.

Here's the part that trips up almost everyone. Pothos can wilt and turn yellow even while the soil is still soaking wet — the leaves lie about what's actually wrong. University of Maryland Extension puts it simply: if the soil feels damp but the plant still looks sick, the fix isn't more water — it's checking the roots instead. Healthy roots are white or cream and firm. Rotted roots turn brown to black and feel mushy. The outer layer slides right off if you pinch it — that's the tell-tale sign of Pythium. You might also notice brown, water-soaked spots on the leaves, or a stem that's gone soft right where it meets the soil. None of that means it's over — it just means we need to move quickly, together.

A neighbor of mine swears her pothos survived a full decade of neglect. Turns out the fungus that killed potato crops across Ireland in the 1840s is cousin to the fungus family rotting your roots today. Back in 1846, a botanist named Miles Berkeley first guessed a fungus was behind the disaster — and got laughed at, because nobody believed a tiny organism could take down entire fields overnight. Wild, right? Thirty years later, a scientist named Heinrich de Bary proved him right. Different scale, same trick: too much water, not enough air. Let's fix that together.

Step one starts with your hands. But first, a quick heads-up: pothos sap has calcium oxalate crystals in it. Wash up after handling it, and keep cuttings away from curious pets and grandkids. If anyone does put some in their mouth, a quick call to the vet or a poison control line is the safe move. Okay, into the sink it goes. This takes about ten minutes — no fancy tools, just a sink and a little patience. You'll gently pull the plant out of its pot and rinse the roots under lukewarm water so you can see what you're working with. Now you'll want to take a good look at the roots. White or light tan and firm means healthy. Black, mushy, and smelly means rot. That's it — no guessing required.

Once you can see the damage, the next cut you make decides whether this plant makes it. The sooner you catch this, the better your odds — once this fungus gets a real head start, even cutting away every trace of rot might not be enough to bring the plant back.

Step two: you'll want scissors or shears, sterilized first with rubbing alcohol. The move here is to cut every root that's mushy or dark, going at least one inch above the damaged area to make sure you get it all. Here's the thing — leaving even a little rot behind is the number one reason these rescues fail. When in doubt, cut more than feels necessary.

Cutting the rot away is only half of it. What happens to the leaves next matters just as much.

Step three, and this one's easy to skip: the move is to trim back about a third of the vine too. Fewer roots can't support as many leaves, so cutting some back gives the roots less work while they recover. It feels like you're doing less to save the plant, you know, but you're actually giving it a better shot.

But before you repot this thing, there's one more spot that decides everything: the stem right at the soil line. Is it still firm, or has it gone soft and dark too? Hold onto that question. We'll come back to it.

Step four is fresh soil, and it's the step most people skip. Pothos need a mix that drains fast — a soilless potting blend with perlite mixed in works well, in a pot with real drainage holes. A bag of perlite costs next to nothing — dirt-cheap compared to buying a whole new plant. Here's why this actually works, and it's kind of satisfying once it clicks. Extension offices across the country explain it differently, but they all land on the same point. It's not really the water that kills the plant. It's the oxygen. When soil stays soaked, air gets pushed out of the root zone, and that's exactly when rot fungus takes hold. Think of it like breathing through a wet towel pressed over your face — roots need air just as much as they need water. That's the whole reason repotting matters even more than trimming — you can cut away all the rot in the world, but if the roots go right back into soggy soil, the rot just starts over. Get the drainage right, and the proof's in the pudding: fewer black roots, more new white ones.

Getting the soil right only works if you change one more habit.

Step five, and it's the easiest one: water less often, not more. You'll go back to that same finger test before every watering — one to two inches down, and if it's still damp, wait. Pothos can go a week or more between waterings and still be perfectly happy. Folks who've kept a pothos alive for twenty years will tell you the same thing extension research keeps confirming: less water beats more, in most cases.

Three mistakes trip people up more than anything else here. First, leaving even a little rotted root behind, thinking it's close enough — it rarely is. Second, skipping the stem check completely and repotting a plant that was already too far gone. Third, watering again right after repotting, out of habit, before the roots have had a chance to settle. A few days of patience goes a long way, and honestly, it's the cheapest tool in this whole process.

Now, back to that stem. If it's soft or mushy right at the soil line, here's the honest answer: that plant likely isn't coming back. Multiple extension sources agree — once rot's gotten that far, the kindest thing is to let it go. No need to panic, though — this isn't the end of the story. If you've still got a healthy vine, you'll want to cut a length with at least one node, strip the lower leaves, and drop it in a glass of clean water. Pothos roots in water faster than almost anything else — new roots usually show up within about ten days. Once those new roots reach about an inch long, that's the cue to move it into soil — moving it too soon is the most common way these cuttings go into shock and stall out. It helps to wash your hands after handling the cuttings, and keep the water glass away from pets and little ones who like to explore with their mouths — if that happens, a quick call to your vet or doctor is the safe move.

Give it about ten days, and you'll see it — a little cluster of fresh white roots reaching down into that glass. That's the sign this one made it.

So that's it: five steps — check, cut, trim, repot, and change the habit.
