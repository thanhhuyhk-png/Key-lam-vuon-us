## TTS SCRIPT

October doesn't close on a date. It closes on your first hard frost.

And here's what trips people up. For the one crop almost everybody puts in this month, you are not supposed to beat the frost. You are supposed to wait for it. One to two weeks after the first killing frost. That's Minnesota Extension. South Dakota State puts a number on it. Soil at fifty degrees, four inches down.

Plant it early and the shoot comes up before winter does. That's the whole reason.

Sixteen things went into the ground this month in your grandmother's garden. One of them has a leaf you should never eat, and I will flag it when we get there. If your ground freezes hard, only seven of them are yours.

The other nine depend on where you live. And you don't need to look up your zone to know which. Just think back to last year. Did your first frost come before November, or after?

Hold that answer. It decides which half of this list you can actually use.

Number one. Garlic.

Here's the thing about garlic. Everybody plants it. Almost nobody waits long enough.

Maryland Extension says mid to late October. Minnesota says one to two weeks after that first killing frost. Both are saying the same thing in different words. Wait until the top of the soil has already given up for the year.

What the clove wants is roots without a shoot. Underground it builds a root system all through November. Above ground it stays quiet. If a green shoot pushes up before the cold sets in, that shoot spends the winter getting burned.

The part most folks get wrong is depth. Point the clove up. The base of the clove sits two to three inches below the surface. Six inches between cloves.

Now the zone line. Hardneck types want a real winter, so they run best in zone seven and colder. Warmer than that and there isn't enough chill to make a proper bulb.

That's it. Garlic goes in the ground and you walk away. Simple as that.

The next one is in the same family, and it will take a colder corner than garlic will.

Number two. Shallots.

Shallots are the quiet one. Oregon State, Clemson, and Maryland Extension all list them as a fall planting.

Here's the trade. A shallot planted now spends the winter doing exactly what garlic does. Rooting, not growing. Come spring it wakes up weeks ahead of anything you could plant fresh. You get a head start you cannot buy.

The tip most people skip is spacing. One shallot doesn't stay one shallot. It divides. Give it room to split into a cluster or you'll dig up a knot of small ones.

The zone line here sits at five. Zone five and warmer, fall planting works. Zone four, Minnesota says wait and plant in spring instead.

That's the last of the ones you plant and forget for a season. This next one you plant and forget for twenty years.

Number three. Rhubarb crowns.

Your grandmother probably called it the pie plant. She was not being cute. Before there was fruit in the spring, there was rhubarb.

And it has been in American gardens a very long time. John Bartram was growing it in Philadelphia in the seventeen thirties. Not seventeen ninety. Seventeen thirties. That plant was in colonial dirt before this country had a name. There's something steadying about that.

Here's why this works. A crown set in October has all winter to settle its roots before it has to grow a single leaf. Vermont Extension and Florida Extension both treat fall as a planting window for the crown.

Now the honest part, and this one is not optional. You eat the stalks. Never the leaves. The leaves hold oxalic acid. Say that out loud once if there are grandchildren in the yard. Or a dog or a cat that chews on things. Go ahead and cut the leaves off at the row, then toss them on the compost pile.

The zone line runs from three to seven. Zone eight and warmer, Florida Extension is blunt about it. It is simply too hot. Rhubarb wants a cold winter the way garlic does.

Now stop for a second, because there is one rule that covers both of the last two.

Nearly every extension service lands on the same point about mulch. Skip the mulch at planting time. Save it for after the ground freezes.

Here's why. Mulch does not warm the soil. It holds whatever temperature is already there. Put three or four inches of leaves down in October and you insulate warm ground. The plant keeps trying to grow. Wait until the ground has frozen, then mulch, and you lock the cold in place. No thawing, no refreezing, no heaving the crown up out of the dirt.

One afternoon in late November does more for these plants than anything you do this month.

Four more in the group of seven. And the next one is the only thing on this whole list that gives you nothing to eat.

Number four. Winter rye.

This is the one people scroll past. It is also the one that pays you.

You are not planting rye for a harvest. You are planting it so the bed is not naked for five months. Bare soil in winter loses itself. Rain moves it, wind takes it, and by spring the bed is tighter and poorer than you left it.

Rye germinates in cold that stops almost everything else. It holds the soil in place and adds organic matter when you turn it under.

Here's the money part, and it is worth being precise. Rye is a grass. It does not make its own nitrogen. Its cousins in the pea and clover family do. North Carolina State and Ohio State measured it. Those legumes fix a hundred thirty to a hundred sixty pounds of nitrogen per acre. At what urea costs right now, that is roughly eighty-five to a hundred dollars an acre. Free. Now that's a deal worth planting.

And here's the thing. There is no money in teaching you to grow your own fertilizer. Nobody is hiding it from you. It just isn't a product, so nobody advertises it. A bag has a barcode. A cover crop doesn't.

That is the one that feeds the dirt. The next one is the first thing on this list you actually get to eat.

Number five. Spinach.

Not any spinach. The crinkled kind.

Wisconsin Horticulture puts it plainly. Savoy types, the deeply crinkled ones, are far more cold hardy than the flat leaf kind. Winter Bloomsdale. Tyee. Bloomsdale Longstanding. Those names matter more than the planting date here.

And spinach has been in this ground a long while. Thomas Jefferson had it in the garden at Monticello in eighteen oh nine, and again in eighteen twelve.

Here's why this works. Spinach does not just tolerate cold. North Carolina State puts it as low as twenty degrees below zero when it has mulch over it. What kills spinach is not winter. It is a wet, warm spell in the middle of winter.

The tip most people miss is sowing thick. Some of it will not make it. Plan for that instead of being disappointed by it.

In colder zones you will want a row cover or a cold frame over the row. Not to keep it alive. To keep it pickable.

That's the toughest leaf on this list. Except for the next one, which is tougher.

Number six. Kale.

Set kale as transplants in October, not as seed. Seed sown now runs out of daylight before it can do anything with it.

Extension work out of California, Utah, Minnesota, and Mississippi State agrees on a range. Kale holds between fifteen and twenty-five degrees. It depends on the variety, and on how old the plant is.

That range matters. An older, well-rooted kale plant takes cold far better than a young one. Which is exactly why you set transplants rather than seed.

Here's the part that surprises folks. Kale gets sweeter after a frost. The plant moves sugars into its leaves as protection. The cold is doing your cooking for you.

Almost anywhere with a real winter, kale will hold. In the coldest zones, give it the same row cover you gave the spinach.

That is the last thing on this list you have to plant. The next one plants itself, and then it walks.

Number seven. Egyptian walking onions.

Virginia Extension says late October into early November. Penn State says simply that fall is best.

Here is what makes this one strange. It does not flower and set seed the way an onion should. It makes a cluster of little bulbs at the top of the stalk. When that cluster gets heavy, the stalk bends over. It plants them a step away from the mother plant.

So the row walks. A step a year, across the bed. Give it a decade and it has strolled clear across the garden, feeding you the whole way.

Green tops in spring. Small bulbs in summer. Something for the pot in January. And you planted it once.

The tip is to leave the bulbils alone once they hit the dirt. The plant knows where it is going.

And that is seven. If your ground freezes hard every winter, those seven are yours. Garlic, shallots, rhubarb, rye, spinach, kale, and the onion that walks.

Now the next three are different. These are not harder. They just need a warmer zone, or a piece of fabric.

Number eight. Softneck garlic.

Same crop, same month, different half of the map.

Softneck is the braiding kind. The kind that hangs in a kitchen. It needs less winter chill than hardneck does, which puts it at its best in zone seven through nine.

Here's why this works. Where the winter is mild, hardneck garlic never gets the cold signal it needs to split into a proper bulb. Softneck does not need that signal. It also stores far longer once cured.

If your winter is mild, this is the garlic your grandmother would have grown. Not because she knew the science. Because the other one did not work.

That one needs warmth. The next one needs warmth and a cover.

Number nine. Overwintering onion sets.

Clemson, Maryland, and New Hampshire Extension all treat fall onion sets as a real option, with one condition.

In zone six b through nine a, you can put sets in bare ground and walk away. New Hampshire Extension is clear that colder than that, you want a low tunnel over them.

The tip is size. Small sets, about the width of a pencil, overwinter better than large ones. Big sets are more likely to bolt in spring, and a bolted onion goes to flower instead of bulb.

The payoff is timing. An onion that overwintered is already growing when the spring-planted ones are still in a bag at the store.

That one goes in the ground. The next one you will be cutting in a few weeks.

Number ten. Mustard greens.

Mississippi State and Maryland Extension both list mustard as a fall green. Fast, sharp, and ready quickly.

And this one carries a lot of history. Mustard greens sit deep in Southern cooking and in African American foodways, going back generations. This is not a novelty green. It is a staple that a lot of families never stopped growing.

Now the honest note, because mustard is the softest thing on this list. Mississippi State puts damage starting around twenty-eight degrees. That is the least cold hardy plant in all sixteen. Treat it as a fall crop, not a winter one.

And here is the rule that governs every leaf we just talked about. North Carolina State research points at a threshold most gardeners have never heard of. Ten hours of daylight.

Below ten hours a day, a plant nearly stops growing. It does not die. It just holds. Whatever size it reached before that line is roughly the size it stays until spring.

So surviving winter and growing through winter are two different things. Your greens need to be seventy-five to a hundred percent grown before daylight drops under ten hours. Plant late and you get a bed of small plants that sit there, perfectly alive, doing nothing until March.

Don't panic if that has already happened to you. It is still a harvest. It is just a spring harvest.

Now here is where this list splits the country in two.

Remember that question from the beginning. Did your first frost come before November, or after?

If it came before, you are done. The ten things we just covered are your October. Everything from here needs a longer, softer fall than you have.

If your first frost comes after November, or barely comes at all, the next six are yours alone. The line is not really north and south. Follow the freeze maps from NOAA and the hardiness map from the Department of Agriculture. The real line sits between two places. The Coastal Plain and the Gulf Coast on one side. The Piedmont and the upper South on the other.

Number eleven. Collards.

Clemson Extension gives collards a long window in the coastal plain of South Carolina. The first of August, all the way to the thirtieth of October.

And no plant on this list has a better record of being handed down. In two thousand eight, a researcher named Mark Farnham went looking. He was working with the Department of Agriculture. He collected eighty-seven different heirloom collard seed lines from farmers across the Carolinas. Most of those farmers were over seventy. Those were not catalog varieties. They were family lines, saved in envelopes and coffee cans, one kitchen at a time.

Collards get better with cold, same as kale. Same reason as kale. The plant sweetens itself as protection.

The tip is to harvest from the bottom up. Take the lower leaves and leave the growing point alone, and one plant feeds you for months instead of once.

That one takes the whole season. The next one is the fastest thing on this entire list.

Number twelve. Radishes.

Clemson gives the coastal plain a radish window from the first of September to the first of November. Right through October.

Here's the thing about radishes. They are the only crop here that will pay you back before Thanksgiving. Most are ready in about a month.

The mistake almost everyone makes is crowding them. A radish needs room to make a radish. Sow them too close and you get a beautiful patch of leaves with nothing underneath.

The other mistake is waiting. A radish left in the ground too long turns woody and hot. So go ahead and pull them young.

That one you sow straight into the ground. The next one you should not.

Number thirteen. Lettuce.

Texas A and M puts lettuce transplants in Region Three around the tenth of October.

Transplants, not seed. Lettuce seed is fussy about warm soil. By mid October in a mild zone, you are asking it to sprout right as conditions turn. A transplant skips that argument entirely.

And lettuce has been here longer than most people think. Jefferson grew a variety at Monticello called Tennis-ball Lettuce. A small, tight butterhead, about the size the name suggests.

The tip is to plant it where it gets afternoon shade if your fall is still running warm. Lettuce turns bitter when it is stressed by heat, not by cold.

That one goes in around the tenth. The next one wants the first, and the two are not interchangeable.

Number fourteen. Cabbage.

Texas A and M puts cabbage transplants in Region Four at the first of October. Nine days earlier than the lettuce, and that gap is not an accident. Cabbage needs longer to make a head than lettuce needs to make a leaf.

Set transplants, same as the lettuce. And give them more room than feels right. A cabbage that has to fight its neighbors makes a small, loose head instead of a tight one.

The zone note here is narrow, and worth saying plainly. This is a Region Four and coastal recommendation. If you are in the upper South, this is a spring crop for you, not a fall one.

That one has a firm date. The next one does not, and that is the honest problem with it.

Number fifteen. Turnips.

Here's where the advice stops agreeing with itself.

Clemson's official South Carolina window closes at the end of September. Texas A and M, in Region Five, keeps turnips going all the way to the middle of December. Georgia Extension gives south Georgia an extra two weeks over the rest of the state.

Those are all correct. They are just describing different pieces of ground.

So the useful answer is not a date. It is a rule. Turnips need roughly two months of decent growing weather to make a root worth pulling. Count backward from the week your ground usually gets cold. If you have two months, plant. If you have six weeks, sow them thick and eat the greens instead. Turnip greens are a crop in their own right.

That one depends on your county. The last one depends on which university you ask.

Number sixteen. English peas.

And I am going to be straight with you about this one, because the sources do not agree.

The LSU AgCenter, down in Louisiana, gives English peas a planting window from the middle of September through January. Clemson, one state over, recommends against a fall planting of English peas in South Carolina at all.

Both of those are real recommendations from real extension services. Neither one is wrong. They are describing different winters, and a pea is fussy enough that a couple hundred miles changes the answer.

So here is what that actually means for you. On the Gulf Coast, plant them. In the middle South, treat it as an experiment, not a plan. Put in a short row, not a long one. And if a neighbor two counties over swears it works, believe them. They may well be right for exactly where they are standing.

That is the whole list. Sixteen things, and only seven of them belong to everybody.

Now, two questions I know are coming.

The first one. What if my zone is too cold for most of this? Then go back to the first seven. Garlic, shallots, rhubarb, rye, spinach, kale, walking onions. That is a real October, and it is a full one.

The second one. What if my first frost already came and went? For garlic, you are likely still fine. The rule is six to eight weeks before the ground actually freezes solid, not before the first light frost. Those are different dates, usually by a month.

So here is what I would ask of you. You don't need all sixteen. Just pick one. One row of garlic. One Saturday afternoon. And next July you pull up something you planted while the leaves were still falling.

I would love it if you told me in the comments what zone you are in. And whether your first frost has hit yet this year. I read them, and it tells me who I am actually talking to.

Somewhere down the line we will get to the root the old growers called sugar root. It came out of the ground in October tasting like an apple and a spoonful of honey. It sat in American seed catalogs until about nineteen oh two. Then it quietly disappeared. That one deserves its own video.

Until then, see if you can find the coldest corner of your yard. The one that stays in shadow, where the frost sits longest in the morning.

Half this list has been waiting for a corner exactly like that.

Happy gardening.
