## Cau hoi 1: Days-to-maturity chinh xac cua 8 cay trong thang 7

**FINDINGS:**

### Radish
- Radishes mature **21 to 30 days** from seeding, depending on soil and air temperatures. (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/carrot-beet-radish-parsnip/
- *Note:* Outline figure of "25 days" falls within this range. Use **21–30 days** for accuracy; 25 days is a reasonable mid-range shorthand.

### Leaf Lettuce
- "Leaf lettuce should be ready to harvest about **75 days** after planting." However, it "can be used as soon as plants are **5 to 6 inches tall**." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/lettuce/
- OSU Extension reports outer leaves can be harvested at **45 to 55 days** for cut-and-come-again style. (OSU Extension, n.d., via search result from extension.oregonstate.edu)
- *Verdict for video:* Use **45–55 days** (baby leaf / cut-and-come-again harvest) as the operative figure, not 75 days (full head). Clarify this distinction in script — "you can start picking leaves at 45 days, not waiting for the whole plant."
- Clemson also notes lettuce "withstands light frosts."

### Bush/Snap Beans
- "Generally, snap beans will be ready to harvest **55 days** after planting, depending on the variety selected." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/bush-pole-type-snap-beans/
- UGA Extension: bush snap beans mature in **48 to 60 days** depending on variety. (UGA Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.uga.edu/publications/detail.html?number=C1006
- OSU Extension: "some bush snap beans mature in **45 days**." (OSU Extension, n.d.)
- *Verdict:* Use **50–60 days** (conservative mid-range, consistent with Clemson 55d + UGA 48–60d). Outline figure "45–50 days" is slightly fast; revise to **48–60 days** for accuracy. **Soil temp requirement: minimum 60°F** (Clemson HGIC).

### Spinach
- "Spinach should be ready to harvest in about **37 to 45 days** after planting." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/spinach/
- OSU Extension: **40 to 50 days** to maturity. (OSU Extension, via search, extension.oregonstate.edu)
- PSU Extension: plant "**six to eight weeks** before the average first frost date in the fall." (PSU Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.psu.edu/growing-spinach-a-cool-season-vegetable
- *Verdict:* Outline figure "~40 days" is accurate. Use **37–45 days**. Spinach can tolerate temperatures as low as **20°F** without injury (Clemson). Seeds germinate best at **45–68°F** soil (PSU Extension).

### Beets
- Clemson HGIC: "about **50 to 70 days** after planting" before harvest readiness. (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/carrot-beet-radish-parsnip/
- OSU Extension: "germination in 10 to 15 days and harvest within **50 to 60 days**." (OSU Extension, n.d., search result from extension.oregonstate.edu/imported-publication/beets)
- *Verdict:* Outline figure "50–60 days" is accurate and supported. Use **50–60 days** (OSU) / **50–70 days** (Clemson). Nebraska Extension (Lancaster) also lists beets as "semi-hardy" — tolerating **light frost (30–32°F)**. — https://lancaster.unl.edu/planting-fall-and-winter-produce/

### Kale and Collards
- **Kale:** "50 to 70 days" after sowing; the 'Darkibor' cultivar specifically "matures around 75 days." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/arugula-kale-mesclun-mustard-and-swiss-chard/
- **Collards:** "ready for harvest **60 to 80 days** after direct seeding, depending on the cultivar." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/collards/
- *Verdict:* Outline figure "50–60 days" is slightly fast for the upper end of both crops. More accurate range: kale **50–70 days**, collards **60–80 days**. For video shorthand, **60–75 days** is defensible if noting "harvest young leaves earlier."
- Collards: "mature plants will withstand frosts and light to medium freezes." Kale: "colder temperatures sweeten the leaves." (Clemson HGIC)

### Summer Squash / Zucchini
- Clemson HGIC: "Summer squash can be harvested about **55 days** after planting." **Soil temp: must be 60°F** at 4-inch depth. Fall planting Piedmont: July 15–Aug 15. (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/summer-squash/
- UGA Extension: "will begin producing in **45 to 55 days** from seeding." (UGA Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.uga.edu/publications/detail.html?number=C993
- OSU Extension: "Start harvesting at **45 days**." (OSU, n.d.)
- *Verdict:* Outline figure "50–60 days" is accurate. Use **50–60 days** (harvesting begins ~45–55 days; peak production ~55–60 days).

### Bush Cucumbers
- Clemson HGIC: "Depending on the cultivar, cucumbers should be ready for harvest in about **50 to 70 days**." Seeds do not germinate below **50°F**; wait for soil to reach **70°F**. (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/cucumber/
- OSU Extension: early-maturing varieties **50 days**; medium **60 days**; late **70 days** at high temperatures (75–85°F). Varieties with 55–65 days from transplant recommended. (OSU Extension, n.d., via search)
- *Verdict:* Outline figure "50–55 days" refers to early/bush types — accurate for those varieties. For script specificity, use "**50–60 days** for bush varieties" and note soil must be warm (60–70°F) for germination.

## Cau hoi 2: "Fall factor" / buffer cong them khi trong cuoi vu

**FINDINGS:**

- **The fall factor is 14 days (two weeks).** The reasoning: "plant growth slows down as temperature cools (respiration is temperature dependent so plant processes slow down as temperatures drop)." (Garden Professors / John Porter, former UNL Extension educator, n.d.) — https://gardenprofessors.com/counting-the-days-to-maturity-calculating-planting-dates-for-fall-vegetables/
  - *Note: Garden Professors is not a .edu/.gov source, but the author is a former UNL Extension educator with extension credentials. Tier 3 source.*

- **Nebraska Extension (Lancaster County) confirms the same 10–14 day buffer:** "Add in another **10-14 days** to account for this 'fall factor'" to compensate for slowed growth as temperatures cool and days shorten in autumn. (Nebraska Extension / Lancaster County, n.d.) — https://lancaster.unl.edu/planting-fall-and-winter-produce/
  - *This is a Tier 1 source (.edu extension). Confirmed.*

- **The mechanism:** Days shorten + temperatures drop in late summer/fall → plant respiration and photosynthesis slow → crops take longer to mature than the seed packet DTM implies.

- **Full countback formula (Nebraska Extension):** Days-to-harvest (from seed packet) + harvest period buffer + **10–14 days fall factor** = total days to count backward from first frost date. That date is your last safe planting date.

- **Example from Garden Professors:** Blue Lake beans (55–60d DTM) + 14d harvest period + 14d fall factor = 83 days. Count back 83 days from first frost = last planting date.

- **PSU Extension confirms the mechanism concept:** DTM on seed packets "begins on the date the plant is transplanted outdoors, not the date it is seeded indoors." For direct-seeded crops, DTM is from sowing. (PSU Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.psu.edu/season-extenders-and-growing-fall-vegetables

- **Simple on-screen formula for video:**
  ```
  Last planting date = First frost date − DTM − 14 days (fall factor)
  ```

## Cau hoi 3: Frost tolerance va co che kale/spinach ngot hon sau frost

**FINDINGS:**

### Frost Tolerance Categories (verified)

- **Semi-hardy — tolerates light frost (30–32°F):** beets, Chinese cabbage, collards, green onions, mustard greens, potatoes, radishes, spinach, Swiss chard, and lettuce varieties. (Nebraska Extension / Lancaster County, n.d.) — https://lancaster.unl.edu/planting-fall-and-winter-produce/

- **Hardy — tolerates several frosts, killed near 20°F:** broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, carrots, cauliflower, **kale**, kohlrabi, rutabagas, turnips. (Nebraska Extension / Lancaster County, n.d.) — https://lancaster.unl.edu/planting-fall-and-winter-produce/

- Illinois Extension confirms: Cole crops (kale, broccoli, etc.) "tolerate below-freezing temperatures (mid-20s)." Root vegetables — carrots, radishes, turnips, beets — "grow sweeter with a light frost." (Illinois Extension, 2023) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/flowers-fruits-and-frass/2023-08-25-fill-fall-vegetable-garden-variety-cold-hardy-choices-may

- Spinach: "It can survive temperatures of **20°F** without injury." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/spinach/

- Kale: PSU Extension states kale and Brussels sprouts "even benefit from a light frost, as it **converts the starches into sugar and improves the taste**." (PSU Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.psu.edu/cool-season-vs-warm-season-vegetables

### Starch-to-Sugar Mechanism [YMYL-adjacent — mechanism claim, not health claim]

- **PSU Extension (Tier 1):** "winter-hardy vegetables, such as kale or Brussels sprouts, even benefit from a light frost, as it **converts the starches into sugar and improves the taste**." (PSU Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.psu.edu/cool-season-vs-warm-season-vegetables

- **Clemson HGIC (Tier 1):** For kale: "colder temperatures sweeten the leaves." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/arugula-kale-mesclun-mustard-and-swiss-chard/

- **University of Wisconsin Extension blog** (available via search, page now redirects) cited: "Frost-kissed kale will taste sweeter because of it" — brassicas get sweeter after frost. (UW-Extension Rock County, 2014) — *URL confirmed via search but page now redirects to extension.wisc.edu; cite cautiously.*

- **Mechanism summary (synthesis from PSU + Clemson):** When temperatures drop below ~32°F, cold-hardy plants convert stored starches to free sugars (sucrose, fructose). This serves as a cryoprotectant — lowering the freezing point of cellular fluid. Side effect: leaves taste noticeably sweeter. Kale and collards also contain bitter glucosinolates; cold exposure may reduce bitterness while the sugar content rises.

- **Note for video script:** The two Tier 1 sources (PSU, Clemson) confirm starch→sugar conversion in kale/collards. For spinach, Clemson confirms frost survival to 20°F but does not explicitly confirm sweetening mechanism. The sweetening claim is strongest for kale/collards/brassicas; for spinach, stick to "cold-tolerant" framing.

## Cau hoi 4: Trong summer squash thang 7 co NEO duoc squash vine borer khong?

**FINDINGS:**

- **Illinois Extension (Tier 1) — key quote:** "Altering your planting date or planting a second crop in **early July** to avoid peak egg-laying can help reduce the likelihood that plants will be attacked." Adults emerge "**mid-June to early July**." (Illinois Extension, 2022) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2022-07-15-managing-squash-vine-borer-garden

- **Ohio State Ohioline (Tier 1):** "Adults emerge **early to mid-summer (typically in June)**" after accumulating 1,000 degree-days at a base of 50°F. "Squash vine borer completes **one generation per year** in Ohio." (OSU Ohioline, n.d.) — https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/ent-0106

- **UMN Extension (Tier 1) — confirmed via search:** Adults are active from **late June/early July to early-to-mid August**. Larvae tunnel through plant stems. Rotate crops because larvae overwinter in soil. A second planting of summer squash in early July will "mature after adult borers have finished laying eggs and will not suffer any damage." (UMN Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/squash-vine-borers

- **Illinois Extension — generations:** "In **southern Illinois**, there can be **two generations**." One generation typical in northern states. (Illinois Extension, 2022) — https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2022-07-15-managing-squash-vine-borer-garden

- **Mechanism summary:** SVB adults (clearwing moths) emerge from overwintering cocoons in the soil in mid-June to early July. Females lay eggs at the base of squash stems. Eggs hatch in ~1 week; larvae bore into stems and feed for 4–6 weeks. By planting summer squash in **early July**, the seedlings emerge after peak adult flight has ended — adults have finished laying eggs, so newly planted crops escape infestation for that season.

- **Caveat for video:** In southern states (zones 7–9), SVB may have two generations — a July planting may not fully escape the second generation. The "avoid SVB" benefit is most reliable in **northern US** (zones 4–6). Add a brief note: "in hotter southern states, check with your local extension."

- **Soil temp for July squash planting:** Minimum 60°F at 4-inch depth required for germination (Clemson HGIC) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/summer-squash/ . In July in most of the US, soil is well above this threshold.

## Cau hoi 5: ANTI-LIST — Cay KHONG nen trong thang 7 vi qua dai ngay

**FINDINGS:**

### Tomato (from seed)
- OSU Extension: from transplant, DTM ranges **50 days (early cherry) to 85 days (full-season)** for most cultivars. **"Direct-seeded plantings take about 25 days longer to mature."** Therefore from seed: ~75–110 days minimum; typically **85–100+ days** for standard varieties from seed. (OSU Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/ec-1333-grow-your-own-tomatoes-tomatillos
- *Note: in cold climates, "add a minimum of 10 days to 2 weeks" beyond listed DTM* (OSU Extension, via search).
- **For video:** A standard mid-season tomato seeded in July needs ~100 days from seed minimum. If first frost is Oct 1, that's only 92 days. Does not compute. **Confirmed: NOT worth starting from seed in July.**

### Pepper (from seed)
- Clemson HGIC: **"When starting from seed, expect 100 to 120 days to maturity."** From transplant: 70–85 days. (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/pepper/
- **For video:** 100–120 days from seed planted in July = harvest October–November at earliest. Past frost for most zones. **Confirmed: NOT worth starting from seed in July.**

### Watermelon
- Clemson HGIC: **"between 85 to 90 days from transplant"** to first ripe fruit. From seed: add ~2–3 weeks germination + establishment = effectively **100+ days from direct seed**. "Fall planting not recommended." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/watermelons/
- **For video:** Even from transplant, 85–90 days from July 1 = harvest ~Sept 25–Oct 1. Too risky with fall factor. **Confirmed: NOT worth it in July.**

### Cantaloupe / Melon (general)
- OSU Extension: melons require **75–100 days** from sowing to harvest. UMN Extension: "only varieties with short growth cycles of **less than 90 days** to maturity can produce a ripe fruit in the north." (UMN Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.umn.edu/fruit/growing-melons-home-garden
- **For video:** 75–100 days from July planting = harvest Sept–Oct. With fall factor, marginal to impossible. Short-season varieties (65–75d) are a borderline case — not confirmed as safe to include.

### Winter Squash (including butternut, acorn, etc.)
- UGA Extension: "**80 to 120 days** to mature" for vining winter squash types. (UGA Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.uga.edu/publications/detail.html?number=C993
- Clemson HGIC: pumpkin maturity "**70 to 120 days**"; 'Big Max' = 120d, 'Big Moon' = 115d. Most varieties should be "planted by early July to ripen in time for Halloween." (Clemson HGIC, n.d.) — https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/pumpkins-winter-squash/
- *Clemson note: "most varieties of pumpkins should be planted by early July" — this means July 1 is already the LAST chance for pumpkins, not a safe window.*
- **For video:** 80–120 days from July = harvest late Sept to November at earliest. Combined with fall factor, risky for most zones. **Confirmed: NOT recommended in July.**

### Brussels Sprouts
- UGA Extension: "Most Brussels sprouts do not produce sprouts until **90 to 100 days**." Varieties like Jade Cross E, Long Island Improved, Prince Marvel, Valiant: **90 day** maturity. (UGA Extension, n.d.) — https://extension.uga.edu/publications/detail.html?number=C1069
- OSU Extension: **90 to 95 days** to maturity. (OSU Extension, n.d.)
- Clemson HGIC: may take **120 days** (~17 weeks) to reach maturity. (Clemson HGIC, n.d.)
- **For video:** 90–120 days from July 1 = harvest late Sept to November. With fall factor, most US zones can't support a July seeding of Brussels sprouts. **Confirmed: NOT worth planting from seed in July.**

## YMYL Flags

- **Spinach frost survival to 20°F** — claim is horticultural, not a health claim. No YMYL issue.
- **Kale/collards starch-to-sugar on frost** — mechanism claim (food science), not health advice. No YMYL issue. However, do NOT extend this to imply pesticide safety, spray-after-frost claims, or consumption of frost-damaged produce without wash. Flag if script adds consumption claims.
- **Squash vine borer "early July planting avoids damage"** — this is regionally variable. Script must include caveat: "in southern states, check with your local extension — a second SVB generation may still be active." Do NOT present as universal guarantee.
- **"Frost-tolerant" vegetables surviving 28–32°F** — the Nebraska Extension categorization (semi-hardy vs hardy) is verified. Do NOT imply these crops are completely frost-proof; correct framing is "light frost-tolerant" and "may survive brief hard freezes with protection."
