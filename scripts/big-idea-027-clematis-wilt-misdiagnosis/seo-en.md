**Clematis Wilt Diagnosis: The Cause You're Probably Missing**

Your clematis collapses overnight—leaves black, stem hanging limp. But the Royal Horticultural Society says most gardeners are wrong: many of those collapses aren't true wilt at all. Learn the 3 causes that look identical, and which one actually matters.

**In this video, you'll learn:**
- How to tell if it's a broken stem, pest damage, or true fungal wilt (with a 10-second stem test)
- Why July is peak time for clematis wilt—and how to spot it early
- The one simple planting step that protects against all three problems
- When to cut back hard vs. when to leave it alone

There are three causes of clematis collapse that look nearly identical. First: a broken stem. Clematis canes are hollow and brittle—wind, rough handling, or a curious dog can snap them clean through. The test is simple: split the stem open. Green inside means physical damage, not fungal wilt. Just prune back to healthy tissue. Second: pests. Slugs and snails leave ragged holes and slimy trails; spider mites create fine webbing and speckled leaves. Both are slower damage than true wilt's dramatic blackening. Third: true fungal wilt (Calophoma clematidina). The fungus enters through wounds near the soil line and blocks water flow, turning the inside of the stem black—that's your diagnostic sign.

The encouraging part: wilt doesn't kill the roots. Clemson Cooperative Extension, Penn State Extension, and the University of Illinois all agree—established clematis usually send up fresh shoots from the base the following year. And one simple step at planting time—burying at least two leaf nodes below the soil—hedges against all three problems before they ever start.

Don't dig up your plant. Split open a stem, check the color inside, and wait. You'll be the gardener with the thriving vine while neighbors are replanting.
