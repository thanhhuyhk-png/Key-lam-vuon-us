## TTS SCRIPT

Here's the thing. If every plant you touch ends up brown and crispy, it might not be you. It might be the soil.

Take a look at this. This is a pothos. No pot. No dirt. Just a jar of water on a windowsill. And it will grow like this for years.

Today I'll walk you through seven houseplants that grow in plain water, with no soil at all — some of the hardest plants in the world to kill. And number five? That's the one you're probably sure hates you. Hang on, because we're going to get there.

Soil is where most beginners go wrong — too much water, too little, the wrong dirt, little bugs riding in on the bag. With plain water, all of that just disappears. You can see the roots, you can see when the water's getting low. And that very first plant roots in about a week — I'll show you the one bump on the stem that makes or breaks it.

Here's our lineup, so you know what's coming: the pothos, lucky bamboo, heartleaf philodendron, spider plant, coleus, wandering jew, and a peace lily that just might flower on you. All you need is a clear glass jar, a sunny window, and about ten minutes. No bending, no heavy bags of soil. Let's start with the easiest one of all.

Number one is the pothos, the money plant. If you've never once kept a plant alive, start right here. A pothos cutting grows roots in a glass of water in about a week, and it doesn't just survive in water — it thrives. One gardener kept a single pothos in a jar of water for over three years, stretching more than five feet long the whole time.

Here's the trick most folks miss. See those little bumps along the stem? Those are nodes, and they're the part that needs to sit under the water — that's where the roots come out. Leaves up above, bumps down below. That's the whole secret. Every two to three weeks, dump the old water out and pour fresh water in — don't just top it off — and once a month, add a couple drops of liquid plant food. Do that, and a pothos will outlive most everything else in your house.

Number two is lucky bamboo — the straight green stalks, sometimes curled into a spiral, sitting in a dish of pebbles. Arkansas and Clemson extension say it'll happily grow in a vase for a year or more. Keep an inch or two of water over the roots, toss a few clean pebbles in to hold it upright, change the water once a week, and feed it a little diluted plant food every couple of months. One heads up: lucky bamboo is sensitive to the fluoride in most tap water, and it can scorch the leaf tips brown. There's a dead simple fix for that, and it works for every plant on this list — I'll give it to you in a few minutes.

Number three is the heartleaf philodendron, sweet heart-shaped leaves on a trailing vine — a true old-fashioned windowsill plant, the kind somebody's grandmother kept in a green glass bottle for thirty years. It roots in water about as easily as the pothos — snip a piece right below a node, drop it in a jar with bright, indirect light, and in a week or two you'll see white roots reaching down. A philodendron that started in water is happy to keep coasting there for a good long while. If it ever looks tired and pale, that's just its way of asking for a pot of soil.

Number four is the spider plant, and this one's a little different. A grown spider plant sends out arching stems with tiny "spiderettes" dangling off the ends. Snip one off, stand it in a glass of water, and in about a week it grows its own roots. Here's the honest part: the water is how you start a spider plant, not where it lives forever. After a month or so, once the roots are a couple inches long, it really wants a pot of soil for the long run — think of the jar as the nursery, not the forever home. But here's the best reason for this one: if you've got a cat or dog, the spider plant is right there on the ASPCA's safe list. It won't hurt them one bit — and that's going to matter a lot for the next few plants.

Number five. This is the one you're absolutely sure hates you. Coleus — those bright, painted leaves in purple and lime green and deep red. So many folks have told me the same story: "Coleus hates me. Within two days it starts to die." Here's the thing: it is not you. Coleus is a drama queen in soil — it wilts the second it gets thirsty. But in water, it's a completely different plant. Snip a healthy stem, pull off the bottom leaves, stand it up in water, and it roots fast, growing happily in that glass for months. Change the water every four to six days, give it good bright light so the colors don't fade, and after a few months it'll do its best moved into a pot. Turns out the plant never hated you. The dirt did.

Number six is the wandering jew, properly called tradescantia — shimmery purple and silver leaves. If there's a plant that roots easier than this one, it'd be news to me. Snip a piece a few inches long, pull off the lower leaves, drop it in water, and you'll see roots in days. This is the definition of a passalong plant — the kind you snip and hand to a neighbor over the back fence.

Number seven, the showstopper — the peace lily, with deep glossy leaves and, every so often, one elegant white flower. I want to be straight about the science here. A peace lily will grow in water, roots down in a vase, but it's a touch fussier than the others — to really live in water long-term, it wants a proper setup, not just a glass you forgot about on the counter. That famous white flower can absolutely surprise you, but it needs bright, indirect light and a little plant food once a month to pull it off. Without those, you'll still get gorgeous deep green leaves — just don't feel let down if the flower takes its time. Keep the water level down at the roots, not up on the leaves.

Now, please really hear me on this one. Six of these seven plants are on the ASPCA's toxic list — the pothos, the peace lily, the philodendron, the lucky bamboo, the coleus, and the wandering jew. They won't usually do serious harm, but a curious pet that chews a leaf can end up with a sore mouth and an upset stomach. So keep these six up high, where pets can't reach — or if your cat treats every leaf like a salad bar, just grow the spider plant. That's the one on the safe list.

And here's the fix I promised. Two simple rules for the water itself. Rule one: change the water out completely every week or two — don't just top it off. Old water runs out of oxygen, and that's when roots turn slimy and start to rot. Rule two: most tap water has chlorine and fluoride in it, and on sensitive plants like the spider plant and lucky bamboo, that shows up as brown, burnt leaf tips that never heal back. The fix is simple — fill your jar and let it sit out overnight before you use it, so the chlorine floats off, or use filtered water. That one habit saves more of these plants than anything else you'll do.

So there you have it — seven plants, no soil, and almost no way to mess it up. If you're starting with just one, make it the pothos: a clean jar, water that's sat out overnight, a cutting with the nodes under water, set on a bright sill. Ten minutes, no bending, no dirt under your nails.

And here's what I love most about these — they get passed down. A cutting in a jam jar was how whole families shared plants, going back to the Great Depression. Start one this week, and by fall you'll have a piece to hand a grandkid for their first apartment.

So go easy on yourself — those dead plants were never your fault. And if your back or knees have been giving you grief in the garden, stick around — next up is a whole list of plants you can grow sitting right down, no bending and no kneeling at all.

Happy gardening.
